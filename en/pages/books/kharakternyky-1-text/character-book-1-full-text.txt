“Weapons won’t be necessary here,” he tossed over his shoulder. “My wife has just made pancakes.”

GRANDFATHER MYKOLA — BACKGROUND

The village stood among forests and fields, which at dawn disappeared into the mist. The smell of fresh earth and charred wood hung in the air. Several houses stood on a rise, surrounded by a tall wooden fence, two metres high. Here and there, small openings had been made so defenders could shoot arrows at potential attackers. The massive gates could be shut as soon as the villagers sensed danger.

The year 1576 in Podillia had been hot, and the harvest was poorer than the year before. Many people from neighbouring villages had stayed away from the fairs because they lacked grain. Not the people of this village, though. They were used to working hard and earning a decent profit. Not every village could boast such prosperity. Many of the men were away that day, driving ox-carts loaded with provisions to the nearest town, where they could sell their goods at a good price and buy what they needed: horse tack, tools, salt, and various little things for the girls.

Mykolka, a lively boy with a slightly upturned nose and a long neck, was once again racing along a narrow field path towards the forest. He was a restless child, and every day one of the adults would chase him with a switch. Laughing, he would run away and hide among the trees. It had become an ordinary sight in the village where he had been born and raised.

Looking back, Mykolka made sure nobody was chasing him, then scrambled up a tree in an instant. No one would see him here, and he would have a good view of the surroundings, so nobody could sneak up on him with a switch. The tree was tall and sturdy, and its branches made a comfortable, safe perch. He could wait until evening and then return home in peace. Nobody would even remember what he had done.

The boy settled onto the thickest branch and began to daydream. Suddenly he jolted, slid down, and raced headlong towards the village, shouting at the top of his lungs:

“Tatars! Tatars!”

Horsemen were riding out of the forest, somewhere near the horizon. They were galloping towards the village, raising clouds of dust. Mykolka’s warning did not go unheard. The villagers immediately began preparing to defend their homes. The men hurried to arm themselves and take positions behind the wooden palisade. The heavy gates slammed shut, cutting the village off from the approaching enemy, while the women rushed with their children into cellars. Such raids were not unusual; they were almost commonplace. These lands lived by their own rules: sometimes the Tatars came to plunder Ukrainian villages, and sometimes the Cossacks set out on retaliatory campaigns against them. It had become part of a cruel order of things: when the enemy came, you had to stand and fight, or they would grow bolder and return without fear.

But this time the raiding party was far too large. The number of horsemen seemed to grow by the minute. The villagers exchanged glances, tightened their grips on their weapons, and prepared either to fight back or to die. Those who surrendered faced slavery. The people here loved their freedom dearly and were ready to defend it to the death.

Mykolka was an orphan. His mother had died giving birth to him, and his father had perished in one of the raids against the Tatars when the boy was five. He barely remembered his parents, and now there was no one to look after his safety. He decided to help in whatever way he could: bringing arrows to the archers, carrying water, and hauling stones to strengthen the defences.

The battle was brutal. This attack was different from the previous ones. The Tatars charged at everything alive like madmen, showing mercy to no one. After two hours, they broke through the defences, stormed into the village, began setting houses on fire, killing the elderly, and tying up the young to drive them away into captivity.

That morning, Mykolka had been living the ordinary life of a mischievous village boy. Now he stood in the middle of a dying village, stunned, breathing in the smoke that was consuming his past. He watched the invaders plunder a community that had been prosperous only that morning. Tears welled in his eyes: tears of helplessness, despair, and grief for his murdered and tortured neighbours.

He screamed. Into the void. Loudly, desperately.

The void answered.

They tied him up and threw him onto a cart among sacks that had once held the community’s treasures. He could see nothing after that, but they were going somewhere. The Tatars argued in their language. Then they shouted and hurried their horses onwards.

Mykolka heard a spirited whoop, and another battle began. He understood from the clash of sabres and the cries of the wounded and dying around him. When it was over, they pulled him off the cart and brought him before the Cossack ataman. The man looked at him carefully and asked:

“Was it you who called us, boy?”

And Mykolka understood who had answered him from the void. It had been the ataman’s voice.

They took Mykolka into their brotherhood, taught him everything, and turned him into a true fighter. He became skilled with two sabres, fighting both on horseback and on foot. But most importantly, they taught him sorcery, for this was no ordinary band. They were the legendary Kharakternyks. As it turned out, in his moment of despair he had called them to his aid. That was how he had been initiated and learned everything he knew, including how to travel between Plans.

Those were cruel years. The Tatars burned many villages and towns, killed the elderly, and took young people and children captive, selling them at markets in the Crimean Khanate. This went on for half a century, perhaps longer, until the Cossack state—the Zaporozhian Host—was established in these lands under the leadership of Khmel’s father, as everyone called him behind his back: Bohdan Khmelnytsky.

Mykolka, by then known as Cossack Mykola, took part in the creation of the state alongside his brothers-in-arms. But it did not last long. Mykola lived to see Austrian and Polish rule in these lands, followed by the long Muscovite occupation.

The community of Kharakternyks to which Cossack Mykola belonged changed over time. There were not many of them in Ukrainian lands, so they could not alter the historical course of their people. Besides, the community was not limited to a single nation. It maintained ties with other communities whose members could also travel between worlds and were skilled in warfare and magic. Their names differed, but their essence was the same.

The Germans had the “Schwarzgards”; the Scandinavians, the “Vandal Wargs”; and the Celts, the “Sidhe Folk”. Even the distant Japanese had their own community, known as the “Shinobi of the Worlds”. Shamans, witches, healers, velds, volkhvs, druids, vidars, sharlakhs.

For a while, Mykola collected these names and wrote them down. Eventually, however, the notes were lost somewhere, and the different names for Kharakternyks remained only in the Cossack’s memory.

As time passed, fewer and fewer new Kharakternyks—whatever they were called—appeared in the world. The old ones died or set off on journeys to other worlds and never returned. Their community grew smaller and smaller. By the beginning of the twentieth century, new initiations had ceased altogether.

Mykola himself lived through both World Wars. He helped create the Ukrainian People’s Republic, but did not live to see its victory. Later he joined the Ukrainian Insurgent Army, fighting both the Old Union and the Reich. He fought as a partisan in the forests, organised hideouts, and transported people and weapons, until the last flame of resistance went out in 1956.

Eventually, no longer Mykolka or Cossack Mykola, but Grandfather Mykola, he left his troubled past behind and began living quietly, watching the changing world. Cities grew, technology advanced, and he watched people forget the old wars.

He firmly identified with his people, although many other Kharakternyks did not concern themselves with such ties. So when a new Ukrainian state rose from the ashes at the end of the twentieth century, he was happy, even though he no longer took an active part in its creation.

He had also become the only Kharakternyk left in the entire world. This became a heavy burden for Grandfather Mykola. It changed the way he saw the world and made him avoid unnecessary heroic deeds. He was quietly fading away.

At the turn of the millennium, Mykola met a woman with the gift of prophecy in a church. She foretold the destruction of the planet, but her prophecies often changed, and he was the only one who believed her. Grandfather Mykola had understood the pattern: the world was constantly changing, and prophecies merely reflected those changes.

They married, and he revealed his secret to her. From then on, they lived together, protecting each other’s secrets and waiting for their time. Perhaps that time had come now.

Three days earlier, she had said that a great nuclear war would soon come and destroy humanity. That same day, Mykola heard the cry of a new Kharakternyk—Dmytro—in his mind. New Kharakternyks did not appear without reason. They appeared only when their time had come.

For three days, Grandfather Mykola searched for the new Kharakternyk and could not find him. He understood that the young man had most likely jumped to another Plan. So yesterday the old Kharakternyk had finally taken a desperate step: he used his own life force to send a message. It had greatly shortened his life—from many years to perhaps six months—but guaranteed that Dmytro would find his way back to his home world.

And today, when he sensed that the message had reached its recipient, his wife told him that the future had changed once again. Nuclear war remained inevitable, but there were now possible outcomes—both good and bad. The future had once again been swallowed by the mist.

DMYTRO — AT HOME

“You can call me Grandfather Mykola. And I’ll say it again: you’re quite safe here. Even the Shaheds don’t reach this place.”

With that, the old man gestured for everyone to come inside.

Nairin was probably the only person present, apart from Dmytro himself, who recognised Grandfather Mykola from the dreams Dmytro had shared with him. So Nairin was the first to put away his weapon and follow the old man and Dmytro inside. The others put their weapons away as well.

The house was spacious and bright. Its minimalist renovation sharply contrasted with the age of its owner. You might have expected such an old man to live in a creaking house full of antiques, but that was not the case. The walls were painted white with a barely perceptible blue tint—clean, fresh, and spotless. The first room was a living room. A large, comfortable table stood in the middle, and five of the visitors gathered around it. Wheeled chairs completed the modern look, while large plastic-framed windows let in enough light that there was no need to switch on the round ceiling lamp, which seemed to lie almost flat against the matte ceiling.

A kind-looking elderly woman came out of the adjoining room. She was carrying a large plate of pancakes and little cups of raspberry jam on a plastic tray. Grandfather Mykola was brewing herbal tea, its aroma spreading through the house and captivating the guests. At last everything was ready. They sat down at the table and began to talk.

“First of all, welcome back, Dmytro. You are the first Kharakternyk to be initiated in the last three hundred years. I’m glad it happened—it means our world still has hope,” the old man began. “And who are your friends?”

The guards and Leila introduced themselves one by one. The old man welcomed each of them, finding a few warm words for everyone. At last it was Nairin’s turn. He looked at the old man and greeted him telepathically. Grandfather Mykola’s eyebrows shot up in surprise. His astonishment was genuine, and it quickly gave way to curiosity.

“Well, I’ll be! So you’re not just visitors—you’re travellers too. Quite a group! Dmytro, you really are a find for our world. Tell me about yourselves.”

Nairin opened his thoughts to the old man. Just as Dmytro had recently experienced, Mykola began receiving information in one enormous stream. The guards had long since realised that Kharakternyks communicated telepathically, so they calmly enjoyed their tea and pancakes. Leila was a little nervous, but kept herself under control. It was clear that it was not easy for her: questions seemed to be straining at the leash like wild horses.

When Grandfather Mykola had received everything Nairin wanted to share, he began speaking aloud, out of respect for those who could access the thought-link through their bracelets. His account was lively and engaging. He briefly recounted his own story without burdening the guests with unnecessary historical detail, focusing instead on the life of Kharakternyks in this Plan. Everyone listened intently. Even Leila remained silent, attentive and focused.

When the old man finished, a pause followed. Nairin broke it with a direct and painful question:

“So your world, like ours, is doomed. And at that very moment a new Kharakternyk—Dmytro—was initiated. What’s more, immediately after his initiation he ended up in our Plan, even though it is very far from yours and the probability of such a journey is almost zero. Have I understood correctly?”

Grandfather Mykola did not answer at once. He looked at Dmytro for a long time, his gaze heavy and searching, and only then nodded.

“We must help each other. Our world and yours,” he said to Nairin, although he was looking straight into Dmytro’s eyes. “I don’t know exactly how. I can only guess, and even those guesses are based on my wife’s prophecies, which change with events. But I can offer you a little trip around our Plan. How about a visit to the capital and a cup of coffee? I know some good places.”

The old man stood up, and everyone else followed. Since their journey to a new Plan had gone well, the suggestion suited them all. Leila was especially pleased; she had barely been able to hold back a flood of questions.

“You’re dressed too lightly,” the old man remarked, opening a wardrobe. It contained a wide variety of clothes, and, to the guests’ surprise, he found warm things for everyone.

At last they all went outside, except for the grandmother, who stayed home to take care of a few things. Grandfather Mykola explained that she did not like large groups, although she was happy to travel with one or two guests.

“Dmytro, open a portal to the capital,” the old man suggested.

“Grandfather, I thought it was impossible to open a portal within a single Plan,” Dmytro replied in surprise. He had been clearly told that portals could be opened only between Plans, not within one.

“Try it the way you opened the portal to me. Don’t argue,” the old man said calmly.

Dmytro shrugged and tried. Nothing happened until Grandfather Mykola gave him the name of a landmark: the lower grounds of the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra.

The monastery immediately appeared in his mind, and the portal opened.

There were no flashes of light or sounds. Just a transparent cut in reality through which a magnificent building could be seen. Dmytro himself could hardly believe what he had done. Nairin’s surprised exclamation rang out beside him—he too had been sceptical about the idea.

Without wasting time, the whole group stepped onto the stone paths of the Lavra’s Lower Grounds.

The air was cool and slightly damp, filled with the smell of old stone, incense, and wet earth. Whitewashed buildings with dark roofs lined the narrow, winding paths. Low metal lanterns, neat benches, slopes leading down towards the Dnipro beyond the walls, and the distant ringing of bells—all of it created a feeling of peace and antiquity.

They headed towards a small, cosy coffee shop. Heavy grey clouds covered the sky, and the light had taken on a muted autumn quality.

Inside, it was warm and bright. Wooden tables, soft chairs, photographs of the Lavra on the walls, and clay pots of rosemary, mint, and thyme on the windowsills. The air was filled with the aroma of freshly roasted coffee, with notes of chocolate and caramel.

The barista was a stocky, dark-haired man with Caucasian features. He worked with confidence and precision. His movements belonged to someone who had spent years studying coffee.

The conversation at the table was calm. When the barista brought coffee and pastries for the second time, Nairin addressed Grandfather Mykola telepathically:

“It seems coffee is something of a sacred drink in your world?”

“Of course,” the barista replied without hesitation as he turned back towards the counter. “We have some rare varieties. This espresso has been roasted to the third stage of caramelisation, with notes of orange and dark chocolate. If you like, I can make you a special Lviv-style coffee.”

Once again, silence settled over the table. The barista smiled politely and returned behind the counter.

“Your world is getting stranger by the minute,” Nairin said, breaking the silence. “Portals within a Plan… another new Kharakternyk? Isn’t that a little too much for one world? Grandfather, what else don’t we know?”

The old man looked at the young man awkwardly.

“The world is changing, and many more wonders await us. We mastered spatial portals back in the days of the steppe wars. As for this young man… I’m no less surprised than you are. I didn’t sense him the way I sensed Dmytro.”

“We should check,” Dmytro said, walking over to the barista.

He looked the man in the eyes and asked silently:

“When did you become able to communicate without saying a single word?”

The barista’s eyes widened. He could hear a voice—clear and distinct—but Dmytro’s mouth had not moved. Bewildered, he twisted the towel in his hands.

MARAT — INITIATION

Marat was an ordinary Ukrainian young man. His slightly wavy black hair, sharp, expressive features, and warm brown eyes that always seemed to be smiling gave him a natural charisma often associated with people of Caucasian origin. He was athletic but not bulky: his movements were precise and light, like those of someone who kept himself fit without showing off. He knew he appealed to girls, but used that advantage carefully.

His greatest passion, however, was coffee. He knew everything about it, from roast levels to the chemistry of aromatic oils. Even as a child, he could distinguish Ethiopian Arabica from Kenyan just by smell. In high school, he could tell by ear whether a coffee grinder was properly adjusted. He joked that he could recognise his own blend in a dark room, so finely could he detect notes of citrus, caramel, cocoa, or berries in freshly ground beans.

Marat’s favourite was natural-process Ethiopian coffee, which he brewed using the pour-over method. He insisted that this was the only way to “hear the true voice of the coffee bean”. Nobody around him understood the phrase except him. He also liked testing the customers who came into his café: with just a few questions, he could tell whether he was dealing with a true coffee enthusiast or someone who had simply come in for something tasty.

Although his parents had been born in Ukraine, they preserved the memory of their ancestors from the Caucasus. Their family had moved to the capital in the time of his great-grandfather, settled there, and put down roots. Since then, they had considered themselves Ukrainians so sincerely and naturally that nobody questioned it. His father worked for a transport company, and his mother was a schoolteacher. Both were quiet, hardworking people with strong family traditions.

When Marat turned twenty-five, his parents gave him some money to start his own business, and he opened a small coffee shop near the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra without hesitation. It was a place where he could indulge his love of coffee every day—quietly, passionately, and in his own way.

The business was not particularly profitable. Tourists came irregularly, monks rarely, and local residents had their own favourite places. But Marat never complained. He did what he loved, preparing coffee using different recipes and serving it with cakes, biscuits, or dried fruit. He always knew exactly which pairing would best bring out the flavour of a particular drink.

His café was known for ensuring that even the most demanding guest received “their” coffee—and remembered it for a long time.

That day, grey clouds covered the sky, and now and then a brief but unpleasant shower fell. There were few customers. Marat had already accepted that the day would end at a loss, but he was not upset. The weather was the weather.

That was when a group of people entered the café. At first glance, they seemed a little odd to him. Not because of their appearance, but because of the way they moved and looked around, as if they had just returned from a long journey.

At first they talked about ordinary things. They were cheerful, friendly, and a little tired. Marat brought their first order, and they thanked him politely. But when he served the second, something strange happened.

One of them came up to the counter and, looking straight into his eyes, asked:

“Tell me, when did you become able to communicate without saying a single word aloud?”

The question struck Marat as very strange. He was about to make a joke, when he suddenly realised that the young man had not opened his mouth.

“Must have imagined it,” Marat thought, although the sensation had been far too real. He grew nervous. He wanted to say something, but the young man repeated the same question aloud.

The barista was bewildered. He did not even notice that he was twisting the towel in his hands. It occurred to him that he was near the Lavra, so if anything happened, the monks would surely protect him from any evil spirits. The awkward silence dragged on until, fortunately, the oldest member of the group came over and invited everyone to sit down and talk.

An hour later, the newly initiated Kharakternyk, still somewhat shocked by what he had become, was receiving his first instructions.

“The main thing is: don’t jump anywhere just yet. You could accidentally end up in a dead world and never return. There are only three of us on this planet as it is,” Mykola said in a didactic tone.

“We can take care of the training,” Nairin offered. “We have an excellent training complex and the best instructors.”

The old man looked gratefully at the young man from another Plan, then turned back to Marat.

“If you agree, we can begin your training tomorrow.”

Marat agreed, somewhat stunned but genuinely interested. He had no idea how radically his life would change because of a single visit from a group of strangers to his café. But he warned them that he had to put a few things in order: temporarily hand the café over to his sister and inform his parents. They agreed that Dmytro would take him to Nairin’s Plan the next day.

As they were leaving, Marat suddenly stopped them.

“Grandfather, one last question. You said that Kharakternyks are an exclusively Ukrainian phenomenon. So how did I become one if my ancestors came from the Caucasus?”

“The name ‘Kharakternyks’ is Ukrainian; you’re right about that,” Mykola replied. “But the ability itself has nothing to do with a gene pool. Accept it as it is. See you tomorrow.”

After saying goodbye, the group walked away, and Marat decided to close the café. After news like that, he could no longer calmly wait for customers. He quickly locked up and headed briskly towards his sister’s home. The whole family lived nearby.

On the way, he met his nephew, a boy of about fourteen, a little mischievous and with school grades slightly above average. Seeing his uncle, the boy got up from a bench and came over to say hello.

Marat could not resist. As they shook hands, he asked silently:

“Can you communicate by thought too, nephew?”

The boy’s confused but slightly surprised expression told Marat that he had guessed correctly. The kid had heard him.

Torn between shock and joy at being able to share his new gift with someone close to him, Marat smiled and suggested they talk at home.

DMYTRO — THE MAP OF THE UNIVERSE

“We have one more now,” Grandfather Mykola announced. The news caught the travellers just outside the house.

Nairin raised his eyebrows in surprise. The others took the words more calmly. For Dmytro, all of this was new and almost unreal, while the guards and the medic girl simply registered the information in silence, without interfering in the affairs of the Kharakternyks.

“Your world is becoming more and more interesting, Grandfather,” Nairin said as he turned towards the portal. “I’ll take my people. We’ll jump back and prepare the instructors and Raien for tomorrow. I think he’ll be very surprised.”

“Nairin,” Grandfather Mykola said, calmly stopping him. “I would like to speak with your Council. It concerns both our worlds.”

The young man studied the old man for several seconds, as if trying to understand how serious he was, then gave a brief nod.

“I’ll pass it on.”

The portal opened almost silently. The Kharakternyk let the soldiers and Leila pass through, then disappeared last into the bright membrane of the passage. A moment later, the yard was quiet again. Only the wind lifted the fresh snow, and somewhere in the distance a dog barked.

Dmytro remained standing by the gate, still not fully realising that in just a few days his life had moved entirely beyond the bounds of anything normal.

“Come inside,” the old man said quietly.

Inside, the house smelled of tea, dried herbs, and fresh bread. Everything felt strangely simple and homely after portals, other worlds, and conversations about the extinction of civilisations. Grandmother silently placed cups on the table and sat a little apart, listening carefully to her husband.

The Kharakternyks settled around the large wooden table.

“I have never seen such a coincidence: two Kharakternyks initiated in a single day. Not in all my long years,” Grandfather Mykola began, gazing thoughtfully out of the window. “And that worries me even more than it surprises me. Such things do not happen by chance. It is as if the world itself is beginning to wake up.”

“Do you think there will be someone else tomorrow?” Dmytro asked.

“I think so. And not just one. Kharakternyks used to appear once every few years, sometimes even once in a decade. Now everything is speeding up, as if someone has suddenly set a long-stopped mechanism in motion.”

Dmytro sighed heavily and rubbed his eyes.

“Together with your wife’s prophecy, it sounds as though we’re supposed to stop the end of the world. Honestly, that seems a little… too much for a handful of people.”

The old man gave a faint smile.

“When the world finds itself on the brink, safeguards begin to activate. Sometimes even safeguards nobody knew existed. Humanity has approached the edge before. We simply did not understand it at the time.”

He stood up and slowly walked to an old storeroom. A minute later he returned carrying a large wrapped bundle, tied with a strap darkened by age.

“When there were many more of us, we travelled between worlds. Back then, Kharakternyks still believed they could find answers somewhere out there, among the other Plans. I was never much of a traveller. I spent more time here, working with records and collecting the accounts of those who returned—and recording even more carefully the stories of those who did not. But I saw enough to lose the ability to sleep peacefully.”

He unfolded an old diagram.

At its centre was their world, a central circle from which dozens of branches spread out. Each branch led to other Plans. Some lines stretched far away and branched again and again, forming an entire web of worlds.

Every circle had been crossed out. Dmytro felt an unpleasant chill in his chest.

“We called it the Tree of Plans,” the old man said quietly. “The central world is ours. More passages branch out from it than from any other. There was nothing else like it. We explored dozens of directions, but no other world had such a branching structure.”

He slowly ran a finger along the diagram.

“There are one thousand one hundred and thirty-eight Plans from which travellers have returned. There may be more. Many more. But these were enough to make us understand one terrible thing.”

Grandfather Mykola fell silent for a few seconds, as if he still hated saying it aloud.

“They are all dead.”

Silence filled the house.

“Not all in the same way. Some looked as if people had simply vanished overnight. The cities were intact, vehicles rusted on the roads, and belongings still lay in apartments—even children’s toys. In one of those worlds, I entered a school. The timetable was still hanging there. An unfinished problem was on the blackboard. But there were no people anywhere. Only dust and silence.

“In another world, we found nothing but ruins. There had once been a war so terrible that the ground near the cities had turned to glass. And there was a world where nature had defeated humanity. Forests had grown through skyscrapers, roots had split the concrete, and underground rivers flowed through the metro. We found records from the final days of that civilisation. To the very end, the people there refused to believe they had lost.”

Dmytro said nothing.

“And there is one world I still see in my dreams,” the old man added quietly. “Everything there looked alive. The sun was shining, power stations were working, and some systems were still maintaining order automatically. But there was not a single sound. No birds, no animals, no people. Absolute silence across an entire planet. It was enough to drive anyone mad.”

He lowered himself heavily back into his chair.

“Our world is the only one where humanity is still alive. The only one of them all.”

Dmytro slowly looked at the diagram again.

It no longer seemed like a strange map. It looked like a cemetery of civilisations.

Grandfather Mykola turned to his wife.

“My dear… what do you see now?”

Grandmother remained silent for a long time. Her fingers trembled slightly as she touched her cup.
