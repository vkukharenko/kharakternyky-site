“Weapons won’t be necessary here,” he tossed over his shoulder. “My wife has just made pancakes.”

Dmytro turned around. The guards were already standing behind him, their needle-guns at the ready, while Nairin held two sabres.

The portal quietly closed behind them.

GRANDFATHER MYKOLA — BACKGROUND

The village stood among forests and fields, which at dawn disappeared into the mist. The smell of fresh earth and charred wood hung in the air. Several houses stood on a rise, surrounded by a tall wooden fence, two metres high. Here and there, small openings had been made so defenders could shoot arrows at potential attackers. The massive gates could be shut as soon as the villagers sensed danger.

The year 1576 in Podillia had been hot, and the harvest was poorer than the year before. Many people from neighbouring villages had stayed away from the fairs because they lacked grain. Not the people of this village, though. They were used to working hard and earning a decent profit. Not every village could boast such prosperity. Many of the men were away that day, driving ox-carts loaded with provisions to the nearest town, where they could sell their goods at a good price and buy what they needed: horse tack, tools, salt, and various little things for the girls.

Mykolka, a lively boy with a slightly upturned nose and a long neck, was once again racing along a narrow field path towards the forest. He was a restless child, and every day one of the adults would chase him with a switch. Laughing, he would run away and hide among the trees. It had become an ordinary sight in the village where he had been born and raised.

Looking back, Mykolka made sure nobody was chasing him, then scrambled up a tree in an instant. No one would see him here, and he would have a good view of the surroundings, so nobody could sneak up on him with a switch. The tree was tall and sturdy, and its branches made a comfortable, safe perch. He could wait until evening and then return home in peace. Nobody would even remember what he had done.

The boy settled onto the thickest branch and began to daydream. Suddenly he jolted, slid down, and raced headlong towards the village, shouting at the top of his lungs:

“Tatars! Tatars!”

Horsemen were riding out of the forest, somewhere near the horizon. They were galloping towards the village, raising clouds of dust. Mykolka’s warning did not go unheard. The villagers immediately began preparing to defend their homes. The men hurried to arm themselves and take positions behind the wooden palisade. The heavy gates slammed shut, cutting the village off from the approaching enemy, while the women rushed with their children into cellars. Such raids were not unusual; they were almost commonplace. These lands lived by their own rules: sometimes the Tatars came to plunder Ukrainian villages, and sometimes the Cossacks set out on retaliatory campaigns against them. It had become part of a cruel order of things: when the enemy came, you had to stand and fight, or they would grow bolder and return without fear.

But this time the raiding party was far too large. The number of horsemen seemed to grow by the minute. The villagers exchanged glances, tightened their grips on their weapons, and prepared either to fight back or to die. Those who surrendered faced slavery. The people here loved their freedom dearly and were ready to defend it to the death.

Mykolka was an orphan. His mother had died giving birth to him, and his father had perished in one of the raids against the Tatars when the boy was five. He barely remembered his parents, and now there was no one to look after his safety. He decided to help in whatever way he could: bringing arrows to the archers, carrying water, and hauling stones to strengthen the defences.

The battle was brutal. This attack was different from the previous ones. The Tatars charged at everything alive like madmen, showing mercy to no one. After two hours, they broke through the defences, stormed into the village, began setting houses on fire, killing the elderly, and tying up the young to drive them away into captivity.

That morning, Mykolka had been living the ordinary life of a mischievous village boy. Now he stood in the middle of a dying village, stunned, breathing in the smoke that was consuming his past. He watched the invaders plunder a community that had been prosperous only that morning. Tears welled in his eyes: tears of helplessness, despair, and grief for his murdered and tortured neighbours.

He screamed. Into the void. Loudly, desperately.

The void answered.

They tied him up and threw him onto a cart among sacks that had once held the community’s treasures. He could see nothing after that, but they were going somewhere. The Tatars argued in their language. Then they shouted and hurried their horses onwards.

Mykolka heard a spirited whoop, and another battle began. He understood from the clash of sabres and the cries of the wounded and dying around him. When it was over, they pulled him off the cart and brought him before the Cossack ataman. The man looked at him carefully and asked:

“Was it you who called us, boy?”

And Mykolka understood who had answered him from the void. It had been the ataman’s voice.

They took Mykolka into their brotherhood, taught him everything, and turned him into a true fighter. He became skilled with two sabres, fighting both on horseback and on foot. But most importantly, they taught him sorcery, for this was no ordinary band. They were the legendary Kharakternyks. As it turned out, in his moment of despair he had called them to his aid. That was how he had been initiated and learned everything he knew, including how to travel between Plans.

Those were cruel years. The Tatars burned many villages and towns, killed the elderly, and took young people and children captive, selling them at markets in the Crimean Khanate. This went on for half a century, perhaps longer, until the Cossack state—the Zaporozhian Host—was established in these lands under the leadership of Khmel’s father, as everyone called him behind his back: Bohdan Khmelnytsky.

Mykolka, by then known as Cossack Mykola, took part in the creation of the state alongside his brothers-in-arms. But it did not last long. Mykola lived to see Austrian and Polish rule in these lands, followed by the long Muscovite occupation.

The community of Kharakternyks to which Cossack Mykola belonged changed over time. There were not many of them in Ukrainian lands, so they could not alter the historical course of their people. Besides, the community was not limited to a single nation. It maintained ties with other communities whose members could also travel between worlds and were skilled in warfare and magic. Their names differed, but their essence was the same.

The Germans had the “Schwarzgards”; the Scandinavians, the “Vandal Wargs”; and the Celts, the “Sidhe Folk”. Even the distant Japanese had their own community, known as the “Shinobi of the Worlds”. Shamans, witches, healers, velds, volkhvs, druids, vidars, sharlakhs.

For a while, Mykola collected these names and wrote them down. Eventually, however, the notes were lost somewhere, and the different names for Kharakternyks remained only in the Cossack’s memory.

As time passed, fewer and fewer new Kharakternyks—whatever they were called—appeared in the world. The old ones died or set off on journeys to other worlds and never returned. Their community grew smaller and smaller. By the beginning of the twentieth century, new initiations had ceased altogether.

Mykola himself lived through both World Wars. He helped create the Ukrainian People’s Republic, but did not live to see its victory. Later he joined the Ukrainian Insurgent Army, fighting both the Old Union and the Reich. He fought as a partisan in the forests, organised hideouts, and transported people and weapons, until the last flame of resistance went out in 1956.

Eventually, no longer Mykolka or Cossack Mykola, but Grandfather Mykola, he left his troubled past behind and began living quietly, watching the changing world. Cities grew, technology advanced, and he watched people forget the old wars.

He firmly identified with his people, although many other Kharakternyks did not concern themselves with such ties. So when a new Ukrainian state rose from the ashes at the end of the twentieth century, he was happy, even though he no longer took an active part in its creation.

He had also become the only Kharakternyk left in the entire world. This became a heavy burden for Grandfather Mykola. It changed the way he saw the world and made him avoid unnecessary heroic deeds. He was quietly fading away.

At the turn of the millennium, Mykola met a woman with the gift of prophecy in a church. She foretold the destruction of the planet, but her prophecies often changed, and he was the only one who believed her. Grandfather Mykola had understood the pattern: the world was constantly changing, and prophecies merely reflected those changes.

They married, and he revealed his secret to her. From then on, they lived together, protecting each other’s secrets and waiting for their time. Perhaps that time had come now.

Three days earlier, she had said that a great nuclear war would soon come and destroy humanity. That same day, Mykola heard the cry of a new Kharakternyk—Dmytro—in his mind. New Kharakternyks did not appear without reason. They appeared only when their time had come.

For three days, Grandfather Mykola searched for the new Kharakternyk and could not find him. He understood that the young man had most likely jumped to another Plan. So yesterday the old Kharakternyk had finally taken a desperate step: he used his own life force to send a message. It had greatly shortened his life—from many years to perhaps six months—but guaranteed that Dmytro would find his way back to his home world.

And today, when he sensed that the message had reached its recipient, his wife told him that the future had changed once again. Nuclear war remained inevitable, but there were now possible outcomes—both good and bad. The future had once again been swallowed by the mist.

DMYTRO — AT HOME

“You can call me Grandfather Mykola. And I’ll say it again: you’re quite safe here. Even the Shaheds don’t reach this place.”

With that, the old man gestured for everyone to come inside.

Nairin was probably the only person present, apart from Dmytro himself, who recognised Grandfather Mykola from the dreams Dmytro had shared with him. So Nairin was the first to put away his weapon and follow the old man and Dmytro inside. The others put their weapons away as well.

The house was spacious and bright. Its minimalist renovation sharply contrasted with the age of its owner. You might have expected such an old man to live in a creaking house full of antiques, but that was not the case. The walls were painted white with a barely perceptible blue tint—clean, fresh, and spotless. The first room was a living room. A large, comfortable table stood in the middle, and five of the visitors gathered around it. Wheeled chairs completed the modern look, while large plastic-framed windows let in enough light that there was no need to switch on the round ceiling lamp, which seemed to lie almost flat against the matte ceiling.

A kind-looking elderly woman came out of the adjoining room. She was carrying a large plate of pancakes and little cups of raspberry jam on a plastic tray. Grandfather Mykola was brewing herbal tea, its aroma spreading through the house and captivating the guests. At last everything was ready. They sat down at the table and began to talk.

“First of all, welcome back, Dmytro. You are the first Kharakternyk to be initiated in the last three hundred years. I’m glad it happened—it means our world still has hope,” the old man began. “And who are your friends?”

The guards and Leila introduced themselves one by one. The old man welcomed each of them, finding a few warm words for everyone. At last it was Nairin’s turn. He looked at the old man and greeted him telepathically. Grandfather Mykola’s eyebrows shot up in surprise. His astonishment was genuine, and it quickly gave way to curiosity.

“Well, I’ll be! So you’re not just visitors—you’re travellers too. Quite a group! Dmytro, you really are a find for our world. Tell me about yourselves.”

Nairin opened his thoughts to the old man. Just as Dmytro had recently experienced, Mykola began receiving information in one enormous stream. The guards had long since realised that Kharakternyks communicated telepathically, so they calmly enjoyed their tea and pancakes. Leila was a little nervous, but kept herself under control. It was clear that it was not easy for her: questions seemed to be straining at the leash like wild horses.

When Grandfather Mykola had received everything Nairin wanted to share, he began speaking aloud, out of respect for those who could access the thought-link through their bracelets. His account was lively and engaging. He briefly recounted his own story without burdening the guests with unnecessary historical detail, focusing instead on the life of Kharakternyks in this Plan. Everyone listened intently. Even Leila remained silent, attentive and focused.

When the old man finished, a pause followed. Nairin broke it with a direct and painful question:

“So your world, like ours, is doomed. And at that very moment a new Kharakternyk—Dmytro—was initiated. What’s more, immediately after his initiation he ended up in our Plan, even though it is very far from yours and the probability of such a journey is almost zero. Have I understood correctly?”

Grandfather Mykola did not answer at once. He looked at Dmytro for a long time, his gaze heavy and searching, and only then nodded.

“We must help each other. Our world and yours,” he said to Nairin, although he was looking straight into Dmytro’s eyes. “I don’t know exactly how. I can only guess, and even those guesses are based on my wife’s prophecies, which change with events. But I can offer you a little trip around our Plan. How about a visit to the capital and a cup of coffee? I know some good places.”

The old man stood up, and everyone else followed. Since their journey to a new Plan had gone well, the suggestion suited them all. Leila was especially pleased; she had barely been able to hold back a flood of questions.

“You’re dressed too lightly,” the old man remarked, opening a wardrobe. It contained a wide variety of clothes, and, to the guests’ surprise, he found warm things for everyone.

At last they all went outside, except for the grandmother, who stayed home to take care of a few things. Grandfather Mykola explained that she did not like large groups, although she was happy to travel with one or two guests.

“Dmytro, open a portal to the capital,” the old man suggested.

“Grandfather, I thought it was impossible to open a portal within a single Plan,” Dmytro replied in surprise. He had been clearly told that portals could be opened only between Plans, not within one.

“Try it the way you opened the portal to me. Don’t argue,” the old man said calmly.

Dmytro shrugged and tried. Nothing happened until Grandfather Mykola gave him the name of a landmark: the lower grounds of the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra.

The monastery immediately appeared in his mind, and the portal opened.

There were no flashes of light or sounds. Just a transparent cut in reality through which a magnificent building could be seen. Dmytro himself could hardly believe what he had done. Nairin’s surprised exclamation rang out beside him—he too had been sceptical about the idea.

Without wasting time, the whole group stepped onto the stone paths of the Lavra’s Lower Grounds.

The air was cool and slightly damp, filled with the smell of old stone, incense, and wet earth. Whitewashed buildings with dark roofs lined the narrow, winding paths. Low metal lanterns, neat benches, slopes leading down towards the Dnipro beyond the walls, and the distant ringing of bells—all of it created a feeling of peace and antiquity.

They headed towards a small, cosy coffee shop. Heavy grey clouds covered the sky, and the light had taken on a muted autumn quality.

Inside, it was warm and bright. Wooden tables, soft chairs, photographs of the Lavra on the walls, and clay pots of rosemary, mint, and thyme on the windowsills. The air was filled with the aroma of freshly roasted coffee, with notes of chocolate and caramel.

The barista was a stocky, dark-haired man with Caucasian features. He worked with confidence and precision. His movements belonged to someone who had spent years studying coffee.

The conversation at the table was calm. When the barista brought coffee and pastries for the second time, Nairin addressed Grandfather Mykola telepathically:

“It seems coffee is something of a sacred drink in your world?”

“Of course,” the barista replied without hesitation as he turned back towards the counter. “We have some rare varieties. This espresso has been roasted to the third stage of caramelisation, with notes of orange and dark chocolate. If you like, I can make you a special Lviv-style coffee.”

Once again, silence settled over the table. The barista smiled politely and returned behind the counter.

“Your world is getting stranger by the minute,” Nairin said, breaking the silence. “Portals within a Plan… another new Kharakternyk? Isn’t that a little too much for one world? Grandfather, what else don’t we know?”

The old man looked at the young man awkwardly.

“The world is changing, and many more wonders await us. We mastered spatial portals back in the days of the steppe wars. As for this young man… I’m no less surprised than you are. I didn’t sense him the way I sensed Dmytro.”

“We should check,” Dmytro said, walking over to the barista.

He looked the man in the eyes and asked silently:

“When did you become able to communicate without saying a single word?”

The barista’s eyes widened. He could hear a voice—clear and distinct—but Dmytro’s mouth had not moved. Bewildered, he twisted the towel in his hands.

MARAT — INITIATION

Marat was an ordinary Ukrainian young man. His slightly wavy black hair, sharp, expressive features, and warm brown eyes that always seemed to be smiling gave him a natural charisma often associated with people of Caucasian origin. He was athletic but not bulky: his movements were precise and light, like those of someone who kept himself fit without showing off. He knew he appealed to girls, but used that advantage carefully.

His greatest passion, however, was coffee. He knew everything about it, from roast levels to the chemistry of aromatic oils. Even as a child, he could distinguish Ethiopian Arabica from Kenyan just by smell. In high school, he could tell by ear whether a coffee grinder was properly adjusted. He joked that he could recognise his own blend in a dark room, so finely could he detect notes of citrus, caramel, cocoa, or berries in freshly ground beans.

Marat’s favourite was natural-process Ethiopian coffee, which he brewed using the pour-over method. He insisted that this was the only way to “hear the true voice of the coffee bean”. Nobody around him understood the phrase except him. He also liked testing the customers who came into his café: with just a few questions, he could tell whether he was dealing with a true coffee enthusiast or someone who had simply come in for something tasty.

Although his parents had been born in Ukraine, they preserved the memory of their ancestors from the Caucasus. Their family had moved to the capital in the time of his great-grandfather, settled there, and put down roots. Since then, they had considered themselves Ukrainians so sincerely and naturally that nobody questioned it. His father worked for a transport company, and his mother was a schoolteacher. Both were quiet, hardworking people with strong family traditions.

When Marat turned twenty-five, his parents gave him some money to start his own business, and he opened a small coffee shop near the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra without hesitation. It was a place where he could indulge his love of coffee every day—quietly, passionately, and in his own way.

The business was not particularly profitable. Tourists came irregularly, monks rarely, and local residents had their own favourite places. But Marat never complained. He did what he loved, preparing coffee using different recipes and serving it with cakes, biscuits, or dried fruit. He always knew exactly which pairing would best bring out the flavour of a particular drink.

His café was known for ensuring that even the most demanding guest received “their” coffee—and remembered it for a long time.

That day, grey clouds covered the sky, and now and then a brief but unpleasant shower fell. There were few customers. Marat had already accepted that the day would end at a loss, but he was not upset. The weather was the weather.

That was when a group of people entered the café. At first glance, they seemed a little odd to him. Not because of their appearance, but because of the way they moved and looked around, as if they had just returned from a long journey.

At first they talked about ordinary things. They were cheerful, friendly, and a little tired. Marat brought their first order, and they thanked him politely. But when he served the second, something strange happened.

One of them came up to the counter and, looking straight into his eyes, asked:

“Tell me, when did you become able to communicate without saying a single word aloud?”

The question struck Marat as very strange. He was about to make a joke, when he suddenly realised that the young man had not opened his mouth.

“Must have imagined it,” Marat thought, although the sensation had been far too real. He grew nervous. He wanted to say something, but the young man repeated the same question aloud.

The barista was bewildered. He did not even notice that he was twisting the towel in his hands. It occurred to him that he was near the Lavra, so if anything happened, the monks would surely protect him from any evil spirits. The awkward silence dragged on until, fortunately, the oldest member of the group came over and invited everyone to sit down and talk.

An hour later, the newly initiated Kharakternyk, still somewhat shocked by what he had become, was receiving his first instructions.

“The main thing is: don’t jump anywhere just yet. You could accidentally end up in a dead world and never return. There are only three of us on this planet as it is,” Mykola said in a didactic tone.

“We can take care of the training,” Nairin offered. “We have an excellent training complex and the best instructors.”

The old man looked gratefully at the young man from another Plan, then turned back to Marat.

“If you agree, we can begin your training tomorrow.”

Marat agreed, somewhat stunned but genuinely interested. He had no idea how radically his life would change because of a single visit from a group of strangers to his café. But he warned them that he had to put a few things in order: temporarily hand the café over to his sister and inform his parents. They agreed that Dmytro would take him to Nairin’s Plan the next day.

As they were leaving, Marat suddenly stopped them.

“Grandfather, one last question. You said that Kharakternyks are an exclusively Ukrainian phenomenon. So how did I become one if my ancestors came from the Caucasus?”

“The name ‘Kharakternyks’ is Ukrainian; you’re right about that,” Mykola replied. “But the ability itself has nothing to do with a gene pool. Accept it as it is. See you tomorrow.”

After saying goodbye, the group walked away, and Marat decided to close the café. After news like that, he could no longer calmly wait for customers. He quickly locked up and headed briskly towards his sister’s home. The whole family lived nearby.

On the way, he met his nephew, a boy of about fourteen, a little mischievous and with school grades slightly above average. Seeing his uncle, the boy got up from a bench and came over to say hello.

Marat could not resist. As they shook hands, he asked silently:

“Can you communicate by thought too, nephew?”

The boy’s confused but slightly surprised expression told Marat that he had guessed correctly. The kid had heard him.

Torn between shock and joy at being able to share his new gift with someone close to him, Marat smiled and suggested they talk at home.

DMYTRO — THE MAP OF THE UNIVERSE

“We have one more now,” Grandfather Mykola announced. The news caught the travellers just outside the house.

Nairin raised his eyebrows in surprise. The others took the words more calmly. For Dmytro, all of this was new and almost unreal, while the guards and the medic girl simply registered the information in silence, without interfering in the affairs of the Kharakternyks.

“Your world is becoming more and more interesting, Grandfather,” Nairin said as he turned towards the portal. “I’ll take my people. We’ll jump back and prepare the instructors and Raien for tomorrow. I think he’ll be very surprised.”

“Nairin,” Grandfather Mykola said, calmly stopping him. “I would like to speak with your Council. It concerns both our worlds.”

The young man studied the old man for several seconds, as if trying to understand how serious he was, then gave a brief nod.

“I’ll pass it on.”

The portal opened almost silently. The Kharakternyk let the soldiers and Leila pass through, then disappeared last into the bright membrane of the passage. A moment later, the yard was quiet again. Only the wind lifted the fresh snow, and somewhere in the distance a dog barked.

Dmytro remained standing by the gate, still not fully realising that in just a few days his life had moved entirely beyond the bounds of anything normal.

“Come inside,” the old man said quietly.

Inside, the house smelled of tea, dried herbs, and fresh bread. Everything felt strangely simple and homely after portals, other worlds, and conversations about the extinction of civilisations. Grandmother silently placed cups on the table and sat a little apart, listening carefully to her husband.

The Kharakternyks settled around the large wooden table.

“I have never seen such a coincidence: two Kharakternyks initiated in a single day. Not in all my long years,” Grandfather Mykola began, gazing thoughtfully out of the window. “And that worries me even more than it surprises me. Such things do not happen by chance. It is as if the world itself is beginning to wake up.”

“Do you think there will be someone else tomorrow?” Dmytro asked.

“I think so. And not just one. Kharakternyks used to appear once every few years, sometimes even once in a decade. Now everything is speeding up, as if someone has suddenly set a long-stopped mechanism in motion.”

Dmytro sighed heavily and rubbed his eyes.

“Together with your wife’s prophecy, it sounds as though we’re supposed to stop the end of the world. Honestly, that seems a little… too much for a handful of people.”

The old man gave a faint smile.

“When the world finds itself on the brink, safeguards begin to activate. Sometimes even safeguards nobody knew existed. Humanity has approached the edge before. We simply did not understand it at the time.”

He stood up and slowly walked to an old storeroom. A minute later he returned carrying a large wrapped bundle, tied with a strap darkened by age.

“When there were many more of us, we travelled between worlds. Back then, Kharakternyks still believed they could find answers somewhere out there, among the other Plans. I was never much of a traveller. I spent more time here, working with records and collecting the accounts of those who returned—and recording even more carefully the stories of those who did not. But I saw enough to lose the ability to sleep peacefully.”

He unfolded an old diagram.

At its centre was their world, a central circle from which dozens of branches spread out. Each branch led to other Plans. Some lines stretched far away and branched again and again, forming an entire web of worlds.

Every circle had been crossed out. Dmytro felt an unpleasant chill in his chest.

“We called it the Tree of Plans,” the old man said quietly. “The central world is ours. More passages branch out from it than from any other. There was nothing else like it. We explored dozens of directions, but no other world had such a branching structure.”

He slowly ran a finger along the diagram.

“There are one thousand one hundred and thirty-eight Plans from which travellers have returned. There may be more. Many more. But these were enough to make us understand one terrible thing.”

Grandfather Mykola fell silent for a few seconds, as if he still hated saying it aloud.

“They are all dead.”

Silence filled the house.

“Not all in the same way. Some looked as if people had simply vanished overnight. The cities were intact, vehicles rusted on the roads, and belongings still lay in apartments—even children’s toys. In one of those worlds, I entered a school. The timetable was still hanging there. An unfinished problem was on the blackboard. But there were no people anywhere. Only dust and silence.

“In another world, we found nothing but ruins. There had once been a war so terrible that the ground near the cities had turned to glass. And there was a world where nature had defeated humanity. Forests had grown through skyscrapers, roots had split the concrete, and underground rivers flowed through the metro. We found records from the final days of that civilisation. To the very end, the people there refused to believe they had lost.”

Dmytro said nothing.

“And there is one world I still see in my dreams,” the old man added quietly. “Everything there looked alive. The sun was shining, power stations were working, and some systems were still maintaining order automatically. But there was not a single sound. No birds, no animals, no people. Absolute silence across an entire planet. It was enough to drive anyone mad.”

He lowered himself heavily back into his chair.

“Our world is the only one where humanity is still alive. The only one of them all.”

Dmytro slowly looked at the diagram again.

It no longer seemed like a strange map. It looked like a cemetery of civilisations.

Grandfather Mykola turned to his wife.

“My dear… what do you see now?”

Grandmother remained silent for a long time. Her fingers trembled slightly as she touched her cup.

GRANDMOTHER — THE CHANGING FUTURE

“It used to be simpler,” she said quietly. “I could see a single line of the future. Terrible, but clear. Our world was perishing in nuclear fire. It was so vivid, as if I were already standing among the ruins.”

She raised her eyes to Dmytro.

“But after you appeared, everything changed.”

“How?” he asked softly.

“The future fell apart. Before, there was one road. Now there are many. They cross, disappear, and change before my very eyes. As though someone were constantly rewriting the fate of the world.”

Fear crept into her voice.

“I see cities in flames. I see a black sky. I see people fleeing danger underground. I see oceans without ships and enormous, empty megacities. But at the same time, other images have begun to appear… images I never saw before.”

She closed her eyes.

“I see people from different worlds standing together. I see new cities that do not yet exist. I see stars… so many stars. But everything is shrouded in a thick mist. As if the world has not yet decided whether it wants to live.”

A chill ran down Dmytro’s back.

“And which outcome is the most likely?”

The old woman smiled sadly.

“Today I can no longer answer that as I once could. The future has become unstable. But for the first time in many years, I no longer see only death. And that frightens me even more than the old prophecy.”

“Why?”

“Because now everything depends on people. Before, the end seemed inevitable. But now… now you have a chance to break the pattern. To save everything—or destroy it once and for all.”

Silence fell.

An old clock ticked quietly somewhere in the corner.

Dmytro slowly got to his feet. Suddenly, he wanted to run away from all this information. To know nothing about dead worlds or the danger hanging over his own. Just to be alone with himself.

“All right. We’ll talk to Nairin and his Council tomorrow. There’s nothing we can decide now anyway.”

Grandfather Mykola looked at him attentively and gave a faint smile. This was exactly the reaction he had expected. He saw neither fear nor panic in this new Kharakternyk, and for the first time in many decades the old Kharakternyk felt not resignation, but hope. Still fragile, almost imperceptible, but real.

NAIRIN — ANOTHER CONVERSATION WITH THE HETMAN

The portal closed behind the Kharakternyks almost soundlessly. After the cold but wonderfully alive air of Dmytro’s world, Nairin’s home Plan greeted him with the familiar smell of metal and plastic, along with the faint vibration of the station, always perceptible somewhere at the edge of awareness.

Leila and the two guards stayed in the recreation area to discuss the day, while Nairin went straight to see the Hetman. The latter was already waiting for him.

On the way, the standard-bearer instinctively raised his hand and tried to picture the room where the Hetman usually worked. This was how Dmytro had learned from Grandfather Mykola to open local portals: by forming a clear image of the destination. But space did not even stir in response. A moment later, Nairin exhaled irritably and lowered his hand.

His thoughts returned to everything he had seen in Dmytro’s world, making his mood even worse. The new Kharakternyks could open local portals within their own Plan. They could move between different places in their world as naturally as they travelled between Plans.

For the people of his civilisation, that was impossible.

Throughout the entire history of the Kharakternyks of the Zero World, no one had ever demonstrated such an ability. Even the most powerful Kharakternyks had to use transport to reach another city or station sector. And this limitation irritated Nairin more than anything else at that moment.

The station carried on with its usual life. Technicians, soldiers, and scientists passed by; route projections flickered in the air, and energy installations rumbled somewhere deep within the complex. But Nairin barely noticed any of it. His thoughts kept returning to the thought-packet he was putting together.

He did not dare transmit such information through the long-distance thought-link. Although the encounter with hostile people possessing abilities had taken place long ago, and no one had seen them since, Nairin had learned one lesson well: vigilance must never be abandoned. The information he carried was intended for the Hetman alone, and only he could decide how to use it.

When Nairin finally entered the spacious office with panoramic windows, the Hetman was already waiting. The old Kharakternyk sat beside a large desk, calm and composed, as if he had known in advance how important this conversation would be.

“You look as though you have returned not from another Plan, but from the abyss itself,” he said quietly, studying Nairin. “What did you see there that has left you so tense?”

Nairin merely nodded and sent him the thought-packet he had prepared. It contained everything he had gathered that day: his impressions of Dmytro’s world, the structure of his thoughtphone, the behaviour of the new Kharakternyks, the peculiarities of their transitions, the very nature of their abilities, their emotions during initiation, and the strange inner feeling that the people standing before him were at once utterly foreign and strangely familiar.

The Hetman closed his eyes. Silence settled over the office.

Several minutes passed before the old Kharakternyk opened them again and looked at Nairin with an entirely different expression. Not surprise, but deep contemplation.

“This truly is a new civilisation of Kharakternyks,” he said slowly. “A completely separate branch of development. Now I understand why you were in such a hurry.”

Nairin watched his reaction closely.

“They are in no way connected to our missing groups, nor to our Plan at all. Their abilities developed independently. The thoughtphone you transmitted is completely different. The foundation is similar, but its structure is radically different. I have never seen anything like it.”

He thoughtfully ran his fingers across the desk.

“And more importantly, I am almost certain they are not dark Kharakternyks.”

Nairin visibly tensed.

“The ones who killed Zairin also seemed like ordinary visitors,” he said quietly. “Calm. Peaceful. We let them into the station ourselves, allowed them to walk through our sectors. And then it was too late to change anything.”

The Hetman remained silent for several seconds, giving him time to speak.

“I remember it no less clearly than you do, Nairin,” he finally replied. “And I remember what you were like when you came back that day. But these people are not them.”

“Why are you so sure?” For the first time, Nairin’s voice carried tension, even anger. “We know almost nothing about the dark ones.”

“We know enough to see the difference,” the Hetman replied calmly. “The dark ones are a distortion of our own branch. We still do not understand exactly how they came into being, but their thoughtphones always bear traces of an internal distortion. You transmitted their structure to me yourself many years ago. Remember what you felt then.”

Nairin silently lowered his gaze.

“There was always instability,” the Hetman continued. “As if something inside the person were breaking. There is none of that here. Quite the opposite—the structure is remarkably stable, even harmonious.”

He leaned forward slightly.

“But that is not even the most important thing. Their emergence almost mirrors our own history.”

Nairin listened intently.

“The first Kharakternyks of the Zero Plan appeared after the global nuclear war, when humanity stood on the brink of extinction. Dmytro’s world is now entering a phase of inevitable catastrophe as well. Fear, political chaos, the threat of a great war, the sense that the end is approaching—all of it is too similar to our archival records to be a coincidence.”

The Hetman fell silent for a moment, as if trying to make sense of his own words.

“It seems as though reality itself sets the emergence of Kharakternyks in motion when a civilisation approaches a critical threshold. As if the world creates its own mechanism for survival.”

“But their abilities are different,” Nairin objected. “Local portals are something we have never been able to create. To us, it seems utterly impossible.”

“That is precisely why I say this is a separate branch,” the Hetman replied calmly. “We have studied our own abilities for years, yet not a single Kharakternyk in our world could even theoretically open passages within one Plan. Space behaves differently for us. We can cross the boundaries between worlds, but we cannot bend our own world in that way.”

He looked Nairin straight in the eyes.

“For them, it is natural. So the very principle behind their portals must be different. Perhaps their world has a different spatial structure. Or perhaps the nature of their abilities evolved in another direction. And, to be honest, I find that more fascinating than alarming.”

Nairin lowered his gaze thoughtfully.

“Even so, it worries me. There are too many coincidences in a single day.”

“That is understandable,” the Hetman said quietly. “After your brother’s death, you can no longer look at unfamiliar Kharakternyks as you once did. But fear must not interfere with analysis. Otherwise, it will begin to control your decisions.”

He leaned back in his chair.

“Does Grandfather Mykola want to meet with the Council?”

“Yes. He says it concerns both our worlds. And judging by his words, the matter is very serious.”

The Hetman pondered for only a moment.

“That is the right decision. If they have reached the same conclusions as we have, we must talk immediately. Preferably before events begin moving faster than we can understand them.”

He stood up.

“Prepare everything for tomorrow’s reception of the new students. Something tells me there will be more than two of them. I will arrange the meeting with Mykola. I think tomorrow’s conversation will be a difficult one.”

“Understood, Hetman.”

Nairin was about to leave when the Hetman spoke again.

“And one more thing. Do not look at them through the fear of the past. Otherwise, you will fail to see what truly matters now. I am almost certain these people have entered our lives for a reason.”

Nairin said nothing. Somewhere deep inside, he was already beginning to understand that this time they really had met not enemies, but something else. He very much wanted that to be true.

DMYTRO — AT THE OFFICE

At the last moment, Dmytro’s mood changed abruptly, and instead of opening a portal to his lonely apartment, he opened one to the office. The whole team was there, as if those days in another dimension had never happened.

Sensing his confusion, Dmytro began transmitting information slowly, step by step, as if carefully opening a door to a new world before him.

The initiation happened easily, as if their abilities had simply been waiting for someone to knock from within.

The question of whom to entrust with the project was settled quickly. They called in Dariia. She had long wanted to take on the role of team lead but had never dared to say so aloud. The whole team had seen her ambition, though everyone pretended otherwise.

When she heard the news, Dariia first went pale. Panic rose from somewhere deep inside, but quickly receded, giving way to determination. She pulled herself together like a tightly wound spring and immediately suggested holding a meeting to coordinate everything.

DAY 6 (22)
EVIGID — RECONNAISSANCE (KAMIL AND MICHELLE)

After the heated debates in the Conclave, some planetary consuls continued to insist on negotiations. In their view, even the worst peace was better than a war whose consequences no one could predict. They proposed considering any possible concessions—territory, resources, technology, and trade privileges.

Their reasoning was simple: if the enemy was capable of dialogue, it would be better to lose a part than risk the existence of an entire civilisation. The other members of the Conclave were becoming increasingly sceptical, but no one had conclusive evidence. All available data came from distant observations, intercepted signals, and analysis of the consequences of planetary takeovers.

To end the arguments and obtain at least some reliable information, it was decided to send a short reconnaissance mission to the last world recently seized by the aliens.

Preparations did not take long. The task seemed simple: survey the planet, assess its condition, look for signs of the occupiers’ activity and, if possible, attempt to establish contact. A small group of Kharakternyks led by Evigid was selected. His regular team would accompany him: Michelle, the pilot, and Kamil, the technician. After years of working together, they understood one another almost without words and were considered one of the best reconnaissance teams of the Zero Plan—and now the Second Plan as well.

The portal opened directly in orbit around the conquered world. As the shuttle passed through, a grey planet slowly came into view. At first, it did not look particularly ominous. From a distance, it was simply a large, dull-coloured sphere. Only after a few seconds did its unsettling features become clear. There were no clouds on its surface. No glint of oceans. No familiar colours in its atmosphere. The planet did not look destroyed. It looked as though every living thing had been removed.

Kamil silently examined the data streams from the shuttle’s scanners. The longer the survey continued, the gloomier her expression became.

“Atmospheric pressure is critically low,” she finally reported. “There is no oxygen. No signs of a complex biosphere either. If anything is still alive here, our sensors cannot detect it yet.”

“We’re going down,” Evigid replied curtly.

The shuttle changed course and began a smooth descent through what remained of the atmosphere. According to archival maps, they were heading towards the coast of one of the planet’s largest oceans. There had once been entire megacities here, major ports, and densely populated coastal regions. Now there was nothing below that resembled the civilisation that had once existed.

The lower they descended, the stronger the sense of unnaturalness became. The surface was not covered in ruins. There were no bombardment craters, remains of cities, or traces of a large-scale war. On the contrary, everything looked as if someone had deliberately removed every reminder of the world’s former inhabitants. Where an ocean had once roared, there now stretched a flat grey surface resembling solidified stone.

The landing capsule detached from the shuttle at a low altitude. Michelle remained in orbit, while the Kharakternyks descended to the planet’s surface, leaving one of their companions aboard the shuttle in case an emergency evacuation became necessary.

When the landing struts touched the ground, almost absolute silence surrounded them. There was no wind. No rustling vegetation. Not even the fine dust that usually rises during a landing.

Evigid was among the first to leave the capsule. For several seconds, he simply surveyed the surrounding area. An endless rocky plain stretched before him. The surface looked as if it had once been melted and then carefully levelled until perfectly smooth. No debris. No traces of cities. No signs of life.

“No organic matter whatsoever,” he said, checking the scanner readings. “Absolute zero.”

A strange feeling was growing inside them. Many had feared seeing the ruins of a civilisation. In reality, the absence of ruins was far more frightening. Ruins at least testified that life had once existed here. This place gave the impression that someone had deliberately erased the very concept of life from the planet’s surface.

Returning to the capsule, they continued surveying the continent with mobile scanners. The farther the group moved from the landing site, the stranger the picture became. The terrain had been levelled with unnatural precision. Entire mountain ranges had vanished. Vast depressions had been filled in. The planet resembled an enormous petrified shell from which someone had removed everything unnecessary.

The anomaly appeared near the polar region. Kamil was analysing another series of scans when she suddenly raised her head.

“There’s biological activity,” she said. “One source. Massive. And very large.”

Tension filled the cabin instantly. Michelle increased speed and steered the capsule towards the marked location. When the object appeared on the horizon, none of them could immediately understand what they were looking at.

At first, it resembled some kind of geological formation. Then they realised it was a tree.

An enormous tree.

Its dark, twisted trunk rose hundreds of metres above the plain. The bark was split by deep fissures, from which streams of dense gas poured continuously. Its roots spread for kilometres, breaking through the rocky surface and forming entire networks of tunnels.

Even from a distance, it looked unnatural. It did not seem to be part of the local ecosystem, or even a plant. It looked more like a tool designed to serve some purpose beyond human understanding.

“They’re rebuilding the atmosphere,” Evigid said quietly as the scanner data began to form a coherent picture. “This isn’t just a tree. It’s a terraforming system.”

After landing, the Kharakternyks deployed portable drones and sent them towards the trunk to collect samples. For the first few minutes, everything went smoothly. The drones transmitted data on the composition of the gases, the structure of the tissues, and the root system. Kamil had already begun compiling a preliminary report when the ground near the roots suddenly shuddered.

At first, it seemed like an ordinary rockslide. But within seconds it became clear that the stone was not moving on its own. It was literally parting, revealing hidden underground passages.

The first aliens emerged silently. Tall, massive creatures came out from beneath the roots as if they were part of the tree itself. Curved horns rose above their helmet-like heads, and their dense skin resembled natural armour. They carried heavy weapons: long, dark axes and rifle-like devices of an unfamiliar design.

The first shot rang out almost immediately. One of the drones exploded in mid-air, scattering into dozens of fragments.

“Contact!” one of the Kharakternyks reported.

Evigid made no attempt to hide his alarm. The situation was changing by the second. Until that moment, the mission had been reconnaissance. Now they were in a combat zone.

The Kharakternyks instantly drew their sabres and activated their wave sharpeners. Familiar waves of light raced along the blades, but even that did little to calm them. The surrounding terrain was completely open. No cover. No way to hide or take up a favourable position.

Meanwhile, Evigid continued extracting data from the surviving drones. He already understood that their main task was no longer to win, but to get home with the information.

The aliens moved strangely fast. Their massive bodies glided over the surface almost soundlessly, while their dull violet eyes constantly shifted direction, as if receiving data from a single source.

“They’re coordinating with one another!” Evigid shouted. “They have a shared signal!”

The next explosion sounded very close. The rocky surface burst apart, and one of the fragments struck his helmet. His vision went dark for a moment. When it returned, he saw one of the Kharakternyks already engaged in close combat. His sabre flashed with a cold light and cut the enemy’s weapon in two with a single blow, but more horned ones were already emerging from beneath the roots.

That was when it became obvious that they could not stay any longer.

“Fall back!” came the order.

It was the only right decision. The mission had been reconnaissance from the start. The information they had gathered had to be brought home at any cost.

Evigid opened a portal to their home Plan, and the Kharakternyks began withdrawing, covering one another. The group commander was the last to enter the passage. Before the portal closed, he caught one final glimpse of the dark tree rising above the lifeless plain and the horned ones continuing to emerge from beneath its roots.

Back at the orbital station, they remained silent for a long time. Each was trying to make sense of what they had seen. They all understood that they had witnessed more than just another world being conquered. They had seen what happened after the enemy’s victory.

“We left the equipment behind,” someone finally said.

“Let them analyse it,” Evigid replied. “There was only the capsule, and we no longer need it.”

He reviewed the collected material once more. The data left no room for other interpretations. Those trees were not weapons. They were not fortresses. They were not industrial complexes.

They were a declaration.

The aliens had not come to conquer people or subjugate their civilisation. They had come to replace it with something else. Completely. Without leaving a trace. Without any possibility of coexistence.

His final report was brief:

“Negotiations are impossible. There is no compatibility. This is not a war for territory. It is a war for the very right to exist.”

After adding his final signature, Evigid stared at the screen for several more seconds. For the first time since arriving in this star system, he was absolutely certain of his conclusion: there would be no compromise.

There would only be war.

DMYTRO — MEETING WITH THE HETMAN OF EARTH-0

The Hetman had blue skin and sharply defined features, almost as if they had been carved from stone. His skin was a dark, cold shade, while the deep wrinkles on his forehead and around his eyes made him look not merely like an elderly man, but like someone who had endured far too much in his life—losses and difficult decisions. His long grey hair was neatly combed back, revealing a high forehead, and his thick white beard gave him the appearance of a stern old warrior from ancient legends. Most striking of all were his eyes: pale, almost icy, with the heavy, attentive gaze of someone accustomed to assessing an interlocutor in seconds and rarely getting it wrong.

For some reason, Dmytro still felt awkward around members of this race, even though his rational mind had long accepted that they were people just like him. Yet his gaze involuntarily lingered on their unnatural skin colour, creating a strange tension inside him. Grandfather Mykola, on the other hand, seemed not to notice it at all. He calmly approached Sukhum, shook his hand firmly, and greeted him.

The handshake lasted much longer than was usual between strangers. The two old Kharakternyks silently assessed one another, as if trying to understand everything that mattered in a few seconds without saying a word. And strangely enough, Dmytro almost physically sensed the moment when genuine respect arose between them. It was neither diplomatic politeness nor formal interest, but a recognition of the strength, experience, and character of the person standing before them.

“Call me Sukhum. I’m glad to meet you,” the Hetman said calmly in a low, slightly hoarse voice.

He spoke slowly and confidently, with no desire whatsoever to impress. Yet that very restraint was far more powerful than any display of authority.

Grandfather Mykola and Dmytro introduced themselves as well. At that moment, Sukhum’s atamans, the standard-bearer commanders, entered the room.

One of them was Nairin. Dmytro was not even surprised that the young man held such a position. After everything he had witnessed over the past few days, it seemed entirely natural. Nairin’s manner had always conveyed more than discipline: it showed someone accustomed to taking responsibility and making decisions.

The second man was dark-skinned, with a bare scalp. He introduced himself as Iman. The third had long, luxuriant hair and gave his name as Budulai.

Everyone took their seats around the table and spent a while silently studying one another. Nairin was the only one who already knew everyone present, so he remained relatively calm. For the others, this meeting seemed almost historic. Two worlds that, only a few days earlier, had not even suspected one another’s existence were now sitting at the same table, trying to understand what was happening to them.

Grandfather Mykola spoke first.

“First of all, I want to thank you for agreeing to this meeting. I believe you already know enough about us, so we can get down to the main subject.”

He looked carefully at those present, as if making sure he had everyone’s attention, and continued:

“Just a few days ago, I was the last Kharakternyk in our Plan. My wife, a seer, and I were already preparing to face the inevitable end of our civilisation with dignity. But Dmytro’s arrival shattered that inevitability. And almost immediately after his initiation, he came to you.”

The old man paused briefly, then continued in a much more serious tone:

“This morning, I became the new Hetman of our world. The previous Hetman died more than a hundred years ago, and the position had remained vacant ever since. The very emergence of a new Hetman is clear evidence that the community of Kharakternyks in my world has begun to revive. But that is not the only thing that surprised me.”

Now it was no longer simply Grandfather Mykola speaking, but Hetman Mykolai. He took a map from his bag—the same one he had shown Dmytro the day before.

“This is everything our community knows about the universe.”

Mykolai began spreading the paper map across the table, but Sukhum stopped him with a gesture. With one swift movement, he scanned the data carrier and activated a holographic projection that reproduced the map precisely.

Grandfather Mykola nodded gratefully and turned the projection so that everyone could see it clearly.

“This is the part of the map our travellers once explored most thoroughly. You could call it the safest and most extensively studied region of all the Plans known to us. And…” He paused. “You have never been here.”

An attentive silence fell over the room.

Having familiarised himself with the projection controls, Grandfather Mykola drew a straight line from the central Plan of his world, across an empty section of the map, and ended it at a new marker.

“Your world has never existed here. No one from our Plan has ever seen a route to you. This connection is new, and it was opened by the first newly initiated Kharakternyk in a very long time.”

Mykolai fell silent, giving everyone time to absorb his words.

With a brief movement of his hand, Hetman Sukhum added another enormous region to the hologram. Dozens of branches and offshoots extended from the Plan marked as his own. His map was almost an order of magnitude larger than the one Grandfather Mykola had presented, with different Plans shimmering in various colours and symbols.

“Our scouts had far greater opportunities and explored the entire accessible sphere around our world,” Sukhum said calmly. “And I can confirm that all the outermost worlds on this map really are outermost. Your world was never here. Until now. But I believe I understand the reason.”

Another sphere of worlds appeared on the map. It was considerably smaller than the previous two, but its central Plan had a direct connection to Sukhum’s central Plan.

“Not long ago, our scouts discovered this route. It had not existed before either—everything happened almost exactly as it did in your case. The central Plan of this sphere is currently under attack by another civilisation: the horned warriors. They have already destroyed the civilisation of the previous Plan, and their intentions leave no room for doubt. The central world of this sphere is doomed.”

“But they have you now, and the story may have a completely different ending, isn’t that right?” Grandfather Mykola finished for him.

Sukhum silently nodded.

“I do not know who governs the universe—God, some unknown laws, or mechanisms we do not yet understand,” Mykolai said thoughtfully. “But I know one thing for certain: new connections between worlds do not emerge by chance. And now…” He paused, searching for the right words. “Let us simply call it the universe. Well, it seems that the universe has deliberately brought our two… and now, probably, three civilisations together.”

“And all three are on the brink of destruction,” Sukhum added gravely.

A heavy silence settled over the room. Those present looked silently at the holographic map, trying to piece together all the information they had received.

Just then, new markers began appearing on the projection. The central Plans were labelled Earth-0, Earth-1, and Earth-2, while the other worlds gradually acquired administrative designations according to Sukhum’s civilisation’s classification system.

“I hope no one will object to organising our new knowledge a little,” the blue-skinned Hetman said calmly. “Let us call our Plan Earth-0. The Hetman Mykolai’s Plan, new to us, will be Earth-1. And the Plan currently under attack by the aliens, which has no Kharakternyks of its own, will be Earth-2.”

“I’ll add some information,” Hetman Mykolai interjected. A new connection immediately appeared between the first and second Plans, forming a triangle between the central worlds.

Silence continued in the hall. Everyone watched, fascinated, as the new map gradually took its final shape and filled with more and more details.

“I have nothing else to add,” Grandfather Mykola finally said, breaking the silence.

“I can only agree that none of this is accidental,” Sukhum concluded. “I suggest we take a break and carefully observe how events develop. I doubt any of us is ready to say with confidence what exactly the universe wants from this union.”

“This union,” Mykolai agreed. “It seems we simply have no other choice. Though I, too, still do not understand the whole picture.”

The meeting ended with a strange sense of incompleteness. Everyone understood that the information they had received was not yet enough. It was as if the universe had just spoken its first word, but had not even finished its sentence.

Grandfather Mykola and Dmytro opened a portal back to the First Plan. Another piece of good news was waiting for them there: the awakening of Kharakternyks continued, and fifteen more people with abilities had appeared in the world during that time.

DAY 7 (40)
THE FRONT

The grey zone did not look like a line on a map. It was blurred and uneven, made up of patches of fields, shelterbelts, broken roads, and isolated houses that had long since ceased to resemble homes. There were no clearly defined positions in the usual sense, only a presence—spread out, concealed, constantly shifting. Every metre of this territory was controlled not so much by people as by the gaze from above.

The commander was farthest from the front line, in the basement of a half-destroyed house where people had once lived. The ceiling rested on makeshift supports, dampness gathered in the corners, and the air was heavy and cold. The place was not safe, but it offered at least the illusion of shelter from what was happening above ground. From there, he maintained contact with fighters scattered across the entire sector and tried to piece together fragments of information arriving from different locations.

The fighters themselves had no trenches in the traditional sense. Some had dug shallow holes beneath tree roots in a shelterbelt; others occupied remnants of trenches left over from earlier battles; still others hid in shell craters, covering them with branches and scraps of fabric. Any shelter here was temporary and conditional. Its main purpose was not to protect someone from a direct hit, but to make them less noticeable.

The end of October was felt not through the calendar, but through the body. The ground had already lost its warmth, dampness lingered even during the day, and at night the cold seeped through clothing and settled in the bones. The air was heavy, often shrouded in low clouds, sometimes with a fine drizzle that did not seem serious but gradually soaked everything. In such conditions, every movement left a trace, and every mistake cost more than it seemed.

Moving openly during the day was almost impossible. The sky was constantly occupied—not by aircraft, but by smaller, quieter things. Drones hovered, changed positions, returned, and appeared again. They were not always visible, but their presence was constant, like a pressure that never lifted. They searched for movement, heat, any sign of life.

At night, the situation did not become easier. Darkness no longer offered an advantage, because thermal imaging came with it. What could be hidden in the daytime by a shadow became visible at night through temperature differences. Even breathing, even the warmth left in the ground, could give away a position.

There was not enough food. Supplies arrived irregularly and were sometimes simply impossible to deliver. What the fighters brought with them had to last for days, sometimes weeks. Ammunition was counted not by the box, but one round at a time. Every shot mattered, and yet most of the time there was no one to shoot at—the enemy was just as reluctant to reveal itself.

Most often, they saw not people but surveillance equipment. And that changed the very logic of war. Instead of battle, there was waiting. Instead of manoeuvre, stillness. Instead of controlling territory, the struggle was to remain unseen within it.

From time to time, the enemy shelled open areas. Without a clear reference point, without confirmed targets. Simply because they could. The shells landed irregularly, sometimes far from any positions, sometimes dangerously close. It was not precision work; it was pressure that allowed no one to relax for even a minute.

The hardest things were not the shelling or even the lack of resources. The hardest were the small things that broke the familiar logic of behaviour.

Dogs appeared unexpectedly. They came from destroyed villages, hungry and confused, drawn to people because warmth and the smell of food could still be found there. In ordinary life, this would have been an everyday occurrence. Here, it became a threat. A drone did not need sophisticated analysis; it was enough to notice an animal persistently running towards one point and staying there.

Once, a dog ran straight up to a shelter. It did not bark or fuss. It simply tried to come closer and lie down beside the fighters. The decision was made quickly, but that did not make it any easier.

The shot rang out quietly, almost muffled in that space.

Afterwards, no one spoke for several minutes. Not because there were no words, but because everyone understood what had happened and why. And they knew that similar decisions might have to be made again.

This war left no room for easy answers. It changed habits and reactions, even attitudes towards things that had once seemed obvious.

And despite everything, the fighters remained at their positions. Not because it was comfortable or right in the usual sense, but because there was no other option. They held this sector not as a defensive line, but as a space that could not be surrendered.

And as long as they remained there, the grey zone remained grey—not fully under control, but not lost either.

DMYTRO — THE DARK ONES AND NAIRIN

The next day, there were already more than forty newcomers. They had to be collected from two locations because some of them had too far to walk. Dmytro took this as a good sign: the Kharakternyks of his home Plan were growing stronger with every passing day.

Following the established tradition, everyone headed to Grandfather Mykola’s. The old man was already standing in his usual place by the porch, greeting the newcomers without the slightest surprise. He kept his hands behind his back, squinting slightly in the cold morning light. His gaze moved over the people so attentively that it seemed he was not merely seeing their faces, but sensing the state of everyone who had come.

“The people of the Zero Plan already know our numbers have grown,” he replied to Dmytro’s unspoken question. “There will be two programmes: a tour for those who knew nothing about the thought-link until yesterday, and a training session for those who took the tour the day before.”

Dmytro simply nodded. He was no longer surprised by how quickly Grandfather received information, how naturally he communicated with Kharakternyks from another Plan, or how easily he navigated things that Dmytro himself had not even suspected existed just a week earlier.

Sometimes he caught himself feeling that the old man standing beside him was no longer the village grandfather he had known, but someone far greater than any familiar notion of authority or experience. Not just a Kharakternyk. The Hetman of an entire world.

“Grandpa, yesterday at the gathering I initially sensed some tension from the people of the Zero Plan. It only disappeared towards the end. Did you notice it too?” Dmytro finally asked.

Grandfather Mykola did not answer immediately. He watched several newcomers entering the courtyard, waited until they had moved farther away, and only then spoke quietly:

“Hetman Sukhum and I exchanged information packages at the end. There was something in them that made them distrust us. I’ll send it to you now as a thought package.”

The world around Dmytro seemed to recede. He had received other people’s memories through thought packages before, but this time the experience went much deeper. His consciousness did not simply see someone else’s images—it seemed to slip inside another person, to look through their eyes, hear through their senses, and perceive space as someone else did.

Now he was Nairin.

A wide station corridor stretched far ahead, illuminated by soft white panels in the ceiling. The metal walls vibrated almost imperceptibly with the operation of the reactors. The air carried the familiar scent of station life: a mixture of ozone, purified air, hot metal, and something technical, almost impossible to identify but long since familiar.

Nairin walked with a confident stride, occasionally touching the bracelet on his wrist with his fingers out of habit. For the first time in a long while, he was genuinely in a good mood.

People passed him. Some hurried towards the technical compartments, others emerged from transport platforms. A few children ran down the corridor, loudly arguing about something of their own. Laughter could be heard nearby. The station was carrying on with its peaceful everyday life, and Nairin suddenly realised how long it had been since he had felt such simple human calm within himself.

Yesterday’s meeting with the Kharakternyks from the new world had changed a great deal. In the Zero Plan, they had long been accustomed to Kharakternyks appearing in much the same way: lost, exhausted, frightened, sometimes half-mad after years of wandering between foreign Plans.

Almost every one of them had followed the same path: the sudden awakening of their gift, another world, fear of their own abilities, and attempts to understand why the world had suddenly begun breaking apart around them, why strange passages appeared, and why reality sometimes behaved in ways it should not.

Then came years of survival. In their search for their home world, people learned to open portals through endless trial and error. Some became stranded in foreign worlds forever. Others died before they could even begin to understand the nature of their gift. Those fortunate enough to reach the Zero Plan usually brought with them not only knowledge, but scars—both physical and psychological.

But the new Kharakternyks were different. They were not surviving by trial and error.

They were being taught.

---

They were taught the nature of their abilities. They trained on special grounds, worked with instructors, and studied the thought-link as an ordinary discipline. There was a system in their movements, in the way they spoke, even in the questions they asked. This impressed Nairin more than any technology.

As he walked down the corridor, he caught himself thinking that this was the first time he had seen a Kharakternyk civilisation that had not been born out of catastrophe, but had developed gradually and in an orderly way, without decades of chaos and struggle for survival.

That was exactly why he had agreed to personally accompany the guests today. And that was exactly why the anxiety slowly growing inside him irritated him more and more.

Nairin was the only Kharakternyk of the Zero Plan who could fully read another person’s thoughtphone. He could do more than catch emotions or separate images, as others could. He could go deeper—see the connections between thoughts, hidden reactions, inner contradictions, and true motives.

He could do more than communicate through the thought-link: he could see the other person’s pattern.

This ability almost never brought him joy. People lied constantly. Often even to themselves. Sometimes Nairin felt that after so many years he had forgotten how to take words seriously, because he was used to judging not what people said, but what they were trying to hide.

His younger brother Zairin was already waiting for him near the airlock sector. He stood by the panoramic window, looking at the snow-covered ruins beyond the station. Unlike Nairin, he had retained a lightness of character, even though he had become an ataman of the standard-bearers while his older brother had reached a rank one level lower.

When he saw his brother, he smiled.

“You look as if you’re going to interrogate someone, not welcome guests.”

“Maybe those are the same thing,” Nairin replied dryly.

Zairin chuckled.

“You’re too suspicious.”

“And you start trusting people too quickly.”

“Yesterday went fine.”

Nairin fell silent for a few seconds, watching the snow slowly drift among the ruins of the old city outside.

“That’s exactly what worries me,” he finally said.

Zairin frowned.

“Explain.”

“People who see another Kharakternyk civilisation for the first time behave differently. They get lost. They start asking hundreds of stupid questions. They either become frightened or amazed. But these people… they’re far too calm.”

“Maybe they’re simply better prepared.”

“Maybe.” Nairin shrugged, though he himself hardly believed that explanation anymore.

They met the guests near the observation sector. Beyond the thick armoured glass lay the nuclear wasteland of the Zero Plan: endless snowfields, black fragments of former cities, and a low grey sky that almost always hung over the ground like a heavy, cold ceiling.

The two Kharakternyks greeted them warmly. One was tall and dark-haired, with the attentive gaze of someone accustomed to assessing everything constantly. The other was stocky and looked older, with a short scar near his lip.

They smiled, joked, and listened attentively as Zairin told them about life on the stations, but Nairin’s inner discomfort grew stronger and stronger.

Something did not add up.

The problem was not even their questions, although those were gradually beginning to seem strange too. The guests were far too interested in technical details: backup reactors, the number of Kharakternyks, security systems, and how quickly sectors could be evacuated.

But the main issue was something else. They showed no genuine surprise.

When someone discovers a new world, it is impossible to hide the reaction. Even the most experienced Kharakternyk responds constantly to little things—with a glance, a change in intonation, a brief emotional surge. These two, however, behaved as if they had known exactly what they would see.

Nairin began cautiously touching their thoughtphones. Superficially. Very carefully, so as not to leave a noticeable trace. And almost immediately he realised he had been right.

Beneath their outward calm lay rigid discipline. Their thoughts were orderly, stripped of spontaneous reactions. That was not how researchers or diplomats behaved. That was how people behaved when they had come on a mission.

When they returned to the observation dome, Nairin finally stopped. Zairin immediately sensed the change in his mood and tensed. Snow drifted slowly over the ruins beyond the windows, while a strange silence settled over the sector.

“I have a question,” Nairin said calmly.

The guests turned towards him.

“You know your way around here far too well for people who have arrived in the Zero Plan for the first time.”

The dark-haired Kharakternyk smiled, but this time the smile was unnaturally brief.

“We’re simply observant.”

“No,” Nairin replied quietly. “You don’t behave like people who have discovered a new world. You behave like people who have known about its existence for a long time.”

For a moment, everyone froze. And at that very instant, Nairin understood the truth once and for all. Not because of their words, but because of the fear that suddenly flared in the pattern of the strangers’ thoughtphones.

Not fear of the unknown, but rather fear of being exposed.

“Who sent you?” he asked, his voice now completely different.

Zairin instantly understood that something was wrong. Through Nairin’s memory, Dmytro felt it as clearly as if he himself had been standing there: the sudden tension in the muscles, the change in breathing, the brief movement of a hand towards the sabre.

But the guests were faster. The stocky Kharakternyk opened a portal almost instantly—so quickly that the air around it merely shimmered darkly for a moment. The dark-haired one was already moving forward.

No hesitation. No words. Only a cold, practised action.

Zairin did not even have time to draw his sabre fully. The blade split his head so quickly that for the first few seconds he remained standing, staring at his brother with wide-open eyes, as if unable to understand what had just happened.

Blood surged across the floor in a bright, dark wave. Nairin lunged forward with such fury that the very space around him seemed to shudder from the force of his movement, but the killers were already retreating through the portal. And at that moment, on pure instinct, before his mind had time to comprehend his brother’s death, Nairin struck the dark-haired man’s thoughtphone with his gift.

Deeply. Brutally. Ripping out a piece of the stranger’s memory along with sensations, images, and fragments of thought.

The portal closed.

The alarm was already sounding all around them. Guards were running somewhere in the corridors. People were shouting. The station was coming alive with danger signals. But Nairin heard almost nothing. He knelt beside his brother’s body, staring down without moving. There was blood on his hands. Something inside him had changed irrevocably at that very moment.

And when he later reported Zairin’s death to Hetman Sukhum, the latter noticed it immediately. Nairin no longer looked like a man who had just lost his brother. He looked like a man who had found his enemy.

DMYTRO — SABRES AND THE STANDARD-BEARER

This time, Grandfather Mykola opened the portal. Dmytro was still under the influence of the information he had received and behaved somewhat distractedly and inattentively. The locals were already waiting for them there.

Raien gathered the newcomers who had not yet seen the station, briefly explained the plans for the day, and sent them an information package about the civilisation of Earth-0, prepared especially for new arrivals. Once they had absorbed the information, he led his group on a tour of the station.

Nairin took those who had arrived for a second time to the training hall Dmytro already knew. And indeed, the same instructor, Iris, who had worked with Dmytro last time, was waiting for them. But this time he was not alone: about a dozen instructors were in the hall. They were warming up, talking among themselves, and carefully studying the newcomers they would be training that day.

After the briefing on clothing, equipment, and the day’s programme, the students headed to the changing rooms, while Iris approached Dmytro. He had already changed and warmed up while the others listened to the explanations. He was eager to practise duelling. Enthusiastically, he spun his sabres, recalling the movements he had learned a week earlier.

“We have enough instructors to train all of you, but something tells me that very soon it won’t be enough. Tomorrow, this group is already scheduled to train in opening portals. First within a single Plan, and later between Plans. Usually this takes months, sometimes years, but you are already following an accelerated programme. It seems we’ll have to prepare the newcomers just as quickly.”

Dmytro nodded gratefully and was about to suggest another sparring session with Iris when the instructor suddenly gestured for him to come to the centre of the hall. Following him without a second thought, Dmytro saw Grandfather Mykola slowly emerge from a side passage. He was no longer wearing his usual everyday clothes. The old Kharakternyk was dressed in light combat gear and carried six real sabres in his hands. Cold light slid along their wavy blades, and the mere sight of the weapons made Dmytro tense involuntarily.

“Grandpa…” Dmytro smiled nervously. “I’ve never fought with real sabres before.”

“Then it’s time you started,” Mykola replied calmly.

Nairin silently handed Dmytro two sabres, while Iris stepped back beyond the training area, making it clear that he was only an observer.

The difference was immediate. The training weapons had seemed light and safe, but these blades carried a sense of concealed danger even at rest. Dmytro involuntarily tightened his grip.

“Ready?” Nairin asked.

“Honestly? Not really.”

Nairin gave a faint smile.

“Good. That means everything is as it should be.”

And the very next moment, Grandfather Mykola attacked. The blow came so quickly that Dmytro barely managed to dodge. The sabre whistled right in front of his face, and the sound of it cutting through the air made him flinch instinctively. Before he could fully process the first movement, a second strike was already coming from the side.

Metal crashed against metal. The force of the impact travelled up his arms so violently that his fingers almost let go. Dmytro immediately understood one thing: no one intended to go easy on him.

Grandfather Mykola fought the way people fought in a real battle. No graceful flourishes, no display of skill for the sake of spectacle. His strikes were heavy, brutal, and frighteningly precise. Every movement bore the weight of decades of real battles and the experience of a man who had seen death at close quarters far too many times.

Then Nairin joined the fight. And everything immediately became much more difficult. If Grandfather Mykola was like a force that simply crushed you with pressure, Nairin was a predator. He moved lightly, almost imperceptibly, constantly changing the rhythm of his attacks. Dmytro had no time to get used to one tempo before Nairin made him falter with a new movement.

Very quickly, Dmytro stopped thinking about how he looked from the outside. He stopped thinking about possible spectators. He stopped controlling his breathing. At some point, everything narrowed down to the gleam of steel, the heavy crash of blows, and the constant sense of danger.

They were driving him back relentlessly. Every step backwards opened him to another attack. Every block drained his strength. Dmytro felt his arms beginning to go numb from the blows and his shoulders gradually filling with fatigue. Sweat had long since been running down his back, even though the hall was cool.

And that was when he began to notice something strange. His mind could no longer analyse the fight quickly enough. His body began to react first. He had not yet seen the strike, but already sensed where it would come from. Nairin’s sabre had only just begun to change its trajectory, and Dmytro was already instinctively shifting aside. Grandfather Mykola was still completing his previous attack, while Dmytro already knew where the next blade would be.

It was frightening. And incredibly exciting at the same time.

Gradually, almost the entire hall gathered around them. The newcomers watched wide-eyed, making no attempt to hide their amazement. The experienced Kharakternyks observed much more intently.

Then everything around them suddenly shuddered. One of the instructors activated the combat arena.

The floor rumbled heavily, and metal structures began rising right between the fighters. Narrow platforms, vertical beams, and unstable walkways grew out of the floor, while moving obstacles descended from the ceiling. Within seconds, an ordinary arena had turned into a complex, multi-level labyrinth. Its layout was generated randomly each time, so the fighters never knew where any obstacle would appear.

One of the newcomers let out a nervous breath. Another moved closer. Even the instructors were now watching the fight with noticeably greater interest.

Dmytro had no time to look around properly before Nairin had already leapt onto one of the platforms and attacked from above. The blow was so powerful that Dmytro barely kept hold of his sabres. Grandfather Mykola came at him from the other side at the same moment.

Now the fight had become truly dangerous. The platforms swayed beneath their feet, beams blocked their view, and walkways shifted while they were moving. Dmytro had to do more than fight: he also had to keep constant track of the space around him. Several times, he only just managed to keep his balance after powerful blows.

But along with the fatigue, something else was coming.

Excitement.

Anger.

The desire to finally stop merely surviving.

At one point, Nairin shouted sharply:

“Stop defending yourself! Fight like a real warrior, not like a mummy’s boy!”

And it was those words that changed something inside Dmytro. Fear seemed to step aside, and the world around him became sharper and clearer. He lunged forward so quickly that even Nairin had not expected it. His strike forced the standard-bearer to retreat properly for the first time in the entire fight. Grandfather Mykola took the next attack.

Then everything exploded into motion.

The three Kharakternyks raced around the arena so quickly that the newcomers could no longer follow the duel. Metal beams cracked under the blows, fragments flew down, sparks scattered in every direction, and the clash of sabres rolled through the entire hall like a heavy, continuous thunder.

From the outside, it no longer looked like training. It looked like a real battle.

When the last obstacle crashed to the floor, the fight gradually began to subside. The three warriors froze amid the half-destroyed arena. Dmytro was breathing heavily, his hands literally trembling from the strain, but a strange emotional exhilaration was spreading inside him. It was as if he had just crossed some kind of threshold.

Nairin was the first to lower his sabres.

“This kind of three-way duel has long been a tradition among Kharakternyks, at least in the Zero Plan,” he said, now much more calmly. “If a warrior can withstand two experienced comrades and not break under the pressure, it means he has reached a new level of skill.”

Silence fell over the hall.

Grandfather Mykola approached Dmytro and looked at him attentively. There was no solemnity or pretentious grandeur in his gaze. Only the quiet satisfaction of someone who had just become completely certain of his decision.

“By the way, Nairin,” he said casually, “where can we find proper armour for our new standard-bearer, worthy of his new sabres?”

Nairin smiled faintly. Only he understood what had just happened. Dmytro did not react at first. Only a few seconds later did the meaning of the words reach him, and a wave of strange excitement rose inside him once again.

And at that very moment, he suddenly felt the world around him grow deeper. He was already accustomed to the thought-link of the Kharakternyks, to fragments of other people’s emotions or intentions, but now everything was different. He began to sense the society of the First Plan itself—all those who were training here now or had been on the tour.

---

He looked at Hetman Mykola with greater understanding now. His newly acquired rank of standard-bearer offered many opportunities, but also brought even greater responsibility.

Day 8 (100)
- Mars. Mission: A New Home

Preparations began before the base had reached its fully stable operating mode. Mars allowed no room for error, and every new stage of rebuilding the base felt like another test of survival. The life-support systems were running steadily, the power supply remained within acceptable limits, and the dust filters were doing their job—but they broke down far too often. That was a troubling first warning. If Mars remained the only option, people would have to live here, in an environment inherently hostile to life.

All the members of the expedition gathered in one of the technical compartments, where it was quieter than in the residential sections. The metal walls muffled the sounds, while the subdued lighting created a sense of isolation from everything else. They said little. Everyone understood that this was not merely a test of whether they could jump between the Red Planet's other Plans. It was a test of whether humanity could get a second chance in the middle of this wilderness.

The preparations required no complicated adjustments or equipment checks, because the mechanism of transition was not technological. Everything depended solely on the Kharakternyks—their inner sense of space, which they had learned to use, and their ability to interfere with it in ways other people could not even imagine. The base was already operating steadily, and people were beginning to adapt to the new conditions. But both Kharakternyks understood perfectly well that this was only temporary support.

"I'll start," one of the Kharakternyks said, more to mark the moment than to ask permission.

The other merely nodded and moved slightly aside, giving him room. His role was simple and yet difficult: to maintain the connection and be ready to intervene if anything went wrong.

The first Kharakternyk concentrated. It was not like a physical action, but rather an inner movement, as his attention narrowed to a single point and everything irrelevant fell away. He sensed space as he had before—not as emptiness, but as something with structure, depth, and direction.

But this time, something was different.

He tried to do what he had done dozens of times before: find another layer, another version of the same reality. Instead, he encountered something impenetrable, as though nothing existed where the transition should have been.

He opened his eyes and slowly exhaled.

"It isn't working," he said calmly, though tension had already crept into his voice.

"What exactly?" the other asked at once.

"It's as if there's nowhere to open a passage."

He concentrated again, this time more deeply and carefully, trying to get around what felt like an obstacle. His perception of space shifted slightly, but the result remained the same. There was no response, not even the slightest hint of one.

"Maybe try once more," his partner said quietly.

He did not answer. He simply tried again. Then again. And again.

Every attempt ended the same way—as if he were reaching out to a place where a passage had once opened, only to find nothing but silence. Not physical silence, but something deeper, something that could not be overcome by effort.

"This is wrong," he said more sharply, stepping back. "This shouldn't happen."

The second man remained silent, but his expression showed that he felt the same. This was not a matter of strength or experience. It was something else.

"You try," the first finally said.

Now the second Kharakternyk took his place. He did not hurry. He tried to sense space in his own way and find the same point, but the result was identical. It was as though Mars refused to open beyond itself.

He lowered his hand and closed his eyes for a moment.

"There's nothing," he said quietly.

The air in the room seemed harder to breathe, although nothing had physically changed. Both men felt they had run into something they did not understand.

"Could Mars have no other Plans? Is that possible?" the second began, but did not finish.

The first slowly shook his head, looking down at the floor.

"We don't know that for certain," he replied. "But even if they exist, we clearly can't reach them."

The silence lingered, bringing with it a wave of hopelessness. If humanity moved to Mars, the Kharakternyks would lose their ability to travel between Plans. They would no longer be able to find essential things on other planets as they had before—technology, resources, or food. Humanity would be left alone with the Red Planet. And there was no guarantee that this planet would let them survive.

- Dmytro. Devastated Lands (Grandfather Mykola–Isaac)

There were even more newcomers that day. Last time there had been a little over forty; now there were nearly a hundred, and they had to be collected from three different locations. Dmytro was a little tired from tracking down exactly where he needed to jump, while Grandfather Mykola—or, more correctly now, Hetman Mykola—could barely fit everyone into his courtyard. At least the newcomers had time to meet in person and talk, both aloud and through the thought-link.

The portal to Z-0 opened without any problems, and the Hetman had already warned the receiving side that the number of newly initiated trainees would be increasing again. The newcomers were quickly divided into separate groups and led away: some went on a tour, others to their first sword training session, while the first Kharakternyks, together with Dmytro and the Hetman, were invited into the portal-training hall.

While the newcomers were receiving their initial briefing, Dmytro warmly greeted Isaac and noticed a new three-dimensional image covering an entire wall. It was a map of the known universe. It was interactive, allowing them to examine numerous details. It included both the part of the universe explored by the Kharakternyks of this Plan and the part contributed by the Hetman of Dmytro's Plan.

"Would you like me to take you on a tour of the dead Plans?" Isaac asked. "We prepared it for other purposes, but it's not being used at the moment, so we might as well take advantage of it."

"Is it dangerous?"

"We've developed something that turns it into an ordinary walk. By the way, ask whether the Hetman would like to join us. I'll get the equipment ready."

Of course, Grandfather was interested. He decided to leave the newcomers with the experienced instructors and take the opportunity to travel between the Plans. So half an hour later, just as the newcomers were beginning to open their first practice portals under the instructors' supervision, the three of them stepped through another opening in the portal Isaac had prepared for them.

They found themselves inside a glass dome, completely sealed and clearly protected by every means humanity had at its disposal. Isaac confirmed this and began to explain:

"This is Plan Z0-15-387-M05-D4, Earth on branch number fifteen from Z-0. I won't explain the rest of the code—that's all in the standard knowledge package—but I'll give you a brief explanation. The fifteenth branch of the Zero World, the three hundred and eighty-seventh planet in the registry. Catastrophe class M05: biological weapon. Danger level D5. Access prohibited."

"When we understand more about the catastrophe, clarifications will be added to the code, but the first three groups of digits will remain unchanged."

As he spoke, Isaac walked over to the window and looked outside. The view was truly beautiful. The dome stood on high ground. Below it stretched a broad field covered in thousands of varieties of grass, seemingly bursting with flowers and millions of insects. In the distance, a dense forest extended to the foot of a mountain that rose steeply, forming the beginning of a mountain range. On the opposite side, a lake shimmered, with another forest visible beyond it. And between all this beauty of forests and mountains lay an endless field.

Not far away, the ruins of a city could be made out: low buildings, no more than five storeys high, so badly ruined and overgrown that they could easily have gone unnoticed without a closer look. Yet they were clear proof that civilization on the planet was gone.

Isaac walked to the other side of the dome, looked at something, and gave a command. A powerful horn sounded over the field, much like the one used on trains in Dmytro's home Plan. For a few moments, nothing happened. Then human figures began rising from the tall grass and moving uncertainly, but purposefully, towards the glass separating the three Kharakternyks from the surrounding world.

"Are those zombies?" Dmytro asked in disbelief, seeing the dead eyes of the approaching creatures. "I thought that was just something from movies."

"Unfortunately, it's another entirely possible reality for your planet," Isaac replied gravely. "I'm certain this kind of bacteriological weapon already exists and is ready to become the final nail in civilization's coffin."

They watched the creatures in silence. No one would have dared call them human—not until they came right up to the dome and began banging against it, trying to reach the living.

Isaac broke the silence again.

"Leaving the dome is forbidden. Everyone who has gone outside has become infected. This disease does not cause immediate death, but when a person dies, it raises the body and makes it search for the living in order to kill them. A vicious cycle that wiped out everything here."

"Are there no survivors at all?"

"There are a few settlements. They're somehow trying to survive, and we even help them a little, but there's nothing more we can do. One of our brothers lives there. We've already diagnosed him with the infection, so he has no way off this planet."

"Terrible," Dmytro concluded.

Grandfather merely nodded.

It was impossible to keep looking at those creatures, and Isaac suggested moving on. But he warned them at once that they would not get a break from the catastrophes until the very end of the journey.

The next dome hung in space. The Sun shone far away, while beneath their feet stretched an enormous blue-and-white sphere: planet Earth. Dmytro stared in astonishment. The entire planet was completely flooded. Not a single patch of land remained. No matter how hard he looked, he could not find even the slightest trace of a continent or an island.

Isaac checked the readings on the panel and explained:

"This is planet Z0-21-044-X02-D5. The twenty-first branch of the Zero World, the forty-fourth planet in the registry. Class X02: aggressive acidic environment. Danger level D5—access prohibited."

Dmytro stepped right up to the observation window and stared at the planet in amazement.

"That's not water," Isaac explained calmly, seeing the unspoken question on his face. "It's an extremely toxic acid. It has eaten away everything it could reach, and now it's corroding the lithospheric plates. When it reaches the mantle, magma bursts out, solidifies, and forms new rock. Then the cycle repeats. There is no atmosphere. No life. The planet is completely lost. Landing to collect samples for scientists is impossible—the acid begins to act from a distance of about one kilometre."

There was not much more to see, so the travellers moved on.

The next planet, with the dome once again positioned in orbit, had been torn apart by eruptions of magma. There was not a single stable patch of surface. Every minute, a gigantic volcanic explosion erupted somewhere, so powerful that it could be seen even from space.

Isaac offered a brief explanation:

"World Z0-09-112-M08-D5. The ninth branch. Catastrophe M08. All the water has evaporated, and the lithosphere is still unstable," Isaac explained. "The result of seismic weapons. There's hope that in a few thousand years the surface will become more stable and perhaps someone will try to settle it again. Although I doubt anything living could have survived such a turmoil."

"Is this the future of your planet?" Grandfather Mykola asked.

"Our planet was struck by tectonic weapons, not seismic ones. We could have survived a catastrophe like this on orbital stations," the Kharakternyk replied, a little tensely.

Grandfather and Dmytro made no further comments. They simply watched in silence, witnessing the consequences of human stupidity and shortsightedness, until Isaac invited them to move on.

The next Plan showed the same field the travellers had seen on the first world, the one with the zombies. Once again, everything looked calm and safe.

Isaac added briefly:

"Planet Z0-11-072-M05-D4. The eleventh branch. The result of a modified biological weapon."

Isaac activated the horn, and at that very moment the dome shuddered from a dull impact below. Then gigantic worms appeared along every wall, as if bursting straight out of the depths of the earth. They rose so quickly, as though they had been hunting for prey for days and now only one lunge separated them from the meat they wanted.

They hurled themselves wildly against the dome's transparent shell, trying to reach those inside. Their broad jaws snapped like the lids of metal containers. A single creature could swallow a person whole.

The attack ended as abruptly as it had begun. The worms disappeared underground again, and silence spread over the dome.

"This is biological weaponry too," Isaac said. "But far more insidious. There are more than twenty species of these monsters here. We haven't found any remnants of humanity. If anyone survived, they've hidden so deep that finding them is practically impossible—for us and for the worms."

Dmytro shuddered again. He remembered the predatory maw rising only a few metres away and followed Isaac onward with relief.

The next dome was once again in space, but this time in a much more distant orbit. What they saw was so unreal that it looked like a scene from a movie.

The Moon was… embedded in the Earth.

Isaac spoke quietly:

"This is Z0-03-201-M09-D5. The third branch of the Zero World. Catastrophe M09—a planetary collision."

The planet and its satellite had fused in a dead, convulsive embrace. The catastrophe that had occurred here was not yet over. Isolated flashes glimmered on the planet's surface, as if explosions were still taking place beneath the layers of debris or the remains of cities were still burning. The place where the giants had collided seemed to breathe slowly, contracting and expanding under the internal pressure of magma and fractured rock.

Huge fragments revolved around this chaotic mass in unstable orbits: pieces of the Moon, parts of the Earth, entire continental plates.

"Tell me, Hetman… how did you even survive travelling between worlds if the catastrophes everywhere are this devastating?" Dmytro asked quietly.

"Because I travelled to only about fifty worlds, and I knew they were safe," Grandfather replied.

"Time to move on," Isaac interrupted.

And they headed for the next portal.

The planet was filthy. No grass, no trees—only ground covered in a foul, viscous sludge. A fine rain fell continuously from leaden clouds.

Isaac checked the readings.

"Planet Z0-18-066-M07-D4. Catastrophe M07. This planet is completely contaminated with toxins," he said quietly. "Life here is impossible."

The next planet greeted them in sharp contrast: bright, welcoming, and radiant.

"This is Z0-08-199-M06-D5," Isaac said. "Catastrophe M06—uncontrolled nanobots. Notice? Everything seems to be covered with blue dust."

He released a drone, and a second later it was gone.

"The nanobots maintain everything they consider 'their own'," Isaac said.

"Have you tried figuring out their code?" Dmytro asked, immediately switching into professional mode. He remembered long video meetings where they had deciphered someone else's code in order to create something better of their own.

"We tried, but not for long. There are many planets, and very few people. Each one requires a full-scale expedition. I'll be honest: a civilization with a population of six hundred million cannot conduct detailed research into every field, even the most interesting ones. If there are no more questions, let's move on."

The next planet greeted them with the hum of air conditioners and unbearable heat beyond the dome. The ground was cracked, as if it had turned into an overbaked crust.

Isaac quickly checked the readings.

---

"This is planet Z0-27-054-M03-D4," Isaac explained. "The twenty-seventh branch of the Zero World, the fifty-fourth planet in the registry. Catastrophe M03—a climate collapse. First the ozone layer burned away, then something happened to the magnetic poles. The atmosphere is literally burning up. The oceans disappeared long ago. Humanity may have survived somewhere underground, but finding them now is almost impossible. There's no point trying radio communications—the solar storms are constant. There is no future here. But the next planet… it will amaze you. Shall we go?"

When Isaac opened the portal, for the first time they did not step through immediately. Their guide stopped them with a raised hand and carefully checked the readings. Only after making sure everything was safe did he gesture for them to follow. He entered the dome of the new Plan first.

The dome was standard, but the view beyond it was anything but. A familiar flower-filled field bordered a landscape that looked unmistakably lunar. Tornadoes swept across the whole scene, vanishing without a trace and then appearing out of nowhere.

Isaac calmly explained:

"This is world Z0-31-004-X10-D5. The thirty-first branch of the Zero World, the fourth planet in the registry. Class X10—overlapping Plans, when two different worlds begin to flow into one another."

Dmytro realized in amazement that the lunar surface was not simply beside the field. It appeared and displaced the field, the forest, and the lake… then vanished, only to emerge somewhere else. Everything was divided into patches and fragments that moved, disappeared, and overlapped.

Isaac gave them time to take in the surreal sight, then spoke:

"This is the most extraordinary world I have ever seen. As a result of some incredible experiments, two Plans converged here. They flow into one another, constantly changing. People had no chance of surviving: one of these Plans is sterile, without an atmosphere—dead."

He pointed towards the horizon.

"To stay alive, a person would have had to keep moving, remaining in the area where their own world happened to dominate at that moment. Otherwise, death. And that's quite a gamble."

He paused briefly.

"Some tried flying in sealed craft… but they didn't last long. We found a lot of wreckage."

They stood by the dome's transparent wall a little longer, studying the anomaly. At last Grandfather nodded, and Isaac opened the next portal.

The next planet was nothing but rock, with an occasional half-dried blade of grass poking through. In places, the surface was cracked like a baked crust, while windblown dust swirled through the air, creating an almost foggy haze.

Isaac glanced at the panel.

"This is Z0-05-017-M02-D2. The fifth branch of the Zero World, the seventeenth planet. Catastrophe M02—complete resource depletion."

Without fear, he opened the dome's sliding doors.

"We can go outside here."

They stepped onto the hot, rocky ground. The air smelled of dust and heat.

"They simply used up all the resources here," Isaac explained as he descended an improvised ladder down the rock. "There was no catastrophe. Humanity slowly faded away and nearly died out."

They followed a narrow path downhill. After the last bend, a small settlement came into view—perhaps ten residential blocks. The buildings were dusty and somewhat untidy, but that was a problem affecting the entire planet, not just this settlement. An old man sat beside one of the blocks, smoking a pipe.

Isaac greeted him like an old friend and introduced Grandfather and Dmytro. The old man's name was Joe. He looked like a character from an old western: tall boots, leather trousers, a wide-brimmed hat, and a scarf around his neck.

"Sit down," Joe said. "Water is worth its weight in gold here, but thanks to you, I always have something to offer my guests."

He placed several bottles and something resembling biscuits on the table. Dmytro looked around. The interior was simple, with a few wooden pieces of furniture, but it was clean.

"Was it always this lifeless here?" Grandfather asked.

Joe took a draw on his pipe.

"At first, things were all right. But gradually everything changed. There are no large cities anymore. The forests disappeared. The seas dried up."

He sighed.

"We didn't die suddenly, like on the other planets. We just withered away here."

He struck the table sharply with his fist.

"With no future."

Silence settled over the room. Isaac spoke quietly:

"Joe has lived here for almost two hundred years. We periodically renew him in a medical capsule."

"But I'm not going into that capsule again," Joe grumbled. "I want to die at last."

He looked out the window.

"Because even death is better than living in this hole."

Dmytro silently watched the old man, unable even to imagine what life must be like for someone surrounded by nothing but hopelessness. The conversation continued for a while. Then Isaac glanced at his watch.

"We need to head back."

They said goodbye to Joe and made their way back to the dome. On the way, Dmytro asked:

"Isaac, can you travel between worlds only one after another?"

Isaac nodded.

"Yes."

"But the planets we've visited are on different branches."

Isaac smiled.

"We used a feature of portals: stabilization. If a portal is used frequently, it can be fixed in place. Then any Kharakternyk can use it. We didn't travel through a single portal, but through several stabilized ones, arranged one after another at the shortest possible distance."

They returned to the training hall. Everything was as usual there: newcomers were practising how to open portals, instructors were watching over them, and some were already resting in the recreation area. Yet memories of the dead lands still raced through Dmytro's mind, making him shudder again and again.

Day 9
- Raien. Earthquake and Its Consequences

The earthquake warning had not come without notice, but the interval between the warning and the first tremors was so short that there was simply no way to evacuate the population from the dangerous sections of the city-path in advance.

People received instructions, evacuation routes were identified, and rescue services were deployed within minutes. Even so, it was not enough, because the earth itself decided to move faster than they could react.

The city-path stretched above the surface like an enormous flattened tube, raised on supports that seemed eternal. In this artificial world, built over decades, people lived, worked, and rested without considering that all this stability depended on a delicate balance. Everything inside had been planned down to the smallest detail: central rail lines carried streams of people and cargo; residential sections ran along the sides, where families were now hurriedly gathering their belongings; laboratories and medical centres were located higher up. Beneath the transparent domes, where the sun had still been shining yesterday, people were now rushing about, losing their sense of solid ground beneath their feet. Deep inside the structure, technical systems were still working to hold everything together, but they were beginning to lose the battle.

When it began, it did not look like an explosion or an instant catastrophe. It was something slower and more frightening: the structure of the enormous building itself began to lose its integrity. First came a slight trembling through the floor, then the sensation that something deeper than the surface was shifting. Only after that, with a piercing screech, did the first cracks appear. They did not stop; they spread and widened, splitting corridors and rooms apart.

The thought-link filled with voices and sensations all at once, but it was not uncontrolled chaos. People knew what to do. They tried to act correctly, supported one another, and passed on information. That was what kept them from sliding into panic. Even so, fear and confusion still slipped into the channel, along with brief flashes of panic: when someone realized they would not make it in time, when a child got lost in the crowd, when a corridor that had been a route to safety only a minute earlier was suddenly blocked by the deformation of the structure.

Raien did not allow himself to be swept away by this flow. His voice in the channel remained steady and clear, and others clung to it when the situation began to spiral out of control.

"Move towards the inner junctions. Keep going," he transmitted. These were not merely instructions; they were what it took to preserve order while the world was falling apart.

Rescue workers were active everywhere action was still possible. They guided people, supported those who fell, and pulled out those who were trapped. There was nothing conventionally heroic in their movements—only stubborn determination and a refusal to abandon anyone who could still be saved. But there were places where this was not enough, where the structure was collapsing faster than anyone could break through it with their hands or equipment.

That was where the Kharakternyks worked.

There were so few of them that each signal in the thought-link could be felt individually, like a knot around which the rescue effort was built. Several thousand among hundreds of millions—and in this city, only a handful, scattered across different sectors where the situation was already slipping out of control.

When a message about a blocked sector appeared in the channel, Raien immediately understood what it meant. Part of the city had shifted, blocking the exits, and people were trapped. Time was no longer measured in minutes, but in seconds.

"Send in a Kharakternyk," he transmitted. The reply came quickly, as if the Kharakternyk were already where they were needed.

Transport kept carrying more and more people out of the danger zone, but the tension was rising too. One of the supports holding up a substantial part of the section had begun to sway, and the incoming data left no room for illusions. The structure was collapsing, and there was no longer any way to hold it.

The rescuers reported that they would not have time to get everyone out. There was no despair in their words, only the acknowledgement that they were already working at their limits.

Raien felt the rhythm change in that sector. Portals opened one after another. People ran through them, heading for the recreational planet, and each one bought them another second. He could feel the flow, feel the tension holding him in place, and at the same time see how quickly time was running out.

"Withdraw," he transmitted when it became clear that staying any longer was dangerous.

But the signal from that location did not change. The Kharakternyk remained.

Not because of a mistake or a failure to understand, but because there were still people who had not made it through. The portal opened once more, then again, and with every movement the tension grew so intense that it could be felt physically, even through the thought-link.

Raien clenched his fingers, although he knew it would change nothing.

"Enough," he said, and the word carried more than an order.

There was no reply. And at that very moment, the support gave way.

The section sagged at first, as if hesitating, then plunged down with everything still inside it—with the rooms where people had been only minutes earlier, the corridors along which they had run, those who had not made it out, and the one who had stayed until the very end.

Raien stood watching the data, but he no longer saw it as numbers. He knew what it meant, and that certainty was heavier than any words. Where an enormous section of the city-path had stood only moments before, nothing remained. Everyone who had not been evacuated had died with it.

Every human death in this already small civilization was felt as a great tragedy. Now hundreds of people had died. The death of one of the Kharakternyks was an even heavier loss. The civilization depended on them, and they were the hope people clung to in the face of the planet's inevitable destruction.

He felt something inside him stop for a moment, but he did not allow it to become anything more. There were still people around him who could be saved.

"We continue," he transmitted, and his voice was steady again.

The evacuation went on, but now there was more pressure, more silence between words, and a greater awareness that every second could be someone's last. The rescuers worked with the same determination, and other Kharakternyks took responsibility for new areas, but the lost section remained a heavy burden in everyone's mind.

When the people were safe and the ground had stopped shaking, the city-path no longer looked as it had just a few hours earlier. Part of it was simply gone. Where life had once been, there was now a deep fissure in the earth, from which streams of molten magma began to burst.

Raien left the place, feeling the pull to look back, but understanding that it was no longer one section that needed saving. It was all of humanity.

On his way to the Hetman, he deliberately narrowed the thought-link, leaving only what was necessary. Even that did not allow him to shut himself off completely from what had happened. The moment when the signal cut out remained in his memory, along with the image of the enormous structure plunging into an equally enormous abyss.

Hetman Sukhum was already waiting.

The map before him was covered with points, each representing the same thing that had just happened here, only somewhere else or at another stage.

"We lost a section," Raien said. His voice remained calm, but there was something else beneath that calm now. "And a Kharakternyk."

Sukhum did not ask him to explain. Raien stepped closer and looked at the map, where the crustal fractures had already formed a network over the past several days.

"We can't respond in time. We have warnings, the protocols are working, but the interval between them and the beginning of the earthquakes keeps getting shorter. The orbital cities are overcrowded; the surface and underground cities are under constant threat. We lose people every time. Waiting is no longer merely impossible—it is deadly."

He paused, longer this time, because what he had to say was no longer just an assessment of the situation.

"We have to evacuate the civilization to Mars."

Sukhum raised his eyes. He knew how impossible such an evacuation would be, but it was their only chance. There was no longer any doubt.

"We need every resource we can find," Raien continued. "All the Kharakternyks, from both the Zero and First Plans. We must salvage everything that remains in the dead worlds—ships, engines, materials, power systems. Everything that might give us a chance."

Sukhum nodded slowly, already moving from understanding to action.

"We need transport, closed life-support systems, and resources that will last not for months, but for years."

Hetman Sukhum nodded heavily. He understood that this was a one-way journey. And there was no guarantee they would survive on the Red Planet.

"I'll inform Hetman Mykola," he replied heavily.

- Dmytro. The Discovery of Full Human Transfer Between Plans

The next morning, Dmytro woke up in his apartment and lay there for a long time, unwilling to get up. He needed to collect his thoughts. Only nine days had passed since he had jumped into another Plan after the Shahed explosion, yet his life had changed so radically that it now seemed more like some science-fiction story.

The decision to have coffee in his usual place came naturally. For some reason, the comfort of his apartment now only distracted him and made him slightly uneasy.

As he put on his shoes, he assessed his situation. Having become a standard-bearer-ataman, he had gained some of the Hetman's abilities and privileges. For example, he could now sense the brotherhood just as Grandfather did. It was as if he knew every Kharakternyk by sight, remembered every name, and could see how far each had progressed in developing their abilities. It was incredible, but it also placed a heavier burden on his shoulders.

---

As he stepped outside, already almost tasting his favourite coffee, Dmytro found some unexpected visitors waiting by the entrance. They were the two guards who had escorted him when he returned to his home Plan, and a female medic. They were sitting on a bench, waiting for him to appear.

The young woman sprang up as soon as she saw Dmytro and immediately switched his brain off by launching into a breathless account of how they had got there, how long they had waited, and how cold they had become because no one knew exactly which apartment he lived in—only the courtyard.

His brain switched back on only when she finally fell silent and looked at him with large, exhausted eyes, as if waiting for a verdict. If only Dmytro had managed to understand at least something…

"Right. I don't understand a thing," he finally said. "But first, let's get upstairs. I'll make you some tea, and then you can tell me the whole story again."

With that, he invited his guests inside and headed towards the entrance. The three travellers followed him, while the young woman no longer even tried to restrain herself. Her nervous exhaustion was taking its toll, and her quiet chatter trembled like a taut string all the way to the apartment.

Dmytro made the promised tea and served it with biscuits and cookies. When his guests had warmed up a little, he asked how they had ended up here. The guard answered first:

"Dmytro, it's a very strange story. We were simply thrown into your Plan. The law of return to one's home world took effect. But we have no idea how it happened that your world became our home world."

And then it was as if all the connections in Dmytro's mind suddenly clicked into place. The realization struck him instantly, filling him with joy and hope.

At first, they had opened the portal together. It led straight to Grandfather, to the Hetman. All three guests and Nairin had travelled through Dmytro's portal. But they had returned through a portal opened by Nairin. If the three travellers had been "thrown" back, then their home world must be the one Dmytro himself had brought them to.

This was further confirmed by the fact that they had appeared in this Plan close to Dmytro himself, rather than near the Hetman's house, where they had first entered and later left this world.

Dmytro jumped to his feet, newly determined. He realized this could change the situation in three key worlds at once. He could not wait any longer. Opening a portal, he invited the travellers to return to the Zero Plan.

The first thing he sensed upon arriving at the familiar station was a summons from Hetman Mykola, as Grandfather was now called. A difficult conversation with Hetman Sukhum was under way, and Dmytro was needed there. The young woman and the guards stayed behind, while Dmytro rushed towards the meeting hall like a whirlwind.

He burst in like lightning. His new rank was perceptible to the local Kharakternyks too, so no one stopped the standard-bearer, even though from the outside he looked as if he intended to demolish the entire council chamber, along with both Hetmans.

Inside, Dmytro saw Hetman Mykola and the blue-skinned Hetman of the Zero Plan. Both sat at a round table, grim and tense. Local standard-bearers and representatives of the council from among the non-Kharakternyks sat around them. The meeting was in full swing. The package of new information Dmytro transmitted to everyone present immediately after bursting in halted the difficult decision the Hetman of the Zero Plan had been preparing to announce.

"If this is true… we can save millions of lives," Grandfather Mykola breathed, stunned.

"Brothers, get every scientist onto the First Plan. Immediately! There is no more important matter for us—and there never has been."

It was not a request, but a stern and urgent order from the Hetman of the Zero Plan.

Civilization Z-0 shifted into a new gear, buoyed by renewed hope. Hetman Sukhum approached Grandfather Mykola and Dmytro and asked quietly and simply:

"Can I count on your help?"

"I think there is no longer any such thing as you and us. There is only us," Hetman Mykola replied just as quietly.

And the two Hetmans clasped hands firmly.

Day 10
- Grandfather Mykola. The Patron (Vasyl–Lech–Yevhen)

The very next day, Grandfather Mykola was receiving new guests. A large group of scientists had been transferred from the Zero Plan to the First Plan. Their leader greeted the Hetman warmly, got straight to business, and began explaining what they needed to work. In fact, the list of requirements turned out to be quite short: premises somewhere nearby and the ability to install equipment and laboratories there. But when he named the approximate area he wanted for his team, Grandfather gave a startled grunt. By all their calculations, they would have to find something like a three-storey mansion with extensive grounds.

When Grandfather explained to the scientist that such a property would require a considerable amount of money, it was the scientist's turn to be surprised. On the Zero Plan, he had long been accustomed to obtaining almost any resource, provided the project had a sufficiently high priority.

"Is that a problem?" he asked sincerely. "If the research is important, we are usually allocated everything we need."

"The rules are a little different here," Grandfather Mykola said with a smile. "Here, money decides everything. And I'm rather short of it at the moment."

And indeed, Grandfather had no money. But the community came to his aid. One of the newly initiated Kharakternyks turned out to have fairly close connections among major businesspeople. They decided to make use of those connections.

With the thought-link, everything could be arranged much faster, while local portals eliminated the need for transport altogether. So within an hour, Grandfather Mykola and the chief scientist stepped through a portal and found themselves beside the tall stone wall surrounding the private villa of their prospective patron.

The same newly initiated Kharakternyk was already waiting for them by the gate.

"Vasyl," he introduced himself briefly, extending his hand.

"Lech," the scientist replied, shaking it.

They approached the gate. Vasyl waved to the security staff. A burly man in uniform emerged from a small building nearby and initially regarded the visitors rather grimly. But as soon as he recognized Vasyl, his expression changed instantly.

"Well, look at that, Mr Vasyl!" he hurried towards the gate. "Why didn't you call?"

The gate began to open slowly.

"They're just not used to me arriving on foot," Vasyl smiled as they set off along the paved path towards the mansion. "I probably should have brought a car. But these portals are so much more convenient that sometimes you forget about ordinary ways of getting around."

The oligarch turned out to be a short, slender elderly man. Despite his age, however, he moved briskly and looked quite energetic. He greeted the guests in the spacious hall of his house.

"I didn't think you'd decide to walk all the way to my place, Vasyl," he joked, studying the visitors with interest.

"You have to keep in shape somehow," Vasyl replied without missing a beat.

All three smiled.

"Quite right," the host nodded. "Especially at your age. You have every chance of living to be as old as I am."

He turned his attention to Vasyl's companions.

"You can call me Yevhen."

"Lech. Senior Research Fellow," the scientist introduced himself politely.

"Hetman Mykola," Grandfather said solemnly. "But you can simply call me Grandfather Mykola."

Vasyl gave him a surprised look. He clearly had not expected such a formal opening.

The host's welcoming manner briefly disappeared. He looked more closely at Grandfather and shook his hand with considerably more restraint.

"Well, as for 'Grandfather'…" he began with a slight smile. "I suspect I may actually be older than you."

But Grandfather did not let him finish and immediately took the initiative.

"I wouldn't be so sure about that," he replied calmly. "So, shall we talk here, or would you prefer somewhere more comfortable?"

The owner of the house stared at him silently for several seconds, as if trying to work out who was standing before him. Then he gestured for the guests to follow him into the living room.

Once they were seated in comfortable armchairs and the housekeeper had brought fragrant tea, Yevhen leaned back and looked at Grandfather attentively.

"So, what is this conversation about… Hetman?" He deliberately emphasized the final word, as if testing how firmly it held.

Grandfather smiled beneath his moustache.

"People usually come to you for money, Mr Yevhen. Isn't that right?"

"Usually, yes," the man nodded.

"We will be no exception. But we need a little more than simple funding. We need long-term cooperation."

The host's cool, assessing gaze spoke for itself. He clearly disliked the fact that Grandfather was leading the conversation. But his intuition told him that these visitors were not ordinary people, and that restrained him from taking any rash action.

Grandfather sensed his mood perfectly and merely smiled faintly.

"You're probably wondering whose Hetman I am, Mr Yevhen?"

The man nodded silently.

"I am the Hetman of the Kharakternyks on this planet. And at the same time, a representative of a spacefaring civilization spanning three Plans."

The host's patience was almost exhausted. So Grandfather simply opened a small portal, just large enough for a hand to pass through, and took out a cup of coffee.

"Forgive me, Mr Yevhen. Your tea is excellent, but I love coffee too much."

The oligarch's eyes showed that he now understood this was neither a joke nor a trick.

"What exactly was that?" he asked cautiously.

"A small demonstration that I'm not some local eccentric. This is a portal to my home. My wife has just made me a coffee."

Grandfather took a sip.

"By the way, that's why we came on foot. Out of respect for you. We could simply have opened a portal and appeared right here in this room."

Yevhen remained silent for several seconds.

"All right," he finally said. "Then tell me plainly: what do you need from me?"

"Mr Yevhen," Grandfather replied calmly, "one demonstration does not give you a complete understanding of our capabilities. And therefore, it does not give me the right to tell you what we need from you. So I propose something better: visit one of our orbital stations. Once we're there, we can calmly discuss business."

It was clear that maintaining his outward composure was taking considerable effort for the oligarch. But when Vasyl opened a portal to the Zero Plan, Yevhen could not suppress an astonished exclamation.

Once aboard the station, Yevhen's emotions were running high. He no longer tried to restrain himself or pretend to be a dignified, wealthy oligarch. He wanted to know everything, all at once. Grandfather answered in as much detail as possible, but it was impossible to convey everything about the new world in ordinary words.

Eventually, they settled in a comfortable recreation area overlooking the planet's frozen expanses beneath the station and ordered coffee.

It was time to return to the main subject.

"So what exactly do you want from me?" Yevhen asked directly.

"Mr Yevhen," Grandfather replied, "our civilization has almost everything. But you have something we currently lack: Ukraine's money."

He nodded towards Lech.

"We need to accommodate a large group of scientists. Ideally, we would establish a separate research centre for them. And I'm sure other projects will emerge in time. We don't need one-off assistance. The best arrangement would be for you to become one of us."

The oligarch was silent for a moment.

"And what do I get in return?"

Grandfather smiled and held out a thought-link bracelet.

"I could talk for a long time. But it would be better if you saw everything for yourself. This bracelet will give you access to the information."

He paused.

"And just one small personal clarification: the average age of our citizens is two hundred to two hundred and fifty years. And throughout that time, a person can control the condition of their body."

Yevhen raised his eyebrows in surprise, put on the bracelet with considerably greater interest, and immediately immersed himself in communication with the artificial intelligence. Grandfather and his companions waited patiently, silently admiring the view of the cold planet beyond the panoramic window.

Day 11
- Olga. Inner Conflict

The first few days after her initiation seemed so strange to Olga that sometimes she caught herself feeling as though she were living in two different realities at once. Until very recently, she had been an ordinary, quiet intern at Dmytro's company—a girl almost no one noticed among the other employees. She arrived earlier than many of them, quietly sat down at her desk, completed her tasks carefully, and always tried to be as inconspicuous as possible. It had seemed perfectly natural to her when people interrupted her during meetings or when colleagues forgot her name just a few days after being introduced.

She had grown so accustomed to being the background to other people's lives that it had become part of her character. Even when she knew the right answer or spotted a mistake, it was easier for her to remain silent than to draw attention to herself. For years, Olga had lived with the feeling that other people were far more important, intelligent, and strong than she was, and that her own place was somewhere off to the side, where nothing particularly depended on her.

That was precisely why the changes that began after her initiation affected her far more deeply than the new abilities themselves. On the Zero Plan, people treated her completely differently. During training, they listened attentively not out of politeness, but because she really was learning quickly. When the instructors explained how portals worked and how to interact with space, Olga noticed that she was beginning to understand certain things sooner than the other newcomers.

One day, a Kharakternyk from the Zero Plan, a century older than her, calmly told her that she had an exceptionally rare sense of the structure of transitions. To others, those were just words, but to Olga they sounded almost painful. All her life she had been used to being someone no one said anything special about. Now the very thought that there might be something unique about her was beginning to change her from within.

She noticed it even in small things. Her movements became more confident, her posture straighter, and her familiar guarded stiffness gradually disappeared. Previously, she had instinctively tried to take up as little space as possible around other people. Now she increasingly caught herself looking interlocutors directly in the eye and no longer fearing that her words might sound foolish or out of place.

Along with this confidence came a sense of power that both exhilarated and frightened her. Olga was beginning to understand how easily someone who had felt worthless for years could suddenly fall in love with their own importance. Sometimes she even enjoyed the way the Kharakternyks of the Zero Plan addressed her, how attentively they listened to her answers, or how approvingly they nodded after another training session.

That was why evenings in her small one-room apartment had become almost painful. Returning home after training, she felt as though she were crossing a boundary between two worlds. Here, everything remained as it had been: the old lift with its scratched doors, the smell of other people's dinners drifting from the stairwell, televisions behind the walls endlessly talking about the front, new shelling, and international aid.

---

The neighbours continued living their ordinary lives in a country at war with a much stronger northern state. Every morning, the elderly woman in the apartment opposite spent a long time discussing with other pensioners where the latest overnight strike had landed. The young couple one floor below argued almost every evening about money and whether the husband should wait for his draft notice or go to the military enlistment office of his own accord. In the evenings, men smoked by the entrance and argued about the front as though their words could change the course of the war.

During air-raid alerts, all of this became even more vivid. Some people hurried to shelters; others had grown so accustomed to danger that they simply switched off the lights and stayed home. Small children stood sleepily in corridors, clutching blankets, while adults pretended everything was normal, even though exhaustion and fear had long since seeped into every movement and conversation.

Olga looked at all this with completely different eyes now, and that was beginning to frighten her. She knew things that shattered these people's entire picture of the world. She knew about other Plans, about Kharakternyks, about the civilization of the Zero Plan, and about how small this war really was compared with what was approaching humanity.

Sometimes this gave her a terrible sense of distance from ordinary people. Not out of hatred or contempt, but because she understood that she now saw so much more than they did. And this was where her main inner conflict began.

Part of Olga was still the kind, quiet girl who worried about other people's problems, was afraid of hurting anyone, and genuinely sympathized with those around her. But alongside her, another Olga was gradually emerging—the one who was beginning to feel special. Not just a person, but one of the first female Kharakternyks of the First Plan.

Sometimes it showed in little things: a brief irritation at someone else's slowness; an inward sense of superiority when she listened to neighbours arguing about matters that now seemed small and naive to her; and sometimes, a dangerous satisfaction in the very thought that she was no longer like everyone else.

She hated herself for these thoughts, but she could not get rid of them completely. Part of her really was beginning to believe that she was better than other people. That frightened her most of all, because Olga understood very well how easily someone who had felt weak all their life could turn into an arrogant, conceited person as soon as they gained power and respect.

The thought-link made everything even more complicated. At first, the very presence of other people in her consciousness frightened her almost to the point of panic. Even when no one was transmitting thoughts directly, she could still sense the emotional background of the other Kharakternyks—their fatigue, calm, tension, confidence, and sometimes even irritation or fear.

But very quickly, she began to fear not other people's thoughts, but her own. She was frightened that others might sense her new arrogance, her fascination with her own strength, or those brief moments when she looked down on ordinary people. Because of this, Olga began unconsciously shielding herself from the thought-link, muting her presence in the shared Kharakternyk network.

At first, it seemed like protection, a way to hide her shame and inner struggle. But she soon realized that along with her bad thoughts, she was also shutting herself off from other people. And that brought a new fear: the fear of being alone even among those who had now become part of her new world.

Sometimes, in the evening, sitting by a dark window and watching people silently smoke in the courtyard while sirens wailed, Olga understood one thing very clearly. What was being decided now was not merely what kind of Kharakternyk she would become. Something far more important was at stake: would she remain the kind and compassionate girl she had once been, or would power gradually turn her into someone who looked down on everyone else?

For now, she had no answer.

Day 12 (1100)
- Dmytro. Wave Sharpening

The training sector was almost empty that day. The main groups had already finished their morning sessions, and the enormous hall had settled into a quieter rhythm. Somewhere in the distance, the rhythmic clang of metal could be heard; in the neighbouring sectors, the heavy mechanisms of the training platforms moved with a dull rumble, while ventilation systems hummed continuously beneath the ceiling, circulating cool station air. The tall, dark walls of the arena were covered with thousands of marks from past training sessions—scratches, notches, dents, and long, thin cuts that looked as though the metal had once simply parted under the blows of some unknown force.

Dmytro stood in the centre of the arena, slowly rotating the sabres in his hands, getting used to their weight again after several days of continuous training. Lately, the sessions had become much harder. The Kharakternyks of the Zero Plan no longer treated him as a novice who needed to be protected or whose fighting pace had to be constantly restrained. Dmytro could clearly feel the change in their attitude towards him.

Nairin stood opposite, lightly rolling his sabre between his fingers as though it were not a weapon, but an ordinary practice staff. His movements seemed calm, even a little careless, but Dmytro already knew how deceptive that relaxed appearance was. The moment the standard-bearer-ataman accelerated even slightly, the air began to whistle from the speed of his strikes.

"Ready?" the ataman asked briefly.

Dmytro nodded silently and raised his sabres.

The first exchange of blows was almost gentle. Steel rang against steel several times. Nairin tested his stance, forced him to shift left, then suddenly accelerated, and Dmytro barely managed to deflect a strike aimed straight at his shoulder. The next thrust came from below, followed by a pommel strike, a quick turn, and a sharp step backwards.

Dmytro felt the familiar heat of combat spread through his body. His mind cleared of unnecessary thoughts, his hearing sharpened, and his movements became quicker and more precise. He surged forward, forcing Nairin onto the defensive. For several seconds they exchanged blows so quickly that only flashing blades and tiny sparks could be seen around them.

"You're tense today," Nairin remarked between strikes.

"And you're far too relaxed today," Dmytro replied, trying to reach him with a sideways thrust.

Nairin gave the slightest smile and easily parried the attack.

Dmytro tried to get around him from the side again, but the ataman was already reading his movements in advance. Nairin's sabre slid along Dmytro's blade, changed direction sharply, and stopped right at his throat.

"Half a second too late."

"But I almost got you."

They stepped apart, breathing heavily. Dmytro wiped the sweat from his forehead with his sleeve and looked attentively at Nairin.

"Can I ask you something?"

"If it's not nonsense."

"The dark Kharakternyks… who are they, exactly?"

Nairin's expression did not change. He simply looked at Dmytro in silence for a few seconds, then slowly rolled his sabre in his hand.

"Let's try the special arena. You need to train under more difficult conditions," he said calmly, as if he had not heard the question.

The subject remained hanging in the air.

They resumed their duel, this time surrounded by obstacles activated through thought-command. During the first few minutes, the fight remained fast, but somehow restrained. There was no real pressure, no attempt to overwhelm the opponent with sheer pace. Dmytro had grown accustomed to the fighting style of the Zero Plan Kharakternyks and now understood much better why they were so dangerous even in ordinary training. They wasted no movement. Every step, every turn of the body, even the slightest shift of the shoulders looked natural and economical, as though the body itself found the most efficient way to act.

The metal rang in short, sharp bursts. The sabres constantly slid against one another, sending tiny sparks flying. Dmytro tried several times to speed up and impose an aggressive series of attacks on Nairin, but the ataman almost always shifted half a step before the strike could reach its target.

This began to irritate Dmytro. He had been told more than once that he moved faster than most new Kharakternyks of the First Plan and learned much faster than others. But beside Nairin, that meant almost nothing. The ataman's experience outweighed everything.

"And still, what do you know about the dark Kharakternyks?" Dmytro asked again, parrying a strike and moving aside.

This time Nairin did not answer immediately. He continued fighting, but Dmytro noticed almost at once that the rhythm had changed. The ataman's sabres began moving faster and more forcefully, as though he were no longer training but fighting a real enemy.

"Almost nothing," he finally said. "We encountered them only once. They killed my brother."

He drove a strike sharply downwards, nearly making Dmytro lose his balance, then immediately launched a series of powerful attacks, each new blow seeming to reinforce the next words.

"We don't know how many of them there are. We don't know how long they've existed. We don't know whether they come from another Plan or are hiding somewhere in the Zero Plan."

Dmytro barely managed to block the next strike.

"You saw them last. How are they different from you? From us?"

Nairin suddenly changed the direction of his attack and almost immediately broke through Dmytro's defence with a short pommel strike to the shoulder.

"From us, by the pattern of their thoughtphones."

The pace was now increasing noticeably. Dmytro felt it almost at once. Nairin was no longer holding back and began gradually overwhelming him with experience. Not through brute force or incredible speed, but through his ability to control the duel.

Every mistake Dmytro made was punished immediately. Every misstep cost him his position. Every overly broad attack opened up another opportunity for a counterstrike. After a few minutes, Dmytro was already breathing heavily. His forearms were growing tired, and his hands were finding it increasingly difficult to withstand the constant, forceful clashes of the blades.

"And from you?" Dmytro asked.

"Nothing," Nairin said quietly, without slowing down. "And yet, everything."

After those words, he launched a series of strikes that Dmytro did not merely fail to see—he did not even have time to comprehend them. The ataman's sabres merged into one continuous dark movement, and Dmytro only managed to leap backwards at the last moment, instinctively escaping a strike aimed at his torso.

At that moment, he understood something very clearly, something he had previously only suspected: talent could accelerate a Kharakternyk's development, but it could never replace years of real experience.

And Nairin had immense experience.

It was as though he could see the duel on a much broader scale. He knew in advance when Dmytro would begin to lose his pace, when he would take a risk, when instinct would make him switch to defence. Dmytro was only making a decision, while the ataman was already setting a trap for his next move.

Suddenly, Nairin stopped and stepped back several paces.

"Let's try something different."

He slowly raised one of his blades in front of him. At first, nothing happened. Then a faint glimmer of light ran along the metal. A moment later, blue waves of light began spreading along the entire blade, as though a hidden energy had awakened inside the sabre. The air around it trembled almost imperceptibly, and tiny sparks occasionally flickered between the ataman's two sabres.

Dmytro froze involuntarily. Even the walls of the arena seemed to change beneath that cold light.

"A duel at full power," Nairin said calmly. "Activate wave sharpening. Let's see what you're made of."

"What is that?" Dmytro asked in surprise.

Nairin looked at him attentively, and for the first time genuine astonishment appeared in his eyes.

"Wave sharpening is another one of our abilities. A Kharakternyk can activate it on any weapon, and then that weapon will encounter almost no obstacle."

He slowly drew the activated blade along the edge of a metal training platform. The thick armour plate split in two as easily as if it were made of wet cardboard. There was no heavy impact or loud grinding sound—the metal simply parted beneath the blade.

"We still haven't found a material that can reliably withstand this sharpening," Nairin continued calmly. "That's why even an ordinary sabre belonging to a Kharakternyk of the Zero Plan is considered a terrifying weapon."

He turned his gaze to Dmytro.

"Give it a try."

Dmytro raised his sabres and concentrated. At first, he tried simply to reproduce the inner sensation he had seen in Nairin. Then he began trying to guide energy along the blade, the way he had once learned to work with the thought-link.

Nothing happened.

The sabres remained ordinary.

Nairin patiently explained it to him again and again. He showed him exactly what the moment of activation felt like, as though the very structure of the metal began to resonate with the Kharakternyk. He was absolutely certain that Dmytro simply had not yet learned how to do it.

Dmytro tried again. Then again. Minutes passed, but the result did not change. Nairin gradually fell silent and simply watched his sabres closely. At one point, he slowly lowered his own blade, and the waves of light along it began to fade.

"Strange," the standard-bearer said quietly.

"What is?"

Nairin was silent for several seconds.

"We tried teaching this to your Kharakternyks from the First Plan before. But back then, everyone decided the problem was insufficient training."

He looked carefully at Dmytro.

"Now I'm no longer sure."

Dmytro silently stared at his sabres.

"It seems wave sharpening really is connected specifically to the Zero Plan," Nairin finally said. "You simply don't have this ability. Just as we cannot open local portals…"

- Grandfather Mykola. 1,100 Kharakternyks

The twelfth day since the beginning of the initiation was the first time in many years that Grandfather Mykola had been genuinely bewildered.

He stood on the porch of his house, as he had done almost every day since it had all begun. Before him lay a quiet village street. Grandfather Mykola could almost physically sense them—as though little lights were being kindled one after another somewhere in the darkness far away.

Just a few days earlier, there had been a little over two hundred of them. Now there were more than a thousand. To be precise, one thousand one hundred and two Kharakternyks.

Grandfather Mykola checked the number through the connection several times, as if afraid he might have made a mistake. But the number remained the same. And every few hours it grew.

The explosive growth had begun around the ninth day. At first, the Kharakternyks simply found new people—people who suddenly began responding through the thought-link. Then it became clear that initiations were happening in waves. It was as if the world's system itself had awakened and begun revealing, on a massive scale, those who had the potential.

New Kharakternyks appeared in the most unexpected places: military units, hospitals, railway stations, small villages, and large cities.

Kharakternyks who had already completed their training travelled across the country in small groups. Some worked with the military, others with civilians, while some focused solely on finding newly initiated people. The system was growing almost by itself. And that was a little frightening.

Grandfather Mykola was used to changes happening slowly. In his long life, most major undertakings had required years, sometimes even decades. Here, everything was changing literally by the hour.

Only yesterday, he had known almost every Kharakternyk by name. Now that was no longer possible.

The connection between them had begun reorganizing itself. Small groups appeared, naturally gathering around the stronger Kharakternyks. They trained newcomers, passed on knowledge, and coordinated their actions. In this way, small centres began to form—little networks within the larger one.

Grandfather Mykola could feel them all.

Sometimes it reminded him of the night sky. You look into the darkness, and suddenly new stars begin appearing one after another. At first there are only a few, then more, until you realize that an entire universe lies before you.

The thought-link was becoming the same.

At one point, he even felt a slight overload—not physical, but something more internal. Too many new minds were entering the network at once. He had to learn to dampen the general noise of thoughts a little; otherwise, concentrating would have been impossible.

A gate creaked somewhere nearby. Two young men entered the yard—still very inexperienced Kharakternyks, brought here a few hours earlier. They looked around uncertainly, as though they had found themselves in an unfamiliar world.

Grandfather Mykola smiled. Two more. Their new, still unsteady signals quietly flickered in the thought-link. They resembled tiny sparks—weak, but alive.

"Don't worry," he said aloud, stepping down from the porch. "The first few hours are always a little strange."

The young men exchanged glances. One of them nodded cautiously.

"Can we… really hear people inside our heads?"

"You can," the old man answered calmly. "And with time, you'll learn to control it."

He closed his eyes for a moment, sensing the network of Kharakternyks around him.

"One thousand one hundred and four."

The number had changed again. Somewhere far away, perhaps in another city or even in a military unit, one more Kharakternyk had appeared. Grandfather Mykola quietly exhaled. He no longer doubted it: this was neither a coincidence nor a series of isolated awakenings. It was a wave.

- Nairin. A Conversation with the Hetman

After the training session, Dmytro silently said goodbye and opened a portal home, to the First Plan. Nairin stood in the middle of the training ground for a few more seconds, looking at the empty space where the passage had just disappeared. The air still carried a faint smell of ozone and heated metal from the activated wave sharpening, and thin dark marks from the sabre strikes remained in places on the floor.

The training ground was almost empty. The instructors were packing away their equipment, and several younger Kharakternyks were discussing among themselves what they had seen during the training. Nairin paid almost no attention to them. His mind kept replaying the entire fight with Dmytro—every movement, every emotional surge, every change in the thoughtphone pattern.

Hetman Sukhum had been waiting for his report ever since this test had begun. He had been the one to ask Nairin not merely to train the new standard-bearer from the First Plan, but to test him as rigorously as possible. As rigorously as one could without actually intending to kill him.

The Zero Planners, on the other hand, possessed weapons capable of cutting through any known material, yet remained bound to ordinary physical movement within their world. "Dmytro understood exactly what I was doing. I could see that clearly through the thought-link. Several times, he even tried to reproduce the correct state almost perfectly. But there was no result. None at all. There wasn't even the slightest reaction that could have been developed through training."

"So the mechanism itself is absent in him."

The Hetman was silent for several seconds.

"This confirms your earlier theory about two separate branches of abilities."

"It does more than confirm it," Nairin replied quietly. "After today's training, I'm almost certain."

"But that was not the main part of the test."

"Throughout the fight, I deliberately provoked strong emotions in Dmytro. First, ordinary combat tension. Then excitement. Irritation. Anger at his own mistakes. Later, I began applying much greater pressure, gradually pushing the duel to the point where he was forced to fight almost at the limit of his abilities."

There was neither regret nor doubt in Nairin's voice. Only dry analysis.

"When we started talking about the dark ones, I deliberately did not change the subject. On the contrary, I increased the pace of the fight even further. I wanted to see how his thoughtphone would behave under fear, anger, and pressure all at once."

Nairin thought for a few seconds, searching for a more precise word.

"Consistent. I could understand every one of his states. Every emotion. There was nothing resembling what I had seen in the dark ones."

Sukhum tilted his head slightly.

"Are you sure?"

"Absolutely."

This time the answer came without the slightest hesitation.

"The thoughtphone of the dark ones changed differently. Not gradually. Not because of emotions. At a certain point, it simply became alien. As if the very structure of the person began to break down or… to be replaced by something else. It's difficult to explain in words, but I remember that state too well to mistake it."

Silence fell in the office.

Sukhum did not hurry him. He knew that Nairin was now recalling not only his own observations, but also his brother's death.

The standard-bearer looked aside for a while, then continued more calmly:

"Dmytro and the other Kharakternyks of the First Plan do indeed share some dangerous similarities with the dark ones. Unstable initialization. Explosive growth in power. Very rapid adaptation of the thoughtphone. A certain emotional instability in the early stages."

He paused.

"But now I'm almost certain: they are not the same dark ones we encountered back then."

Sukhum watched him attentively without interrupting.

"I cannot rule out the possibility that Kharakternyks of the First Plan could turn into dark ones under certain conditions," Nairin said quietly. "Perhaps even more easily than we could. But if that happens, it will be a different form of darkness. A different nature of change."

He slowly raised his eyes to the Hetman.

"Those who killed Zairin were not First Planners. I have almost no doubt about that now."

Day 13.
- Dmytro. Changes in Training

The Zero Plan Station was now almost unrecognizable. It had changed so much in just ten days.

The view of the training complexes from one of the observation balconies fascinated Dmytro. Until very recently, only a few dozen instructors and several hundred students had worked there. Training had proceeded calmly, almost academically—each group was taught in detail, sometimes even too slowly.

Now everything looked completely different.

The flow of new Kharakternyks had become so large that the old training system had to be rebuilt literally on the fly. Groups replaced one another almost without interruption, the training grounds were rarely empty, and instructors worked from morning until night. Where a single group had trained just a week earlier, three or four were now working at the same time.

Some were practising the basic exercises of the thought-link—learning to distinguish other people's thoughts, quiet the noise of the network, and transmit simple images. Others were already working on movement: short-range portals, group synchronization, and the evacuation of the wounded.

The pace of training had almost doubled. And even that was no longer enough.

The greatest problem had suddenly turned out to be the instructors. There simply were not enough old trainers from Z-0 to teach so many people. At first, they tried to stretch their schedules, work longer hours, and take larger groups. But it soon became clear that even they would not be able to keep that up for long.

So they had to do something that had seemed premature just a few days earlier. Kharakternyks from the first wave were brought into the training process. These were the very people who had been initiated among the first on Earth-1 and had already completed the full basic training cycle. Many of them had been students themselves only recently, but now they were quickly becoming assistant instructors, taking charge of younger groups and helping newcomers master the first steps.
