When the pain finally receded, a strange lightness took its place. Georges had not yet opened his eyes, but he could already smell the familiar scent of cold metal, sterile air, and antiseptic. After three days surrounded by smoke, blood, and the smell of fires, it seemed almost unnatural. Yet Georges immediately understood where he was.

The medical sector of the Zero Plane.

He drew a slow breath, as if checking that he really could breathe again, and only then opened his eyes. Above him was the ordinary white ceiling of a military hospital. Not the clear blue sky, not the nuclear clouds on the horizon, and not the fragments of an orbital station scattering high above a doomed world.

Georges stared at the ceiling for a long time without moving. He felt that if he closed his eyes, he would find himself among the containers again, hearing gunshots or feeling the concrete tremble beneath his feet from a distant nuclear explosion. But all around him was a silence he had not heard for many days.

The door opened quietly, and an elderly doctor entered the room. He carefully checked the monitors tracking the wounded man’s condition, reviewed several readings, and only then looked at Georges.

“Finally,” he said softly. “We were beginning to worry.”

Georges did not answer. The doctor did not seem to expect him to. People forcibly returned from other planes often did not regain consciousness immediately, and during the first hours after waking they rarely spoke. This time, however, it was not only the patient’s physical condition that concerned the doctor. There was something in Georges’s eyes that no instrument could measure.

“You were found unconscious immediately after your return,” he continued. “A head injury, multiple bruises. Your bracelet was completely destroyed. To be honest, we still do not understand how you survived the forced return in that condition.”

Georges slowly turned his head.

“The others?”

The doctor did not answer at once, and that brief pause was enough. Georges closed his eyes. He did not know what answer he had hoped for when he asked, but at least now he did not have to wait for it.

Several more minutes passed before the doctor spoke again.

“The characternyks have returned. Not all of them, but those who did are already reporting what happened. As soon as the doctors allow it, they will want to speak with you as well.”

Georges gave a barely perceptible nod. He thought of Viktor, of the young soldier who had dropped to his knees in the middle of the platform with his hands pressed to his head, of the elderly technician with the metal mug, and of the driver who had been laughing while talking through his bracelet only minutes before the shooting began. The little child’s backpack with its model spaceship appeared before his eyes again, followed by the blinding flash in the sky that had swallowed the orbital station.

All those people had remained there. He had come back.

Georges could find neither justice nor logic in it. He knew there had been nothing he could do to change what happened, but that did not make it any easier. Of the millions of people who had been living ordinary lives only three days earlier—building cities, raising children, and preparing to travel to other planets—he was one of the few who had been destined to leave that world alive.

The next morning, the doctors allowed Georges to have a brief conversation. He was sitting by the wide window of his room, looking out over the endless white expanses of the Zero Plane, when the door quietly opened. A tall Black man in simple dark clothes, without any show of luxury, entered the room. Only a small silver insignia on his collar indicated that the man before Georges was the hetman.

An adjutant followed him in, placed a small folder on the table, and immediately left. The hetman did not hurry to sit down. He walked to the window and watched the snow falling slowly over the plain for a few seconds. Then he turned to Georges.

“Thank you for surviving.”

There was no ceremony in those words, and that was precisely why Georges could not think of a reply.

“I’m not sure that’s something to thank me for.”

The hetman looked at him a little longer than Georges found comfortable, but did not argue. He sat down opposite him and placed the folder on his knees.

“Perhaps you’ll change your mind when you understand what you brought back with you.”

Georges frowned.

“I didn’t bring anything back.”

“Information.”

The hetman opened the folder but did not take anything out.

“The characternyks managed to learn a few things before the situation became uncontrollable. What happened on that plane was not an accident. They found traces of people who had prepared this catastrophe in advance. We still do not know who they really are, how large their organization is, or what they were trying to achieve. But at least now we know that someone was behind the destruction of that world.”

Georges felt something tighten inside him. For three days he had tried to find an enemy, convincing himself that someone had to be behind everything he had witnessed. Then, when people began destroying their own cities and nuclear missiles started flying into territories belonging to their own states, he had almost accepted that there was no opponent he could see or name.

“Have you found them?”

“No,” the hetman replied. “Only traces. And that is not enough. We know someone set this process in motion, but we still do not understand how.”

He leaned forward slightly.

“That is why I need to hear everything you saw. Not just what you think is important. Everything.”

At first, Georges did not know where to begin. He told him about the first shot, about the soldier from his unit who had opened fire on his comrades for no apparent reason, and about people killing one another without fear or hatred. He mentioned the characternyks, who had tried to stop the chaos but could not understand whom to save, because the person they rescued could become an attacker a moment later.

The hetman asked very few questions. Only occasionally did he ask Georges to clarify the timing or return to a particular detail. Georges gradually reached the moment when he had regained consciousness on the empty platform, then described the three days during which he had watched the world fall apart.

“I kept looking for an enemy,” he finally said. “Even when it became clear that people were shooting one another, I was certain there had to be someone directing it all from somewhere. Saboteurs, the military, someone in the command centers. I was looking for some kind of pattern.”

“And did you find one?”

Georges shook his head.

“No. People were simply doing it themselves. The worst part was that they didn’t look insane. They knew how to use weapons, operated machinery, carried out complicated actions. One soldier calmly changed his magazine before continuing to shoot his own people. He acted as if he were following an ordinary order.”

The hetman remained silent, but Georges noticed that his fingers had stopped moving along the edge of the folder.

“There was something else,” Georges recalled. “It may mean nothing.”

“Tell me.”

Georges rubbed his left wrist. The bracelet was no longer there, but after years of habit his hand still searched for its familiar surface.

“At the hospital. I saw a recording from a security camera. A man attacked the medical staff. During the struggle, his bracelet broke. Completely. And after that… he stopped.”

The hetman’s expression did not change, but Georges immediately sensed that something had happened.

“What do you mean, ‘stopped’?”

“As if he had come to his senses. Before that, he was trying to kill them. Then he just sat on the floor and looked around. I don’t know what happened next. The recording ended.”

“Are you certain the bracelet was destroyed before his behavior changed?”

Georges thought for a moment, reconstructing the short recording in his memory.

“Yes.”

The hetman slowly closed the folder.

For the first time during the conversation, Georges saw a genuine reaction on his face. Not fear or surprise, but the sudden, almost cold concentration of someone who had just noticed a connection between things that had previously seemed unrelated.

“And your bracelet?”

“It broke before the transition. I tried to restart it that morning, but it was already dead. The impact destroyed it completely.”

The hetman looked at Georges in silence for several seconds.

“Characternyks don’t use bracelets,” he said, more to himself than to his companion.

Georges felt the pieces begin to fall into an unpleasant sequence. Characternyks did not use the technological thought-link. His own bracelet had stopped working before the catastrophe began. The man in the hospital had stopped attacking immediately after his device was destroyed.

He looked at the hetman.

“You think…”

“For now, I think nothing,” the hetman interrupted calmly. “For now, we are checking.”

But Georges understood that this was not true. The hetman was already thinking. He might even know exactly whom he would pass this information to as soon as he left the room.

Georges leaned back in his chair. Suddenly he felt cold, although the room was warm. Before his eyes appeared the young transport-platform driver laughing as he received someone’s words through his bracelet. The elderly technician with the metal mug. The soldiers from his unit. Millions of people for whom the thought-link had become such an ordinary part of life that they had almost stopped noticing the device on their own wrists.

“We gave them this technology,” Georges said quietly.

The hetman did not answer immediately.

“We gave them a tool that was supposed to make their lives better. The fact that someone found a way to turn it into a weapon is the responsibility of those who did it.”

Georges looked at him.

“And you’ll find them?”

The hetman held his gaze.

“We’ll try.”

For some reason, that answer did not satisfy Georges. Only a few days earlier, he would have accepted it without question. There were soldiers, characternyks, intelligence services—people whose job it was to find enemies and stop them. He was a soldier. His task was to receive an order and carry it out. But now there were people somewhere who had managed to destroy an entire civilization without ever showing themselves to their victims. They had not arrived with an army, seized cities, or declared war. They had found a weakness no one had noticed, then forced billions of people to do all the work for them.

Georges suddenly realized that he wanted to know who they were.

Not for revenge. At least, he tried to convince himself it was not for revenge. He needed to understand how something on such a scale could have been prepared without anyone noticing. How long had they been operating? Who had helped them? Had this world been the first? And the worst question came last. Would it be the last?

The hetman had already risen, but he seemed to notice something in Georges’s expression and paused by the door.

“For three days, you searched for an enemy no one could see,” he said. “And yet you found what he left behind.”

Georges shook his head.

“By chance.”

“Most important things are found by chance at first. The difference lies in who is capable of noticing them.”

The hetman opened the door, but before leaving he looked at Georges once more.

“Get well. I think we’ll have more to discuss.”

The door closed, and Georges remained sitting by the window. Outside, snow fell slowly over the endless plain of the Zero Plane. Until recently, the sight had seemed bleak and joyless to him, especially after the green forests and clear skies of the other world. Now he looked at the familiar landscape and felt only relief that people were still alive here.

But his thoughts remained far away. He saw the transport platform again, the people with their eerily calm, empty faces, and the little bracelet on the wrist of the man who had stopped killing as soon as the device broke.

Somewhere out there were the people who had done all this, and Georges was filled with determination to find them and prevent any more civilizations from being destroyed.
