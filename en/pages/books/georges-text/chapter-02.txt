A few moments later, a portal opened in the middle of the warehouse. It did not glow or roar. It was as if someone had carefully cut an enormous opening into reality itself, revealing another world beyond it.

The commander gave a brief signal with his hand.

“Move out.”

The cargo platforms rolled toward the portal one after another, while Georges’ unit took up positions around the convoy. Through the breach in space, the clear blue sky of another world was already visible. As he approached it, Georges found himself staring at that blue, almost unreal to someone from the Zero Plane.

No matter how hard physicists tried to explain the nature of the planes, or philosophers the origins of the characternyky, there was still something incomprehensible to Georges about the very existence of such a passage. On one side lay a cold orbital station above an Earth that was slowly dying. A few steps away was another world, with its own sky, oceans, cities, and billions of people who, until quite recently, had not even suspected that the Zero Plane existed. The crossing took only an instant. Georges had long grown used to the brief chill that accompanied every passage between planes, but his body still shuddered involuntarily. One more step, and the cold air of the orbital station was behind him.

Warmth was the first thing he noticed. The air here was humid and remarkably light, filled with the scents of grass, sun-warmed earth, and something else for which Georges could not even find a name. On the Zero Plane, such smells could be experienced only beneath the protective domes of natural complexes, where every patch of living soil was maintained by dozens of sophisticated systems.

Georges slowly removed his helmet. He did that whenever he had the chance. A vast transport complex spread out before him, but his gaze soon moved beyond the concrete yards and hangars. Forests darkened the horizon, ribbons of rivers glimmered between them, and far in the distance a mountain range rose, its peaks lost among white clouds.

Yet what impressed him most, as always, was the sky. Clear, deep, and boundless, it made Georges forget for at least a few seconds why he had come here. On the Zero Plane, several generations had grown up knowing the true color of the Earth's sky mostly from old photographs and recordings made before the Great Ash.

Here, all of this was simply part of everyday life. A light breeze stirred the treetops, unfamiliar birds circled above the grass, and somewhere nearby children were laughing. The sound came from a small park beyond the complex fence, where several children were chasing one another, paying no attention at all to the soldiers who had just arrived from another world.

Georges found himself smiling. Every time he came here, he thought that their own Earth might have remained just like this if people had never turned it into the battlefield of the last great war. No endless city-paths hidden from deadly cold and ash. No constant fear of new earthquakes, any one of which might one day bury thousands of people.

Viktor stopped beside him. He had also removed his helmet and stood silently for a few seconds, breathing in the warm air.

“You know...” he said at last. “Every time I come here, I feel like a tourist.”

Georges laughed.

“And I feel like a savage.”

“Why?”

“Because I still can't stop staring at the trees.”

Viktor smiled and nodded. There was no need to explain. On the Zero Plane, real trees had long since become a rarity. Green spaces existed beneath protective domes or inside multi-level natural complexes, where every plant was monitored almost as carefully as a museum exhibit once had been. Here, forests stretched all the way to the horizon, and no one even thought of them as anything unusual.

That was exactly why Georges liked these assignments. They reminded him what the people of the Zero Plane were still searching for. Not new territory or weapons, but the chance to stop counting how many centuries their planet had left.

The commander raised his hand again, bringing everyone back to work.

“Stay focused. The transport platform is five hundred meters ahead. We have ten minutes to hand over the cargo.”

Georges put his helmet back on, lowered the visor, and followed his squad.

The transport platform was located several hundred meters from the warehouse complex. The unit moved along a wide road running through an enormous logistics hub. Cargo platforms streamed past in both directions, automated loaders carried containers, and dozens of workers in light-colored overalls went about their business. No one paid much attention to the armed soldiers. After years of cooperation, the arrival of characternyky and units from the Zero Plane had become almost as routine for the locals as the arrival of another transport ship.

Georges walked a little behind the commander, automatically surveying the area. Military habit made him note the positions of buildings, possible cover, and escape routes. Yet this time his eyes kept returning to the city that began just beyond the transport hub.

Light-colored buildings rose above the treetops, their broad glass façades reflecting the sunlight. Passenger platforms glided silently between them, while an orbital elevator climbed high into the sky. Its slender silver line disappeared among the clouds, connecting the planet's surface to orbital stations that glimmered faintly in the blue.

“Look,” Viktor said quietly. “They've finally completed the second station.”

Georges looked up. During their previous visit, construction ships had still been working around the new structure. Now the station looked complete, and transport vessels moved constantly around it, traveling between orbit, the planet's surface, and the lunar bases.

“They build fast,” he said with a smile.

“I hear they've already approved a new program.”

“What program?”

“The first expedition to Mars.”

Georges kept looking at the sky for a few more seconds. The Zero Plane had also returned to space long ago, and the orbital station they had just left was the best proof of that. Still, the two civilizations had taken very different paths toward the stars.

His own world had spent decades rebuilding at least part of what it had lost in the Great Ash. Even now, the Zero Plane devoted much of its strength to fighting the consequences of the ancient war and searching for a way to save its doomed Earth. This civilization, by contrast, could afford to look ahead and plan for the future not just in terms of the next few decades, but entire generations.

When the characternyky first made contact with this planet, they had prepared for the worst. Among the worlds they had discovered, living civilizations were far less common than dead ones. The causes were almost always different: wars, epidemics, natural disasters, technological accidents, or events that even decades of research had failed to explain. In some places, entire peoples had left behind nothing but ruins. In others, life had vanished along with the last traces of civilization. And sometimes the characternyky arrived in a world just as its inhabitants were still fighting for survival.

No one knew why there were so many dead worlds. At first, the characternyky had tried to find a pattern. They compared the histories of fallen civilizations, the causes of their catastrophes, and the final years of their existence. But the more worlds they discovered, the more confusing the picture became. The causes of their destruction were too different, and too little connected them. Eventually, dead worlds simply became another part of the endless diversity of the planes.

That was why this planet was such a rare exception. Here, the characternyky had found neither ruins nor a civilization in need of rescue, but equals—people capable not only of accepting new knowledge, but of sharing their own discoveries.

That was why cooperation had so quickly grown into a genuine partnership. Sometimes Georges felt that this was what the future of humanity was supposed to look like: no struggle for the right to be stronger, with each world developing along its own path, yet knowing that when the hardest moment came, someone on the other side of a portal would be there to offer a hand.

“It's a shame we can't just move here,” Georges said thoughtfully.

Viktor glanced sideways at him.

“We can. For three days.”

“Very funny.”

“I'm serious. After that, either a characternyk brings you home, or the Universe does it for you. I wouldn't recommend the second option, though.”

Georges grimaced. He had heard stories from people who had experienced forced return. They all described it differently, but agreed on one thing without exception: no one would willingly go through it a second time.

“I hear it hurts.”

“From what people say, that's putting it mildly,” Viktor replied.

Georges smiled, though the rule they were discussing was no joke. An ordinary person could cross into another plane, but could not remain there forever. After three days, an unknown force returned them to their home world, wherever they happened to be. The return happened on its own and depended neither on the distance between the planes nor on the person's wishes.

Only the characternyky were exempt from this restriction. They could remain in foreign planes for as long as necessary, move from one world to the next, and find their own way home. That unique ability had once allowed those whose powers had awakened far from the Zero Plane to spend years making their way back through dozens or even hundreds of worlds.

Scientists still did not know why the Universe made an exception for the characternyky. Nor did they know why only the characternyky could open portals on their own. Over many decades of research, humanity had learned to use many aspects of their abilities and had even created thoughtbands based on the study of their natural thought-link. But the true nature of the characternyky remained a mystery.

The transport platform was already visible ahead. The enormous open yard was crowded with containers of every size. Automatic cranes moved slowly above them, while dispatchers coordinated the constant flow of traffic.

The characternyky who had crossed through the portal behind the convoy were already waiting beside the cargo. Georges noticed their behavior almost immediately. Over the years of his service, he had grown used to seeing these people calm. They almost never showed anxiety, even when heading somewhere they might never return from. But this time, something was different.

All three were constantly scanning the platform. Their eyes moved across the containers, control towers, vehicles, and people. It was as if they were listening for something, or trying to notice something no one else had seen yet.

“They're acting strange today...” Viktor said quietly.

“That's true.”

“Think something's happened?”

Georges only shrugged. At that moment, one of the characternyky turned his gaze toward him. It lasted no more than a second, but that was enough. Georges saw neither fear nor confusion—only the intense concentration of someone who sensed that something was wrong around him but could not yet understand what it was.

Everything around them remained just as it had been a few minutes earlier. Cranes were working, cargo platforms rumbled, people laughed, talked about their jobs, and hurried about their business. And yet, deep inside Georges, an almost forgotten soldier's instinct had awakened—something that could not be explained by experience or logic.

And the longer he watched the characternyky, the stronger his conviction became: something was wrong.
