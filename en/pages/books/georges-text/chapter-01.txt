Georges woke to a brief pulse from his wristband.

“Unit assembly. Fifteen minutes. Sector Theta. Full combat readiness.”

The message vanished almost at once. He acknowledged it automatically, got up from the narrow military cot, and stood for a few seconds beside the small porthole in the barracks.

Beyond the thick armored glass, the surface of the Zero Plane drifted slowly past. Even from here, he could make out endless gray expanses covered in ash and ice. Life had once flourished down there, but the planet had never recovered from the Great Ash. Sunlight glimmered faintly across the frozen plains, where clouds of dust occasionally rose, while long bands of city-paths stretched across the surface like arteries. They had long since become the true home of most of humanity.

Georges kept his eyes on the planet for another moment. He knew the geologists’ calculations well. The Earth beneath them was slowly dying. The ancient war had left behind more than radiation and ash. The tectonic weapon used back then had disrupted the planet’s very balance, and now pressure was building relentlessly in its depths. Scientists no longer argued about whether the catastrophe would happen. They argued only about whether humanity had three hundred years to find a way to escape. That was why expeditions to other planes—sometimes called parallel worlds or other dimensions—had never been merely scientific missions. Every new ally, every new technology, every discovery could become another step toward saving all of humanity.

It had not always been this way.

After the Great Ash, humanity lost almost everything it had achieved over the previous centuries. For many years, the survivors had to think not about new discoveries, space exploration, or scientific progress, but about the bare essentials: warmth, food, safe shelter, and simply making it through another winter on a planet devastated by war.

Georges knew about those times mostly from his parents’ stories. They had both lived through the final months of the war and its aftermath in one of the damaged sections of a city-path. The central systems barely worked, communications with the other sections had long since gone silent, and supplies dwindled by the day. For months, people searched abandoned rooms for anything that could burn, repaired old water-purification systems, and shared what little food remained. No one knew whether anyone was still alive beyond their section.

His father used to say that, by the end, they had almost stopped expecting help. People simply tried to last one more day, then another, though everyone understood that the supplies would run out sooner or later. Each week grew colder. Equipment kept failing, and often there was nothing left to repair it with. For the first time in the history of civilization, people who had once been capable of building orbital stations and exploring space found themselves facing the simplest question of all: how not to die of hunger and cold.

Then, one day, a portal opened right in the middle of their section.

A young man stepped through—a man Georges’ parents had known before the war. Not a close friend or a relative, just a familiar face from a life that now seemed almost unreal. They had often seen him back then and could never have imagined that one day he would return to them from another world.

He was a characternyk.

Like so many others, the awakening of his power had cast him far from home, onto one of the foreign planes. He had been luckier than most: the Zero Plane turned out to be relatively close, and the young man had learned to control his ability and open portals fairly quickly. The others had been scattered much farther away—dozens, sometimes hundreds of planes from their home world. Some had died without ever finding their way back. Others had stayed in the worlds where they had landed. And some had spent years moving from one plane to another, gradually making their way home.

That young man was only one of those who had managed to return.

At first, he found several groups of survivors, then began searching for others. People gradually gathered around him, just as they did around other characternyky returning in different parts of the planet. Communication between isolated settlements was restored, routes opened across territories that had once been impossible to cross, and portals provided access to resources and knowledge that had almost entirely disappeared from the devastated Earth.

As the years passed, more and more characternyky returned. They came by different routes, bringing with them knowledge of the worlds they had visited. Their return became one of the main factors that helped scattered groups of survivors feel part of a single civilization again.

For Georges’ parents, it was much simpler than that. They remembered until the end of their lives the day a portal opened in the dark corridor of their half-dead city-path and a familiar young man stepped through. Until that moment, they had been almost certain their world would end with them. Afterward, for the first time in many months, they had hope that a future still existed beyond the walls of their section.

The revival of the Zero Plane took decades. Humanity gradually rebuilt what it had lost, restored and expanded the city-paths, returned to orbit, and recovered its ability to look toward the future. Contact with other planes accelerated the process, bringing access to new knowledge and technologies. But perhaps that was not even the greatest change. After years in which every group of survivors had thought only about getting through the next day, people began to believe again that their civilization would have a next day at all.

Now, many decades later, orbital stations once again operated above the devastated planet, city-paths stretched for thousands of kilometers, and people traveled to other planes. Yet the past had never disappeared. It remained in the ash-gray sky, the dead lands, and the calculations of scientists, which served as a reminder: humanity had been given time, but it had not yet been given salvation.

Georges turned away from the porthole and quickly put on his field uniform, fastened his body armor, and checked his weapon. His thoughtband, which had long since become an ordinary part of everyday life, still would not respond. The day before, the device had begun rebooting on its own before finally shutting down for good.

Thoughtbands had emerged from research into the natural thought-link of the characternyky. What was an inborn ability for them, scientists had spent many years trying to reproduce with technology and make available to ordinary people. Eventually, they succeeded. Transmitting information in short thought-packets gradually became as commonplace as voice communication once had been. Later, the bands were adapted to control equipment as well.

That was why the malfunction irritated Georges more than it should have. Without the band, he felt as though he had suddenly lost one of the familiar parts of his own body.

Finally giving up on his fruitless attempts to revive the device, Georges left the barracks. His unit was already gathering by the station’s internal transit line.

“About time, sleepyhead,” said Viktor with a grin. “We were starting to think we’d have to leave without you.”

“And who would carry your pack, then?” Georges shot back.

A few soldiers laughed. Jokes like that had long since become a familiar ritual before every assignment. The commander waited until everyone had taken their places in the transit car, then brought up the tactical display.

“Simple assignment. We’re to deliver a specialized scientific shipment from the transport depot to our allies on another plane. Characternyky will be waiting for us there. They’ll open a portal directly to the research complex. Once we’ve crossed over, we secure the cargo and escort it to the research team.”

“Another escort mission,” someone muttered behind them.

“Thank God for that,” Viktor said with a smile. “I’d rather spend a few hours walking alongside containers than spend a week chasing smugglers.”

The commander gave a faint smile.

“If everything goes smoothly, you’ll be home by evening.”

Georges was not expecting any surprises either. Missions like this had long since become a routine part of service. The allies constantly exchanged equipment, research findings, new materials, and experimental samples. Every civilization had its own achievements, and what was ordinary technology on one plane could open up an entirely new field of development on another.

Cooperation with the plane they were heading to had been going on for quite some time and had gradually grown into a genuine partnership. One of their most important joint projects had been the introduction of thoughtbands. The technology, developed on the Zero Plane through research into the characternyky’s thought-link, had been shared with their allies. Now almost the entire population of that world used the bands. The next step was to be the large-scale adaptation of equipment to respond to thought commands.

The train glided silently through the orbital station’s circular transit tunnel. Beyond the panoramic sections of the hull, enormous production modules, repair docks, warehouses, and hangars drifted past one after another. Everything around them operated without pause, like a single living organism on which the lives of millions of people on the planet’s surface depended.

A few minutes later, the transport stopped beside one of the station’s largest logistics sectors. The heavy airlock doors slowly slid apart, and Georges entered the enormous storage hangar with the rest of his unit.

Automatic cranes moved constantly beneath the high ceiling. Cargo platforms carried containers between dispatch zones, while technicians completed their final equipment checks. Almost in the center of the hangar stood the container they had been called in to escort.

Three characternyky were waiting silently beside it. As always, they had arrived before everyone else.

Even among hundreds of people, they were impossible to miss. Tall and composed, dressed in identical dark armor, they barely spoke to one another. Swords hung over their shoulders—blades that had long since become legends even among the military. People said their world-edge could cut through any material, from tank armor to the toughest alloys. Georges did not know how much truth there was in those stories, but he had once seen with his own eyes a characternyk cleave through the armored door of an emergency shelter, nearly half a meter thick, with a single strike.

Yet even without their swords, they remained the most dangerous warriors of the Zero Plane. Their speed, reflexes, and strength had become the stuff of countless stories, though the characternyky themselves never liked talking about their abilities.

One of them gave the commander a brief nod. That was enough. The portal was due to open in a few minutes.

“This time they’ve assigned three of them to escort us,” Viktor said quietly.

“Is the cargo really that important?”

“Or the route especially dangerous.”

Georges merely shrugged. He had long since stopped trying to understand the logic by which the characternyky assessed danger. If they thought it necessary to accompany the shipment themselves, they had their reasons.

One of the characternyky stepped forward and stopped in the open space between the containers. There were no devices or complicated mechanisms around him—only the smooth warehouse floor, usually crossed by forklifts.

Georges had always been amazed by how simple it was. Humanity built underground cities, orbital stations, and interplanetary ships, yet crossing between planes required only a single characternyk.

The warrior slowly closed his eyes.

At first, the air in front of him merely trembled, barely perceptibly, like heat haze. Then the distortion began to spread. Space seemed to lose its familiar depth, and suddenly light flowed in ways light was never supposed to behave.

Georges already knew what that meant.
