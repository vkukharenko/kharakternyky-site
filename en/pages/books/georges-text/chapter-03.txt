The unit quickly spread out across its assigned sectors, and within minutes the transport platform had returned to its usual rhythm. Armed soldiers had long been as ordinary a part of the complex workers’ day as the characternyks who occasionally escorted particularly important cargo. People went on with their work, barely paying attention to the guards. Beneath the cranes’ towering booms, automated platforms moved slowly; dispatchers checked shipping documents, while forklifts darted ceaselessly between the rows of containers, creating a steady industrial hum that soon faded into the background. Georges took up a position beside the outermost row of containers, from where he could see almost the entire platform. He checked his sector out of habit, automatically noting several places that could provide cover if needed, then leaned his shoulder against the cold metal. Over the years, he had learned not to stay tense without reason. When everything around you was orderly, there was no point imagining danger where none existed.

Yet this time, the sense of calm never came. Georges looked toward the characternyks again and saw their commander speaking with one of them. The conversation did not last long, but he noticed the commander ask the same question twice, as if hoping for a clearer answer. At last, the characternyk merely shook his head, and the commander returned to the unit.

“Spread out a little farther,” he ordered calmly. “And stay alert.”

“Is there some information?” one of the soldiers asked.

The commander paused for a moment.

“No. The characternyks just don’t like something.”

No one asked anything else. After years of service, they were all used to characternyks sometimes noticing things ordinary people could not sense. Even the characternyks themselves could not always explain what had caught their attention, but no one was willing to ignore such instincts.

Georges surveyed the platform once more. The sun had already climbed high, and the metal containers cast short shadows across the concrete. Near the dispatch tower, two workers were arguing animatedly about something, gesturing with their hands. Nearby, a young transport-platform driver was laughing at something someone had sent through his thought-link bracelet, while an elderly technician leisurely sipped coffee from a metal mug, watching an automated crane lower another container into place.

The scene was so ordinary that Georges began to feel awkward about his own vigilance. Perhaps the characternyks really had been mistaken this time?

The thought crossed his mind for only an instant.

Georges never quite understood what happened first.

For a fraction of a second, several people nearby froze almost simultaneously. The young driver stopped laughing mid-sentence. One of the workers by the dispatch tower lowered his hand without finishing the gesture, while the elderly technician slowly moved his mug away from his lips.

It lasted so briefly that Georges might not have noticed it at all.

Then a shot rang out.

The sound rolled across the platform so unexpectedly that, for the first few seconds, no one even understood what had happened. People looked around in confusion, trying to locate the danger. One of the guards by the far entrance slowly lowered his eyes to the dark stain spreading across his body armor. He seemed unable to believe what had happened. He took a step forward, tried to say something, but no words came. A moment later, his legs gave way and he fell heavily onto the concrete.

Georges was already raising his needle-gun, scanning the surrounding buildings. The first thought was obvious: an attack. Someone had infiltrated the complex or taken up a position on one of the rooftops. His eyes swept across windows, ventilation shafts, and observation platforms, but there was no sign of a shooter anywhere.

And just as Georges began to realize that something about the scene did not add up, a long burst of gunfire erupted nearby.

He spun around and froze for a moment, unable to believe his eyes.

One of the soldiers from his own unit was firing.

Only a few minutes earlier, they had checked the perimeter together, exchanged jokes, and discussed how long this latest assignment would take. Now the same man stood in the middle of the platform, calmly and almost expressionlessly shooting people he had served alongside for years.

Georges did not immediately realize that some of those falling to the concrete were his own comrades. One of them had only managed to turn toward the gunfire, as if to ask what was happening, before another burst cut short the movement—and his life.

“What are you doing?!” Georges shouted.

The soldier did not even turn his head. It was as if he could hear neither the cries around him, nor Georges’s voice, nor the shots now beginning to ring out from other parts of the platform. His face remained strangely calm, his movements precise and practiced. He changed the magazine without haste, as though he were on a firing range rather than in the middle of a transport complex where people he had been standing beside moments earlier were dying.

Georges had already lunged forward, intending to knock him down, when shots rang out to the left. Almost immediately, a burst answered from the opposite side of the platform, followed by another somewhere beyond the rows of containers.

Gunfire erupted in different parts of the complex, quickly turning the platform into utter chaos. Georges lost all sense of where the attack was coming from—and, more importantly, who was attacking whom.

He struggled to find some explanation for what he was seeing. Experience told him that every attack had its logic. There was always an enemy, a direction of assault, a goal for which blood was being spilled. Even the most brutal battle followed certain rules when viewed through a soldier’s eyes.

But here Georges could see no pattern for his mind to grasp. An armed guard suddenly shot his partner and immediately turned his weapon on a group of workers who had not even had time to understand what was happening. One driver steered a transport platform straight into a crowd, then jumped out of the cab and began shooting at those trying to get back to their feet. Nearby, two mechanics who had been arguing over a malfunctioning forklift just a minute earlier fell into a vicious fistfight, striking each other with heavy metal tools as if they had hated one another all their lives.

Not everyone behaved the same way. Some people were still trying to escape. They ran toward the buildings, hid between containers, helped the wounded, or simply stood motionless, unable to believe what was happening around them.

Georges saw a woman in work overalls dragging a wounded man toward the nearest shelter. She kept glancing back at the gunfire but stubbornly continued pulling him by the collar, trying to get him behind a concrete barrier.

Suddenly, the woman froze.

Only for a moment.

Her hand twitched toward the bracelet on her wrist, as if she had received a message. Then she slowly released the wounded man’s collar, straightened up, and looked around.

Georges did not even have time to wonder what had happened to her.

She bent down, picked up the weapon lying beside the body of a dead guard, and opened fire on the people running toward shelter.

Georges froze for only a second, but the sight burned itself into his memory. Just a moment earlier, that woman had been risking her life to save a stranger. Now she was just as determined to kill everyone nearby.

Through the chaos, he caught sight of the characternyks.

All three had already joined the fight, but even they could not stop what was happening around them. Someone a characternyk had just disarmed immediately lunged at the nearest person with his bare hands, while the person he had saved could grab someone else’s weapon and open fire a moment later.

With several almost imperceptible movements, one of the characternyks knocked weapons from the hands of two soldiers, shoved aside a man who had raised a metal pipe over a child, and was at another group of people an instant later. They moved with a speed Georges had witnessed only a handful of times in his life, but even that was not enough. It was impossible to protect people when any one of them could turn into an attacker the very next moment. Georges had never seen characternyks like this. They were used to fighting an enemy, protecting their own, and rescuing those in danger. But now even they could not tell whom they should protect—or from whom.

Georges could never remember how much time had passed since the first shot. Perhaps a few seconds, perhaps a minute. Later, he would return to those events in his thoughts again and again, but his memory always broke apart into fragments separated by empty gaps.

What he remembered most clearly was the feeling of utter helplessness. Everything he had been taught over many years of service had become useless. No regulations, no instructions, no combat experience could tell him what to do when the people around him began killing one another for no apparent reason.

Georges spotted one of his soldiers—a very young man who had joined the unit only a few months earlier. The boy dropped his needle-gun, clutched his head in both hands, and sank to his knees in the middle of the platform. He was shouting something, but his words were lost beneath the continuous thunder of gunfire.

A moment later, another soldier ran up and shot him in the back of the head at point-blank range. Then he calmly moved on, as if he had simply carried out an ordinary order.

That image would later remain in Georges’s memory more vividly than anything else. Not the death, not the blood, but the indifference with which people killed one another. They did not hesitate or stop. They showed neither hatred nor fear. Killing had become as natural a movement for them as talking, laughing, or taking a sip of morning coffee had been only minutes earlier.

That was when Georges became truly afraid for the first time. Not so much for his own life as because he realized he no longer understood the world he had found himself in.

He rushed toward the nearest wounded man, hoping at least to drag him out of the line of fire. The man lay on his side, pressing a hand against his bloodied shoulder. Just a second earlier, he had seemed barely aware of Georges.

Then his expression changed.

The man suddenly reached for the needle-gun lying beside the body of a dead soldier.

Georges saw the movement and lunged forward to knock the weapon away, but at that very moment something struck him hard in the head.

A blinding white light flashed before his eyes, almost immediately swallowed by darkness. Georges tried to stay on his feet, but his body no longer obeyed him. Through the dull ringing in his ears, he could still hear screams, gunshots, and the rumble of machinery, but all those sounds seemed to be receding, growing quieter and quieter. As he fell onto the cold concrete, Georges raised his eyes to the sky one last time. It was still as perfectly clear as it had been when he first stepped through the portal. Nothing in that endless blue gave any hint that people were dying below.

Then everything disappeared.
