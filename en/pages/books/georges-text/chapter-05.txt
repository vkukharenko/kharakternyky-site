The next morning, the transport hub was still empty. Georges woke to a distant rumble and lay motionless for a few seconds, listening. The sound did not come again, but he was fully awake now. He got up, checked his weapon, and left the dispatch center. Nothing had changed overnight. No one had come to help. Neither soldiers, nor rescue workers, nor characternyks had appeared. The dead still lay on the concrete, abandoned machinery stood between the containers, and an unfamiliar silence hung over the vast complex, broken only occasionally by the wind.

Georges walked to the edge of the transport hub and stopped beside the road leading into the city. The day before, cargo and passenger platforms had moved along it without interruption. Now the broad highway was almost deserted. Abandoned vehicles stood in the middle of the lanes; one had crashed into a barrier, while two others had collided at a nearby intersection. The city itself had changed, too. The bright towers that had glittered in the sunlight only yesterday were now partly hidden by smoke, and dark columns were rising above several districts. From time to time, a muffled rumble drifted from that direction. Georges could not identify its source from so far away, but he knew the sound too well to mistake it for anything else.

Military aircraft appeared over the city several times. They flew at high speed, changed course sharply, and vanished beyond the horizon. Georges watched them, trying to understand what was happening, but even here he could see no pattern. Once, two aircraft passed almost directly above one another. A few seconds later, a bright point flashed high in the sky. One of the machines began to fall, leaving a black trail behind it, while the other continued on as though nothing had happened.

Georges returned to the dispatch center. The day before, he had still considered making his way into the city to find the military, but after what he had seen, the idea no longer seemed sensible. He was alone, with a head injury, no thought-link, and almost no information about what was happening even a few kilometers away.

The worst part was that he still did not know whom he needed to protect himself from. Someone who had asked for help only seconds earlier might suddenly try to kill him, while a person who had just gunned down unarmed people might just as unexpectedly stop. Under such circumstances, a city of millions was the worst place he could go alone.

Besides, the characternyks knew where his unit had been stationed. If even one of them had survived, sooner or later they might return. Georges decided to wait. He used the time to search the transport hub and gather water, food, and anything else that might prove useful. He found a working first-aid kit and treated the wound on his head, then spent several hours trying to restore at least one communications channel to the military. None of them answered.

The information network, however, was still working, and through it Georges gradually realized that what he had witnessed on the transport platform was not an isolated incident. Reports of attacks were now coming from every continent. Military units were losing contact with command or suddenly attacking neighboring units, after which fighting broke out within their own ranks. Combat aircraft took off without orders and struck their own bases, air-defense systems shot down friendly aircraft, and in one metropolis dozens of passenger platforms accelerated to full speed simultaneously and crashed into the central stations.

Georges moved from one report to another, trying to assemble them into a coherent picture. He was looking for the same thing he had searched for from the first minute of the shooting on the platform: an enemy. A sabotage team, an unknown army, at least a direction from which the attack had begun. But the longer he watched, the clearer it became that there was no familiar logic to any of it. People attacked their own, then became victims of the same kinds of attacks themselves. A unit might capture a military base, only for its soldiers to start killing one another an hour later. The police tried to evacuate civilians until one of the officers suddenly opened fire on his own colleagues.

Governments were still trying to control the situation. Officials appeared on screens, declared states of emergency, urged people to remain indoors, and assured them that the military was restoring order. Georges soon stopped paying attention to these statements. Some broadcasts cut off in the middle of a speech, and an hour later came reports that the building from which the transmission had been made had been seized or destroyed. No one knew what was happening, yet everyone continued speaking as though an answer were just around the corner.

On the evening of the second day, Georges came across a recording from a security camera in one of the hospitals. At first, he barely paid attention. He had already seen dozens of scenes like it over the past day. A man had attacked medical staff right in the corridor and behaved just like the people on the transport platform: no shouting, no visible fury. It looked as though he were simply doing his job. Two orderlies tried to stop him. During the struggle, he struck his hand against a metal support, and the bracelet on his wrist shattered.

Georges was about to move on to the next report, but something made him keep watching. The man lay on the floor for several seconds, then slowly raised his head and looked around in confusion. One of the orderlies stepped back, expecting another attack, but the man made no further attempt to strike anyone. He looked at his own hands, then at the wounded medic in front of him. The expression on his face changed so abruptly that Georges instinctively leaned closer to the screen.

A moment later, the recording ended.

Georges tried to rewind the feed, but the system reported that the source had been lost. Only the final few seconds remained in local memory. He watched them twice more, following the man’s movements carefully. His gaze drifted to his own left wrist, where the broken bracelet hung—the one that had stopped working even before he crossed between planes.

The coincidence was strange, but he was not about to draw conclusions from a single recording. Georges did not know what had happened to the man in the hospital. He did not know whether the man’s behavior had really changed because his bracelet had been damaged. And, most importantly, he had no way to share his suspicions with anyone. He saved the recording in the dispatch system’s memory and returned to the information channels.

By the morning of the third day, the world beyond the transport hub had changed so much that Georges could barely recognize the place he had admired after stepping through the portal. An enormous cloud of smoke hung over the metropolis. Several districts were burning, and where passenger platforms had once glided silently between the bright towers, new columns of fire now rose from time to time. Explosions were becoming more frequent, and some were so powerful that the concrete beneath Georges’s feet trembled seconds later.

The information network was also falling apart. Channels disappeared one after another, communications with entire regions were severed, and automated cameras increasingly replaced professional broadcasts. On one of those channels, Georges saw a military column moving down the main street of an unidentified city. Tanks fired at buildings, infantry took up positions at intersections, but within minutes one vehicle turned its turret and opened fire on the one ahead of it. Almost immediately, the rest of the column began firing in every direction, and the broadcast vanished behind a cloud of dust.

Georges was staring at the blank screen when he heard a new sound somewhere in the distance. This time it was neither a brief explosion nor the rumble of battle. A low roar quickly grew louder, making the windows of the dispatch center vibrate. He stood up and went outside, trying to determine where it was coming from.

The metropolis on the horizon was almost completely hidden behind smoke. Georges stared for several seconds at the dark line of buildings when a blinding white light suddenly flashed above one of the distant districts. He instinctively turned away and shielded his face with one hand, but even through his closed eyelids he could see the glare. When he looked again, a fireball was already rising above the city, rapidly expanding and engulfing the surrounding districts in dust and smoke.

He knew at once what he had seen.

Before Georges could grasp the scale of the first strike, a second flash erupted almost in the very center of the metropolis. The black silhouettes of high-rise buildings appeared for an instant against the blinding light, then vanished beneath an enormous cloud. Another explosion occurred much farther away, beyond the city, and Georges could no longer tell whether it had struck a military base or another settlement.

He ran for the dispatch center. The shock wave caught him when he was still several dozen meters from the entrance. First came a rumble that turned in an instant into a deafening roar. Then the air struck with such force that Georges lost his footing. He was thrown onto the concrete as fragments of glass and metal flew over his head and the enormous structures of the transport hub groaned under the pressure of the blast.

For several seconds, Georges lay with his arms over his head. When the noise subsided a little, he got up and looked around. Part of the dispatch center’s panoramic glazing had been blown out, several lightweight structures had collapsed, and one of the empty transport vehicles had overturned on the platform. But the complex itself had held.

The city on the horizon was gone. Several enormous clouds rose above it, slowly expanding and merging with the black smoke from the fires. Georges was still staring in that direction when another flash appeared far to the right. This time, he did not even flinch.

Back in the dispatch center, Georges switched on every channel that was still available. Reports came in fragments. Strategic weapons were being used in different parts of the planet, but no one could explain who was issuing the orders. Missiles were launching from military installations and striking cities belonging to the very same states. Defense systems tried to shoot down their own missiles; others opened fire on the very targets they were supposed to protect. Some command centers had stopped responding, and where communications still existed, the orders contradicted one another.

Georges no longer searched the map for a front line. For three days he had failed to find an enemy, even though he had spent the entire time behaving as if there had to be an army somewhere behind the attack. Now, looking at the nuclear strikes appearing one after another on the planetary map, he allowed himself for the first time to consider that whoever had started all this might not have been seeking victory.

If someone really stood behind the catastrophe, they did not care who won. All that mattered was that fewer people remained.

Georges did not know where the thought had come from, but he could no longer get rid of it.

A few hours later, the last information channel disappeared. The screen tried to restore its connection for a while, then went dark. Georges was left alone in the half-ruined dispatch center, with a world burning beyond its windows—a world that had seemed almost perfect only three days earlier.

There was no point in waiting any longer. The characternyks had not returned, and Georges did not know whether even one of them was still alive. He checked his weapon, gathered water and whatever food he could find, then decided to inspect the transport hub. If the forced return did not begin soon, he would have to find a safer place.

Near one of the abandoned service electric vehicles, he noticed a small child’s backpack. Georges had already walked past it, but after a few steps he stopped and turned back. Inside were a bottle of water, a wrapped sandwich, and a small model spaceship made from construction-kit pieces.

He picked up the little ship and immediately remembered the morning of the first day, when he and Viktor had walked along this same road. Back then, an orbital elevator towered above the city, and a new station glittered high in the sky. The locals had completed its construction only recently. Viktor had pointed toward it and told him about preparations for the first expedition to Mars. Georges had thought about how far this civilization had come on its way to the stars.

Now the child’s spaceship lay in his palm, while the city on the horizon burned after several nuclear strikes.

Georges put the toy back into the backpack, and at that very moment he felt pain. It began deep in his chest and quickly spread through his body. He braced one hand against the body of the electric vehicle and tried to breathe, but the pain only intensified. For an instant he thought he had been wounded, but almost immediately he understood the real reason.

The third day had passed.

Everyone who traveled to another plane was warned about the forced return. A person could remain in a foreign world only for a limited time. After that, unless a characternyk brought them back, the connection with their home plane began pulling them back on its own. Georges knew it would hurt, but no story could have prepared him for the sensation that his very body was being torn apart and dragged through space.

He dropped to one knee, struggling not to lose consciousness. The containers around him began to blur, the outlines of buildings doubled, and the sounds grew muffled and distant. Georges knew he had very little time left. Almost without thinking, he raised his head toward the sky, trying to see one last time the world he had admired only three days earlier.

Through the smoke above the destroyed metropolis, the thin silver thread of the orbital elevator was still visible. Georges followed it upward with his eyes and found a small bright dot in the blue. He recognized the station almost immediately. It was the one Viktor had pointed out, delighted that the locals had finally completed its construction.

Georges was watching it when a brief flash suddenly appeared nearby. At first, he thought sunlight had reflected off the hull of some spacecraft, but the glow quickly grew brighter. A few seconds later, a brilliant sphere blossomed where the orbital station had been, and hundreds of fragments began flying away in every direction.

Georges forgot even about the pain.

He watched the station break apart before his eyes. Part of the structure vanished in the flash, while other fragments continued along their orbits, leaving bright trails behind them. The thin line of the orbital elevator remained in place for a little longer, but then it began to bend slowly, losing tension.

Only three days earlier, Georges had stood here with Viktor and looked out at this same world. Back then, cranes had been working all around them, transport platforms had moved along the road, children had been laughing somewhere beyond the fence, and the newly completed station had hung in the sky. Now the metropolis on the horizon was burning, nuclear clouds were rising above it, and the fragments of a station that was meant to be another step in this civilization’s journey to the stars were slowly scattering across orbit.

The space around Georges began to lose its shape. The pain became so intense that he could barely feel his own body, yet he kept looking upward until the sky before his eyes dissolved completely into light.

The last thing he saw in that world was neither the war nor the enemy he had searched for unsuccessfully throughout all three days. What remained before his eyes was a clear blue sky, and against it, the burning fragments of an orbital station that the people of this world had been so proud of only a short time before.
