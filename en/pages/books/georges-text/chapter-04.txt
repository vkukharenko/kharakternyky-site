Consciousness returned slowly, as though Georges were rising from a great depth, unable to tell where a heavy dream ended and reality began. First came the pain—a dull, heavy ache concentrated at the back of his head, so intense that every heartbeat seemed to pound against his skull. Then he felt the cold concrete beneath his cheek and smelled dust, hot metal, and blood. Only then did he dare to open his eyes.

For a while, Georges simply lay there, trying to remember what had happened. The sun, which had been almost overhead when the shooting began, had now sunk toward the horizon, and long shadows from the containers stretched across the transport platform in dark bands. He must have been unconscious for several hours. Georges carefully pressed a palm against the concrete and tried to get up, but the world immediately tilted. Dark spots swam before his eyes, and nausea rose in his throat. He had to remain still for a few seconds, waiting for the dizziness to ease. His memory returned along with his awareness. The first shot, which no one had taken seriously. A soldier from his unit suddenly opening fire on his own comrades. People attacking one another for no apparent reason. The characternyks trying to stop a battle in which there was neither an enemy nor a front line. Last of all, Georges remembered the wounded man he had rushed to help. The man had raised his weapon, a shot had rung out, and then something heavy had struck Georges in the head.

He slowly sat up and touched the back of his head. His fingers found dried blood, but the wound itself no longer seemed to be bleeding. Something else troubled him far more: the silence around him was almost complete. After the chaos that had filled this place before he lost consciousness, it felt so unnatural that Georges instinctively listened for even a distant gunshot or a human voice. But the transport hub was silent. Automated cranes stood frozen above the containers, cargo platforms no longer moved, and the familiar industrial hum that had seemed inseparable from this place that morning had vanished.

When Georges finally managed to stand, an entirely different scene lay before him. Bodies, abandoned weapons, scattered tools, and personal belongings covered the concrete. Near the far entrance, several cargo platforms had collided. One had overturned onto its side, blocking part of the road. A large container hung motionless beneath a crane boom, never having been lowered to the ground. It looked as though the entire vast complex had stopped in a single instant, after which everyone had simply abandoned it.

Georges approached the nearest soldier and crouched beside him. He recognized the man as one of the first to open fire on his comrades. Now he lay motionless, a needle-gun beside his hand. His face showed neither fear nor pain, nor the fury Georges would have expected to see in someone who had been mercilessly killing men he had served with for years only a few hours earlier. His expression was almost peaceful, which made the sight even more chilling.

After checking for a pulse and finding none, Georges moved to the next body, then another. There were no survivors among those he found nearby. But soon he noticed something strange: there were far fewer dead than there had been people on the platform before the shooting began. Hundreds had worked here, not counting the guards and his own unit, yet most of the transport hub was now empty. Georges did not know where the others had gone. Perhaps they had managed to escape. Perhaps they had hidden in the surrounding buildings or made their way into the city. After what he had seen, he did not want to consider another possibility—that those who had survived here had simply carried the madness elsewhere.

Then he remembered the characternyks.

Georges began examining the bodies more carefully, moving from one to another in search of the familiar dark armor, but he found none of the three. The characternyks had disappeared. Even before the shooting began, they had behaved as though they sensed danger approaching. Once the chaos erupted, Georges had seen them trying to stop people and pull the wounded to safety. If they had survived, they might bring help—or at least understand what had happened. Georges clung to that thought, though he did not know how realistic it was. He instinctively raised his left hand to contact his commander, only to remember his own bracelet. The device that had refused to switch on that morning was now completely destroyed. Its protective casing had split in two, several internal components had turned black, and thin wires protruded from beneath the shattered panel. Georges tried several times to initiate an emergency recovery, though he knew perfectly well it was pointless, then lowered his hand in frustration.

Only a few hours earlier, the malfunctioning bracelet had seemed like a minor inconvenience, something he would have to spend time repairing after he returned. Now Georges was without his thought-link, without access to the information network, and unable to find out what was happening even a few kilometers away. He had grown so accustomed to the technology’s constant presence in everyday life that only now did he realize how helpless a person could become when all the information they were used to receiving almost instantly suddenly became unavailable.

After checking his needle-gun and collecting several spare magazines, Georges headed for the dispatch center. The building still had power, although some of its equipment had switched to emergency mode. Messages kept appearing on the monitors: transport lines halted, collisions, automated systems losing control, and communications with individual districts going down. Georges tried to contact local security, then the military. When neither responded, he switched to the medical service. This time the connection went through, but instead of an operator he heard only heavy breathing, a distant scream, and some indistinct rumbling. A few seconds later, the channel went dead.

On one of the adjacent screens, the thought-link network monitoring system was still open. Georges was no specialist and understood almost nothing of the technical indicators changing constantly before him, but even he could see that the network was still operating. Millions of devices remained connected, and the volume of transmitted data was so high that the counters changed faster than he could read them.

For a few seconds, Georges stared at the screen, trying to understand whether there was anything unusual about it. Perhaps people were trying to contact their families en masse. Perhaps emergency services were sending warnings and the military was trying to coordinate its actions. In any case, the information told him nothing, so he turned his attention to the news channels, hoping at last to find some explanation.

The news was worse than any of his assumptions.

Reports of attacks were coming in from several cities at once. The police were trying to restore order, but some units suddenly stopped following orders and opened fire on their own colleagues. Soldiers at one military base began shooting one another. In another region, several passenger platforms were deliberately steered toward each other. Reports came in of air crashes, power-plant accidents, and transport systems shutting down, but nowhere was there even a mention of who might be behind it all. That was the hardest thing for Georges to understand. He had spent his entire life among the military and was used to every conflict having at least some internal logic. There was an enemy, a goal, a direction of attack, orders, and people carrying them out. Even in the most brutal war, one could understand who was fighting whom and what they were trying to achieve. There was nothing like that here. No one had declared war. No state had reported an invasion. No unknown army had crossed any borders. People had simply begun killing one another, and with every passing hour the madness spread across more and more of the planet.

By evening, reports were arriving from every continent. In some places, military units were still trying to defend cities, only to fall apart within hours when some of their soldiers suddenly opened fire on their comrades. Police officers who had just been pulling people out of dangerous districts abruptly turned their weapons on those they had been trying to save. Dispatchers directed vehicles into collisions, pilots changed course without explanation, and automated systems received contradictory commands from people who had been acting perfectly normally only minutes earlier.

Georges sat in front of the screens until deep into the night, considering one explanation after another. Sabotage, an unknown weapon, poisoning, disease—something had to be behind what was happening. Yet none of the theories explained the most terrifying part: the people he had seen on the transport platform did not look insane. They did not scream with rage, lose the ability to use weapons, or act chaotically. On the contrary, many moved calmly and methodically, as if they knew exactly what they were supposed to do. It was only the purpose of their actions that Georges could not understand.

Before trying to sleep, he glanced once more at the thought-link network monitoring screen. Millions of bracelets remained connected, and the flow of information had not diminished.
