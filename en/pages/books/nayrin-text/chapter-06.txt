She ran as though death itself were already at her heels.

The cold morning air cut into her lungs. Every breath burned in her chest, and her heart pounded so violently that its beats echoed in her temples. The cobblestones, slick after the night’s rain, kept slipping beneath her feet, making her lose her balance, while her heavy dress tangled around her legs as though it too were trying to stop her escape. Marta had long since stopped fighting it. She ran without thinking about fatigue or pain, because she knew that if she slowed down, there would be no second chance.

Behind her, the familiar pounding of heavy boots grew clearer. Metal buckles clinked in time with the footsteps, fragments of brief commands were lost in the echoes of the narrow streets, and passers-by hurriedly pressed themselves against the walls, making way for the armed gendarmes. Her pursuers were no longer trying to hide the chase. They no longer cared about accidental witnesses or the eyes of strangers. They could feel the net almost closing, and were certain that the exhausted girl would not escape.

Marta abruptly turned into a side alley, hoping to gain at least a few precious seconds. The stone walls of the old houses immediately shut out the noise of the main street, and the world narrowed to a strip of cobblestones between gloomy façades. Here, her own breathing sounded especially loud, while the pounding of dozens of feet behind her echoed again and again off the stone. The alley curved gently twice, and Marta had already begun to believe that another passage would open ahead when, beyond the next turn, she found only a tall brick wall.

Carried forward by her momentum, she took a few more steps, braced her palms against the cold masonry, and froze. There was no way through.

When Marta spun around, the gendarmes were already entering the alley. They were no longer running. Breathless after the long pursuit, the men paused for a few seconds to catch their breath. Then their commander gave a short signal with his hand, ordering them to spread out in a semicircle. No one hurried to draw a weapon. Before them stood an exhausted girl, cut off from every possible escape route, and even the youngest among them understood perfectly well that everything now depended on their commander’s decision.

The commander advanced at an unhurried pace. A burly man with a thick grey moustache studied Marta carefully, as though trying to understand why an ordinary-looking girl had been slipping away from the men he considered the finest in his unit for so many weeks. There was neither anger nor impatience on his face. Only the cold confidence of someone accustomed to seeing every task through to the end.

At that very moment, an unfamiliar voice sounded calmly behind him.

“It seems she won’t be going with you today.”

The gendarmes turned almost in unison. Two young men stood at the entrance to the alley. Their clothes were much like those of other travellers; worn road bags hung over their shoulders, and long swords in plain scabbards were visible at their belts. They did not draw their weapons, assume fighting stances, or try to look dangerous. They simply watched the men who had just trapped the girl.

Marta froze. She recognised the mind-link she had been sensing for a long time, making her stop in her tracks with the hope that someone else from her former life might exist in this world. It was because she sensed this mind-link that she had done something she had forbidden herself to do during all her years of survival in this world of steam engines and gendarmes. She had not merely entered the city; she had dared to remain there for weeks, constantly hiding from the hounds of the law.

The commander took another step.

“This is a matter of state,” he said coldly. “If you don’t want trouble, go on your way.”

Lior gave a faint smile.

“We can’t. We’ve been looking for her for too long.”

The commander did not answer. He merely gave one of his men the slightest nod.

The gendarme moved forward calmly and confidently, as though all he had to do was push an ordinary passer-by out of his way. He did not even manage to touch Lior. The young man shifted aside with ease, caught the outstretched arm, and used the attacker’s own momentum to throw him smoothly over his hip. The man hit the cobblestones hard and opened his mouth helplessly, struggling to catch his breath.

At the same moment, a second gendarme charged at Nayrin. He did not even step back. He merely turned his body slightly and met the attacker with his shoulder. The blow was so powerful that the man flew backwards and knocked down a companion who was rushing to help him. A third tried to circle behind Nayrin, but the young man sensed his approach before he heard the footsteps. He spun sharply, caught the attacker’s arm, broke his balance, and shoved him against the brick wall. The stone gave a dull thud, and the gendarme sank helplessly to the ground.

The whole clash lasted only a few moments, but that was enough for the gendarmes to understand one thing. These were no ordinary skilled travellers. The two young men moved as though they could see an attack before it even began. When two men charged Lior at the same time, he simply took a small step aside, allowing them to collide with each other, then struck both with two precise palm blows, taking them out of the fight.

The commander quickly surveyed his men. Some were already struggling to their feet; others were still sitting on the wet cobblestones, trying to recover from the blows. For the first time during the pursuit, he felt the situation slipping out of control. These two were not men who could be detained by ordinary force, and every passing moment only increased the cost of this confrontation.

Meanwhile, Nayrin had already approached Marta. She was still standing by the brick wall, her eyes fixed on him. The fear, exhaustion, and tension of the past weeks had not disappeared, but another feeling was beginning to break through them: relief, something she had long since stopped expecting.

Lior stopped beside them.

“We found you. Come on. There’s nothing left for us here.”

He held out his hand, and the air beside the wall shivered softly, as though an invisible wave had passed through space itself. Within seconds, a narrow dark crack opened in it, quickly widening into a passage leading to a dense forest flooded with sunlight.

Marta glanced back only once. The gendarmes were already getting to their feet again, but now their eyes held far more confusion than confidence. She hesitated no longer. Gripping Lior’s hand, she stepped through the dark passage. Nayrin entered last, and as soon as his figure vanished on the other side, the opening closed soundlessly, leaving behind only the old brick wall.

When the commander ran up to the place where the three strangers had stood only moments earlier, all he found was wet cobblestone, cold stone, and dead silence. He slowly ran his palm over the rough masonry, as though hoping to find some trace, but the wall remained ordinary. Nothing suggested that people had just vanished without a trace right before his eyes. It was the first defeat for which he could find no explanation.

As soon as Nayrin stepped through the dark opening, the portal closed soundlessly behind him, as though it had never existed. Only a moment earlier, the heavy boots of their pursuers had thundered around them, equipment had rattled, and fragments of commands and shouts had filled the air. Now it had all dissolved into the quiet rustle of leaves. The crowns of old trees swayed slowly overhead, birds called to one another nearby, and a light breeze carried the smell of damp earth, moss, and young grass.

Marta ran on for several more steps, not noticing that the danger had already been left on the other side of the portal. Her legs moved on their own, obeying not her mind but the fear that had driven her forward for weeks. Only when her strength was completely spent did she brace a hand against the rough trunk of an old oak and slowly sink onto its roots, covered with last year’s leaves. Her chest heaved with every breath, but for the first time in a long while she did not need to look over her shoulder, expecting to see armed gendarmes behind her.

Lior and Nayrin did not hurry to break the silence. They had not yet fully recovered from the brief but exhausting fight themselves. Standing nearby, the young men silently caught their breath, listening to the unusual quiet of the forest, which seemed almost unnatural after the clash of weapons.

At last Marta raised her head.

“Where are we now?… And how did you do that?”

Nayrin gave a faint smile.

“To be honest, we don’t understand everything ourselves yet. But I know one thing for certain: you’ll be able to open portals too. I’ll show you how…”

He had managed to say only a few words when Marta gently raised her hand to stop him.

“Don’t explain it aloud. Send it to me in a thought-packet. It’ll be much faster.”

Nayrin exchanged a surprised glance with Lior. Both looked at the girl as though she had just started speaking an unfamiliar language.

Seeing their reaction, Marta smiled kindly.

“Now I understand why you stayed silent all this time, even though I kept sensing your presence. You don’t know how to transmit thoughts yet. You were only unconsciously broadcasting your feelings. It seems I have something to teach you as well.”

She slowly stood up, came almost right up to Nayrin, and looked calmly into his eyes.

“Don’t strain yourself. Quite the opposite. Just let your thoughts grow quiet. Don’t try to hear anything or imagine anything. Simply stop holding on to your own thoughts.”

Nayrin wanted to ask what exactly she meant, but the words never left his lips.

The world around him did not disappear. He could still hear the rustling leaves, smell the forest, and see Marta standing before him. Yet something entirely different burst into his consciousness at the same time. Not individual words or fragments of memories, but an entire stream of life that could not be divided into separate parts.

Within a few seconds, he saw more than a person could tell in many days. The first hours after she had arrived in this world flashed before him: her fear and confusion, her meeting with a family of revolutionaries who had given shelter to a strange girl, the endless months of learning, escape, and struggle. He felt her despair when she realised she was alone, her stubborn determination as she learned to live among strangers, and the day she accidentally discovered that her body could move faster than the human eye could follow.

Night raids flashed before him, along with explosions at warehouses, attacks on government convoys, and the liberation of arrested rebels. He saw how this seemingly fragile girl had repeatedly risked her own life, undermining the power that had turned its people into obedient slaves. Years of struggle passed before him in a matter of moments, yet he felt every emotion she had experienced almost as sharply as if it had all happened to him.

When the stream of images ended, Nayrin slowly turned his gaze towards Lior. He stood motionless, looking somewhere through the trees, with the same expression of astonishment frozen on his face. It was obvious that he had just experienced the same thing.

Marta smiled.

“If you had known how to send thought-packets from the very beginning, I would have found you much sooner. I wouldn’t have had to wander blindly, relying only on the feeling of your presence. And most likely, the gendarmes would never have caught up with me.”

There was neither reproach nor regret in her voice. She was simply stating an obvious fact.

“Now you try. Send me what you wanted to explain in words.”

Lior managed it first. He closed his eyes for only a few seconds, after which Marta gave a barely perceptible nod. Nayrin succeeded a minute later. Only moments ago, they had not even suspected such a thing was possible. Now they were sitting beneath an old oak, exchanging entire layers of knowledge, memories, and feelings without saying a word. Ordinary human speech suddenly seemed slow and clumsy. What would have taken hours to explain in words could now be conveyed in a few seconds.

About half an hour passed before Lior finally spoke aloud.

“Could you do this from the very beginning?”

The smile slowly faded from Marta’s face.

“No. My friend taught me.”

“Is she one of us?”

“Yes. Her name is Olena. We met after arriving in this world. Just like you, we searched for each other for a long time, until we realised that we could do more than sense one another’s presence—we could also communicate through our thoughts.”

She closed her eyes for just a moment.

“She’s alive. Very far away. But I can feel her right now.”

The forest fell silent again. Lior looked at Nayrin, and he needed no explanation.

“If she can send us her exact location…”

“…we’ll open a portal straight to her,” Nayrin finished calmly.

Marta looked carefully at them both.

“You want to return to that same plane?”

“We do,” Lior answered. “There are already too few of us to leave anyone alone in a strange world.”

Marta nodded silently.

The three of them stood together. She closed her eyes and focused on Olena’s distant presence. A moment later, indistinct outlines of another place began to emerge in all three minds: smells, sounds, fragments of a landscape. It was not enough to see everything clearly, but it was quite enough to understand the most important thing—they now knew where they had to go.

Lior slowly stretched out his hand. The air between the trees trembled almost imperceptibly, and a new portal began to open silently in the middle of the forest. This was no longer a portal opening at random or in desperation. It was the first portal opened deliberately, with a clear destination in mind.
