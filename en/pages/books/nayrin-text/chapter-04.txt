The journey was already in its second month. Nayrin had passed through dozens of villages and several small towns, and had crossed two wide rivers, hiring himself out to boatmen as an ordinary labourer.

During that time, he had seen far more than in the previous five years he had spent in Boren’s village. Every land had its own customs, dialect, rulers, and wars. Yet the farther east he travelled, the more often he noticed one strange pattern. The invisible sensation that had once seemed barely perceptible now arose almost every day. It no longer felt like random flashes of someone else’s emotions. Sometimes Nayrin felt as though a thin thread stretched between him and an unknown person. It was impossible to see, but he could sense it if he stopped and listened carefully to himself.

One cool morning, the road brought him to a high hill, from which he could see a large city. It was several times bigger than any settlement Nayrin had seen in this world. Stone walls enclosed dozens of districts; the spires of temples and the towers of a castle rose above the rooftops. Carts, riders, and travellers on foot streamed continuously towards the main gate. Life swirled before the entrance. Merchants argued loudly over the prices of their goods, shepherds drove flocks of sheep, craftsmen displayed their wares in the open air, and the city guards carefully checked everyone entering.

Nayrin joined the crowd, trying not to attract unnecessary attention. During his years among the villagers, he had learned to dress in a way that did not make him stand out. Now only the sword on his back hinted that he was not an ordinary traveller. The guards let him through without asking questions, and within minutes he found himself among narrow stone streets, where the smells of fresh bread, smoke, horses, and crowds made his head spin.

That was where it happened.

At first, Nayrin thought someone had simply bumped into him in the crowd. He even turned, looking for the person whose shoulder might have struck him carelessly, but only ordinary people, busy with their own affairs, were passing nearby. Yet the sensation did not disappear. It struck him a second time, far more strongly than ever before.

Someone’s consciousness was very close. Not hundreds of kilometres away or beyond the horizon, but here, in this very city.

Nayrin froze in the middle of the street. His heart began beating so fast that he could no longer hear the noise around him. The inexplicable call that had led him through forests, rivers, and roads for two months had suddenly become an almost tangible presence. It was so close that he could nearly feel the other person’s unease physically.

He slowly moved forward, not even understanding why he had chosen that direction. At the first crossroads, the sensation weakened. Nayrin immediately turned back, and it grew stronger again. He repeated this several times until he was certain he was not mistaken. The invisible connection really was showing him the way.

The streets grew narrower. The market stalls were left behind, and the bustle of the great marketplace gradually faded. This was the craftsmen’s quarter. The tapping of hammers came from open workshop doors; the smell of freshly worked wood mingled with leather and hot wax. Nayrin walked slowly, carefully studying the faces of passers-by, though he did not know whom he was looking for.

And then he saw him. A young man of about twenty-five was standing beside a well, drawing water. He was dressed like everyone else around him, but his manner was different. His gaze kept moving over the people, rooftops, and windows, as though he were used to constantly expecting danger. At that very moment, the stranger suddenly raised his head and looked straight at Nayrin.

They both froze. No more than thirty paces separated them. Neither said a word. Yet each already knew that he had found the one he had been searching for.

The stranger slowly set the bucket down on the stones without taking his eyes off Nayrin. Distrust, surprise, and hope passed across his face—the very same feelings Nayrin was experiencing. They began walking slowly towards each other, paying no attention to the people passing between them.

When only a few steps remained between them, the stranger spoke quietly in a language Nayrin had not heard for many years:

“You… are you from there?”

Nayrin felt something tighten painfully inside him. His native language. The first familiar words he had heard in so many years. He could not answer at once. He only nodded, then managed to say hoarsely:

“Yes… I’m from the European continent.”

The young man closed his eyes and let out a deep breath, as though he had been holding it all this time.

“So am I… from the European continent.”

He spoke quietly, but to Nayrin those words sounded louder than the noise of the entire city. Over the past five years, he had grown used to hearing another language. At first, it had grated on his ears and forced him to listen carefully to every unfamiliar word so he would not miss its meaning. Then it had become familiar, and eventually it had almost pushed out of his daily life the language he had once spoken with his brother. And now a few simple words had brought back everything that, he thought, had long ago been left on the other side of his life.

He did not answer immediately. He simply looked at the stranger, as though afraid that if he blinked, the man would dissolve into the city crowd, leaving behind only the painful memory of yet another hope that had come to nothing.

Nothing around them had changed. The square before the market was still bustling with life. Merchants argued with customers, teenagers rolled wooden barrels towards the warehouses, women drew water by the well, and the blacksmith’s workshop nearby kept sounding with the dull blows of a hammer against an anvil.

The great city continued its measured daily life, and no one suspected that, for the two people standing beside the old stone well, this moment had become more important than all the years that had come before it.

“So that means…” Nayrin finally smiled, though he could feel his voice trembling slightly with emotion. “I was beginning to think I’d gone mad. That everything I remembered had simply been a dream after some fever.”

The stranger gave a quiet laugh. It was brief and somewhat awkward, as though he had long since forgotten how to laugh.

“I thought so too. During the first year, I was afraid even to speak aloud in my native language. I thought that if I heard my own voice, I wouldn’t be able to bear it. Later, I caught myself forgetting individual words. I had to make a deliberate effort to remember the names of things, people, cities… As though I were afraid of losing the last connection to home.”

He fell silent and ran his palm over the rough stone of the well.

“It’s strange… I don’t even know your name, but right now I feel as though I’ve met someone from my own family.”

“Nayrin.”

The young man immediately held out his hand.

“Lior.”

Their palms clasped firmly.

It was an ordinary handshake between two men, but for both of them it meant much more. Over the years, they had met people, worked alongside them, shared meals, fought together, or traded with them. Yet none of those people could understand what a Path City was, why they had travelled across continents by monorail as children, or why the words “nuclear winter” still made their hearts tighten.

Now each of them stood before someone who needed none of it explained.

“Were you transported on the day of the strike too?” Lior asked quietly.

Nayrin simply nodded.

“My brother and I were running towards the evacuation sector. After that… I remember nothing. I woke up in a forest.”

Lior looked at him carefully.

“Your younger brother?”

“Yes.”

“Have you found him?”

Nayrin lowered his eyes. There was no need to answer. Lior slowly nodded, as though he understood everything the other man wanted to say without words.

“My parents were still alive when I left. I still don’t know what happened to them. For almost a year, I convinced myself nearly every day that it would only be a little longer before I found my way back. Then I began to realise that I didn’t even know where ‘back’ was.”

A flock of pigeons rose from the roof of the town hall and flew overhead. For a moment, the birds blocked the sun, and their shadows raced across the cobblestones of the square. Lior followed them absently with his eyes, but then his gaze suddenly fixed on something else entirely.

His fingers, which had been resting calmly on the edge of the well a moment earlier, slowly tightened. Nayrin noticed almost immediately.

“What happened?”

Lior did not answer. He was looking over the heads of the crowd towards the main street. His expression did not change abruptly. He did not turn pale or twist with fear. It was simply that the brief relief brought by meeting someone from his own world gradually vanished from his face. In its place appeared the familiar look of someone who had spent far too long looking over his shoulder.

Nayrin turned instinctively. At first, he saw nothing unusual. Dozens of people were passing by. Someone carried a sack of grain on his shoulder; two apprentices were rolling a heavy barrel towards a wine shop; an elderly woman was arguing with a fruit seller over the weight of her apples. But when his gaze moved a little farther, it lingered on four men slowly making their way through the crowd.

They did nothing to attract attention, and that was what made them most unsettling. Long black cloaks without a single ornament, plain scabbards, identical gaits, identical postures. They did not call to one another, gesture, or try to push people aside. The crowd simply parted before them, though no one seemed to realise why they were stepping out of the way.
