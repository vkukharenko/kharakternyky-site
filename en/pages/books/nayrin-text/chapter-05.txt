Lior looked away and spoke quietly.

“We can’t stay here any longer.”

He said it as calmly as if he were suggesting they cross to the other side of the square, but Nayrin heard in his voice the weariness of someone who had begun the same escape many times before.

“Who are they?”

Lior was silent for a few more seconds. Then he gave a bitter smile.

“If we’re lucky enough to stay alive today, I’ll tell you everything I know on the way. But right now, do me one favour. Don’t look back again. And whatever happens next, just stay close.”

He pushed away from the stone rim of the well, adjusted the belt holding his sword, and set off at an ordinary pace along the market stalls, as though he were one of the hundreds of travellers who had come to the city market looking for goods. Nayrin silently walked beside him, though he could feel tension slowly building inside him. He still did not know who the people in black were, but he had already made up his mind: his meeting with Lior had lasted far too briefly, and he would not let anyone end it so easily.

Lior walked quickly, but not so quickly as to attract attention. From the outside, he was no different from dozens of other travellers hurrying through the market on their own errands. Only Nayrin, walking beside him, could see the muscles in his neck twitch almost imperceptibly whenever a loud shout or the clink of metal came from the crowd. It was the habit of someone who had lived too long expecting an attack and no longer allowed himself to relax, even for a moment.

They turned between the market stalls, where fabrics, leather, and forged goods were being sold beneath awnings. It was more crowded here than in the main square. Customers bargained and examined the merchandise, shopkeepers praised their own craftsmanship, and children ran laughing between the adults.

Nayrin noticed that Lior deliberately chose the busiest places. Several times he changed direction as though merely interested in the stalls, paused briefly to let loaded carts pass, and then continued. It seemed he was not trying to flee so much as disappear into other people’s lives, becoming just one of countless unfamiliar faces.

“Have you been running from them for long?” Nayrin asked quietly, pretending to examine the knives on a blacksmith’s stall.

Lior did not answer immediately. He waited until a group of peasants carrying sacks of grain had passed, then gave the faintest shake of his head.

“So long that I’ve stopped counting the years. At first, I thought it was a coincidence. Then I kept telling myself I was imagining it all. But when people who had helped me started disappearing, I understood there were no more coincidences.”

Nayrin silently scanned the crowd. He could not see a single black figure, but for some reason Lior’s words sounded so certain that he did not feel inclined to doubt them.

“Why are they hunting you in particular?”

“I don’t know.”

Lior smiled faintly, but there was nothing cheerful in it.

“If I knew the answer, I would have stopped running long ago.”

They had almost passed the market stalls when Lior suddenly slowed. Ahead, beside an archway leading into the craftsmen’s quarter, stood a man in a dark cloak. He pretended to be listening closely to an elderly rope seller, but his gaze was fixed on something other than the goods. A few moments later, another man appeared on the opposite side of the street. He leaned his shoulder against the wall of a stone house and calmly watched the crowd, as though simply waiting for someone he knew.

Lior did not even turn his head.

“Left.”

Nayrin followed without asking questions. They passed through a narrow passage between two houses and emerged into a small courtyard, where several carpenters were installing a new gate. Heavy oak beams lay on the ground, wood shavings were scattered around, and the air smelled of fresh timber and resin. One of the craftsmen was loudly scolding a young apprentice who had driven a peg in badly. For a moment, the everyday noise brought back a sense of ordinary life.

Lior used the opportunity to look back at last. He did it very quickly, for only a fraction of a second, but that was enough. His face grew even darker.

“They’re no longer hiding the fact that they’ve spotted us.”

“How many?”

“More than I’d hoped.”

Nayrin wanted to ask another question, but did not get the chance. A sharp whistle came from the direction of the square. It was not the kind of whistle merchants used to call their assistants or boys used to signal to one another. It was a short, precise signal, after which the city’s noise seemed to be cut for an instant by an invisible blade.

Lior closed his eyes.

“It’s started…”

“What has?”

“Until now, they were still giving me a choice.”

He grabbed Nayrin sharply by the elbow and, for the first time, looked him straight in the eyes.

“Remember one thing. Whatever happens now, don’t try to be a hero. If we get separated even for a minute, we definitely won’t get out of here alive.”

Without waiting for an answer, he released Nayrin and hurried towards a narrow street running between tall stone houses. This time he no longer pretended to be a calm passer-by. His pace quickened, his movements became shorter and more precise, and his gaze constantly swept ahead, assessing every turn, every courtyard, and every open gate.

Nayrin silently stayed beside him. He still did not understand whom they were dealing with, but his years of training under Orm had taught him one simple truth: when an experienced warrior stops looking at his feet and begins watching what lies ahead, danger is already very close.

They were almost at the end of the street when two men in black emerged unhurriedly from around the corner. One stopped right in the middle of the passage. The other took a few more steps and froze, leaving exactly enough space between them to prevent anyone from slipping past.

Lior let out a heavy breath, abruptly changed direction, and turned into the next alley. This time he was no longer walking. He ran.

Nayrin dashed after him, without even trying to work out where they were going. During his years in this world, he had learned a simple truth: when fleeing, the decisions should be made by the person who knows the area. Everything else only gets in the way.

Lior weaved through the narrow streets with such confidence that he seemed to know every stone beneath his feet. He never hesitated at a crossroads, never searched for a route with his eyes, and did not look back even when quick footsteps sounded behind them. Their pursuers were no longer trying to conceal the chase.

They raced through the craftsmen’s quarter, where sheets of cloth hung drying in the open air and the clatter of looms came from open workshops. Frightened townspeople hurried aside as the two young men rushed past.

Someone shouted angrily when Lior leapt over barrels stacked in the road. One apprentice lost control of a handcart loaded with bricks, and it overturned with a crash right in front of the pursuers. Stones scattered across the cobbles and people screamed, but the men in black did not even try to jump over the obstacle. They split in different directions, went around the wreckage with astonishing coordination, and within seconds were moving together again, as though they had not been delayed at all.

“They won’t fall behind,” Nayrin said between breaths.

“I know.”

“Then where are we running?”

Lior did not answer at once. He turned sharply into a narrow passage between two houses, barely wide enough for two people to pass, waited until Nayrin caught up, and then said briefly:

“To somewhere they can’t stop us. We just have to make it there.”

But as soon as they emerged from the passage, both stopped involuntarily. Three men were already waiting ahead at the crossroads. The same black cloaks and calm faces. One of them was not even looking at the fugitives. He calmly adjusted his glove, as though he knew there was no longer any need to hurry.

Lior swore sharply in his native language.

“Impossible…”

He spun around so quickly that he nearly knocked Nayrin off his feet, and they raced back the way they had come. But now footsteps were pounding behind them too. The street they had just run through was rapidly filling with people in black.

They did not run as a disorganised crowd. They did not shout or wave their weapons. Each took up a position as though following an order rehearsed long ago.

For the first time, Nayrin felt genuine alarm. Orm often repeated that the most dangerous opponent was not the one who wielded a sword better, but the one who made you fight exactly where and when it suited him. Looking at their pursuers, Nayrin suddenly understood that this was precisely what was happening. They were not simply being chased. They were being slowly driven somewhere.

They burst into a small courtyard enclosed on all sides by stone houses. An old well stood in the middle, beside which several women were washing clothes. Seeing the two breathless young men, the women froze in confusion. A moment later, a man with an axe ran out through a neighbouring gate, thinking that robbers had broken into the city.

“Get out of the way!” Lior shouted.

The man had not even managed to raise his axe when the first men in black appeared behind the fugitives.

Everything happened so quickly that the women did not even understand what was going on. Seeing the armed strangers, they hurriedly abandoned their wash tubs, gathered up their children, and rushed into their houses. Doors slammed one after another, shutters closed, and the courtyard, noisy only a moment earlier, fell silent.

The man with the axe retreated too. He looked from Lior to the men in black, trying to decide whom to help, but not one of the pursuers even glanced in his direction. To them, everyone else had simply ceased to exist. They were interested in only two people.

Lior quickly surveyed the courtyard. There were three exits, and one was already blocked. A second was beginning to fill with black figures. And just then, another man in a black cloak slowly emerged from the third alley.

He looked no different from the others, but his appearance made Lior freeze. For the first time since the chase began, Nayrin saw him truly afraid. The young man whispered so quietly that he could barely be heard:

“Anyone but him…”

The courtyard seemed deserted. Only moments earlier, children had been shouting, wooden wash tubs had been clattering against the stones, women had been arguing, and carpenters had been joking as they urged one another to work. Now all of it had vanished as quickly as daylight before a storm. Only the old well in the middle of the yard remained, an indifferent witness to what was about to happen.

Lior stood motionless. His gaze moved from one exit to another, but Nayrin already understood: he was not searching for a way out. He was only confirming what he already knew. Every route really was blocked.

The men in black did not hurry closer. They silently took their positions, keeping equal distances between one another. There was something in that cold order far more frightening than open aggression. They behaved as though they already knew how everything would end and were simply waiting for their prey to take the final step.

“Don’t draw your sword first,” Lior said quietly without taking his eyes off the pursuers. “I don’t know what they want from us.”

Nayrin said nothing. He watched the men in black and felt a strange irritation. Throughout the entire chase, they had not uttered a single word. They had not threatened them or demanded that they surrender. Even now, with the circle finally closed, they were in no hurry to draw their swords. Their silence pressed harder than any shouting, and their confidence was gradually turning into an insult.

At last, one of the pursuers stepped forward. He moved slowly, almost lazily, showing not the slightest haste. His right hand rested calmly on the hilt of his sword, but the blade remained in its scabbard. He was no more than three steps from Nayrin when the stranger reached out, as though he intended simply to grab him by the shoulder.

At that very moment, Nayrin understood that he could wait no longer. His sword slipped from its scabbard so swiftly that even Lior flinched in surprise. Orm had made him repeat the same exercise hundreds of times: the first movement had to be so fast that an opponent realised the fight had begun only when it was already too late. His body remembered every training session on its own. Before the steel had even cleared the scabbard, the blade was already sweeping in a short arc towards the black-cloaked man’s hand.

But something happened that Nayrin had not expected at all. Instead of recoiling or trying to parry the strike with his sword, the stranger abruptly shifted his stance and raised a short metal bracer hidden beneath the wide sleeve of his cloak. Steel rang against steel. Sparks burst in every direction, and the force of the blow threw the man almost two steps backwards. His boots slid over the cobbles, leaving pale scratches on the stone, but he stayed on his feet.

For the first time, the men in black reacted. Not with a shout or a command, but with a movement all at once. Three of them immediately drew their swords, and the gleam of their blades briefly lit the gloomy courtyard. Lior was already standing beside Nayrin. He silently drew his own sword and took half a step forward, protecting Nayrin’s left flank.

“I asked you not to…” he said quietly, without taking his eyes off their opponents.

“They weren’t going to let us go anyway.”

This time, the men in black struck first.

They did not all move at once. Two attacked almost simultaneously, but from different sides, forcing the young men apart. Nayrin met the first man’s sword with such force that the opponent could not withstand the collision. His blade was knocked sharply aside, and he lost his balance. Nayrin turned his whole body and drove a short pommel strike into the man’s chest, hurling him against the stone wall. The air burst from the man’s lungs in a dull groan, and he slowly slid down, struggling to breathe again.

But Nayrin had no time to take advantage of his success. A second sword was almost immediately upon him. The blade whistled so close that it cut the sleeve of his jacket at the shoulder. Nayrin instinctively stepped back, parrying another strike, but now he realised that his opponent was his equal in both reaction speed and skill. The only difference was strength. Every clash of blades forced the pursuer to retreat a step, but he immediately returned to the fight, never allowing Nayrin to build on his advantage.

Out of the corner of his eye, Nayrin saw Lior. He fought quite differently. Not a single unnecessary movement. Not a single powerful swing. He did not try to defeat his opponent with one blow; instead, he gradually forced him to make mistakes. When one of the men in black lunged too widely, Lior slipped aside instantly and lightly cut his thigh. The wounded man staggered but did not fall. Two of his companions immediately stepped between him and Lior, giving him a chance to retreat.

It was then that Nayrin understood for the first time that they were not fighting individual warriors. They were fighting a unit that breathed, moved, and fought like a single living creature. The realisation sent a chill down his back, although the sword in his hands had not once trembled with fear.

Nayrin managed only one deep breath before another opponent took the wounded man’s place. The injured fighter was pulled back with such speed and coordination that it looked more like the work of a well-tuned mechanism than the actions of living people.

No one rushed to help their comrade, bent over him, or even looked back. Two of them merely shielded him for a moment with a tight wall of swords, allowing him to withdraw. Then a third took his place without the slightest pause, and the fight continued with the same steady, relentless rhythm, as though the brief injury had meant nothing to the unit.

Time gradually lost its shape. Nayrin could no longer have said how long the fight had lasted—a minute or half an hour. Before his eyes, only identical black cloaks, flashes of blades, and the grey stone walls of the courtyard kept changing. Between them, he and Lior were retreating step by step, unable to break out.

The men in black moved as though connected by invisible threads. None hurried to distinguish himself; none tried to win with a single personal strike. Each simply performed his part of a shared task. Whenever one stepped back, another took his place flawlessly, leaving the young men not even a brief moment to catch their breath.

Nayrin parried another blow, slipped aside to avoid a blade that whistled past his face, and suddenly felt something strange change. The world around him did not fall silent, as sometimes happened under intense stress. On the contrary, the clash of swords, the pounding of feet on stone, the fighters’ laboured breathing, and the cries of people beyond the walls remained just as loud. Something else had changed. Through all the chaos, another sensation began to emerge, entirely unfamiliar yet so distinct that it could no longer be ignored. It had no sound, smell, or colour. It simply existed, gradually filling his consciousness as inevitably as water fills a hollow in stone.

At first, Nayrin thought he was sensing Lior, but almost immediately realised his mistake. This sensation was entirely different. It carried none of the familiar warmth of a human presence and did not resemble the silent exchange to which he had already grown accustomed. It was as though dozens of consciousnesses had opened before him at once, merging into something single.

He could not hear individual thoughts or see images, but suddenly he began to sense the very order in which they arose. One of them already knew that in a moment he would replace the wounded man. Another was preparing to circle around them on the right. A third had not yet moved, but the decision about his next attack already existed in all of them. They did not confer. They gave no signals. They waited for no orders. Their unit thought like one creature divided among many bodies.

The touch made everything inside him turn cold. Instinctively, Nayrin wanted to recoil from the other consciousness, just as a person pulls their hand away from hot metal. This mind-link did not frighten him. Its unnatural orderliness, its complete absence of doubt, hesitation, or emotion, filled him with something close to physical revulsion. It was as though he had accidentally looked inside a complex mechanism that only resembled human beings on the outside.

At that very moment, the pursuers changed formation. Two of them sharply accelerated their attack, forcing Nayrin farther back into the courtyard, while the others silently began circling them from the sides. Out of the corner of his eye, he saw more men in black appearing in the passage through which they had run in a few minutes earlier.

The exit had silently disappeared behind a dense wall of figures. Without exchanging a word, the two young men almost simultaneously stepped towards each other until they felt one another’s support at their backs. Now each could see only his own half of the courtyard, but that was enough to prevent the enemy from getting behind them.

The fight continued without leaving a single moment for thought. Nayrin parried another blow, shoved an attacker away with his shoulder, and only then felt Lior suddenly freeze behind him. It lasted only an instant, but even that was enough to make his heart tighten with alarm. He could not turn around: another blade was already descending in front of him. After parrying it almost blindly, Nayrin glanced sideways and saw something that should not have been possible.

The air beside Lior seemed to lose its transparency. It shuddered like the air above sun-heated stones, then slowly began to part, revealing a narrow dark slit. There was no flash, no thunder, no other display of power. The very fabric of the world simply gave way, opening a passage to a place where leaves stirred among dense trees and sunlight fell across an unfamiliar forest clearing.

Lior stared at the opening with the same astonishment as everyone else. He spoke no words, made no mysterious gestures, and seemed not to understand what had just happened himself.

The men in black hesitated too, but only for a moment. That was enough. Nayrin kicked the nearest pursuer off balance, seized Lior by the shoulder, and almost forced him forward. They stepped through the dark opening together, and in that instant the world around them changed abruptly. The clashing swords, footsteps, and shouts were left behind as unexpectedly as they had appeared, replaced by the smell of wet grass, the rustle of leaves, and the cool air of the forest.

They sat on the ground for several minutes, in no hurry to get up. After the frantic clash, the forest seemed almost unreal. Birds sang somewhere overhead, the wind swayed the treetops, and sunlight slowly moved across the damp grass, as though there had never been chases, swords, or men in black anywhere in the world.

Lior was the first to break the silence. He stared at his own palms for a long time, slowly clenched and unclenched his fingers several times, and then looked at Nayrin.

“Did you see that? I didn’t do anything… I just… wanted to get out of there.”

“I don’t understand what happened either,” Nayrin replied quietly. “But we didn’t pass through a door or a wall. That was a passage between worlds.”

Lior fell silent, thinking it over, then slowly said:

“If I could do that just now… maybe that’s exactly how we ended up here after our world was destroyed. It wasn’t a miracle. It wasn’t an accident. Perhaps we opened that first passage ourselves without even understanding it.”

Nayrin did not answer. He simply stretched his hand forward, repeating the same movement Lior had made a few minutes earlier. At first nothing happened, but a moment later the air in front of him shivered softly, and a narrow dark slit opened between the trees, just wide enough to peer through.

On the other side was the same courtyard. The fight was already over. The men in black stood in a small circle, discussing something animatedly and occasionally pointing towards the place where the portal had appeared only moments earlier. Nayrin looked for just an instant, then instinctively clenched his hand. The darkness closed silently, as though it had never existed.

They sat in silence for a long time after that. They no longer needed to convince each other of what had happened. The answer they had so desperately sought since the day they appeared in a strange world had unexpectedly found them instead.

They had survived neither by chance nor through someone else’s intervention. A power they had never even suspected existed lay hidden within them. It was that power which had once torn them from a doomed world, and now it had opened a path before them to countless other planes.
