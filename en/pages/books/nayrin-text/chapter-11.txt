A wide plain stretched all the way to the horizon, covered with old snow. It had long since ceased to be white. The wind had mixed it with ash, earth, and fine dust, so the surface was now a dirty grey. Here and there, stiff yellowed grass pushed through the frozen soil, and charred tree stumps that had never survived the old fires stood black among them. Nature was slowly reclaiming what was hers. Young pines and birches grew among the fragments of concrete, as though they had spent all these years patiently pushing humanity out of a world it had once destroyed itself.

But the forest was not the most striking sight. Several kilometres ahead, a metropolis rose above the plain. Or, more precisely, what remained of it.

Enormous concrete towers still stood above the ground. Most had retained their outlines, but none was alive anymore. Dark, empty window openings stared into the distance like vacant eye sockets. In places, entire floors had collapsed, exposing rooms where fragments of staircases, mangled walls, and once-cosy apartments could still be made out. Trees had long since taken root on the rooftops. Their roots split the concrete slabs, while mighty pines rose above the high-rises as though they had always been part of the landscape.

A portal opened soundlessly in the middle of the snow-covered field. The dark rift parted the air, and an old armoured all-terrain vehicle slowly emerged. It had long outlived its best years. Its armour, darkened by time, was covered in scratches and dents. Traces of countless repairs were visible along its sides, and some metal plates were noticeably different in colour. They had been replaced many times in the field, using whatever materials could be found in each new world. The vehicle travelled a few more metres and came to a halt. The portal quietly closed behind it, leaving only the monotonous moan of the cold wind across the plain.

Without switching off the vehicle’s systems, Lior carefully studied the outlines of the ruined city through the observation equipment. His gaze moved from one high-rise to another, from elevated roads to twisted bridges, as though he were trying to find something familiar in this picture of decay.

Olena was already working at the diagnostic module. Over the years of travelling, this had become a familiar ritual. She connected the external sensors and brought up readings for the atmosphere, temperature, radiation levels, and chemical composition of the air. One by one, the green indicators lit up. Then she opened the biological analysis results, checked for toxic contaminants and possible unknown compounds. Only after confirming that the automated systems had found nothing dangerous did she take out a portable analyser and personally check the readings once more.

Meanwhile, Nayrin, Isaac, and Marta silently prepared to leave the vehicle. They checked the seals on their protective suits, helmet locks, air-filtration systems, weapons, and internal communications. After years of travelling together, these movements had become so familiar that everyone performed them almost mechanically. No orders were needed; each of them knew their responsibilities.

They had left the Major about five years earlier. He had let them go surprisingly easily, even offering them an armoured all-terrain vehicle and providing everything they needed for long-range reconnaissance. At the time, Nayrin had even thought that the Major felt a certain relief when the four travellers and Isaac, who over the years had finally become one of them, left his settlement. In a way, that was understandable. Their very presence restrained the Major’s growing ambitions. They remembered him as a completely different man: someone who had once gathered survivors around him not for the sake of power, but to save them. Perhaps that was why it had been harder for him to forget who he had once been when they were nearby.

Everything changed after the Great Loss.

That was the name they later gave to the day when more than a thousand people they had believed were saved began disappearing one after another from the new world. They were all ordinary people, born on the plane that the Major and the four travellers had left because of the fiery catastrophe. They had passed through portals, begun settling in a new place, and already believed the worst was behind them. Three days later, the world took them back.

The first weeks after the Great Loss remained in Nayrin’s memory as one continuous nightmare. People could not come to terms with what had happened. Some blamed the Major, some blamed the four travellers, and some blamed themselves. A few opened portals on their own for the first time and left for an unknown world without saying a word to anyone. Others simply could not bear losing those they had grown to love and had already believed were safe. There were suicides, and there were deaths that could have been prevented. Several people almost stopped communicating even through the mind-link, becoming silent shadows of their former selves.

The Major did everything he could to keep the community from falling apart completely, and the four helped him as best they could. But even he, a man who had once seemed unbreakable, had changed. The Great Loss had affected everyone, and when the first terrible weeks had passed, only a little more than fifty of their people remained beside the Major out of the slightly more than a hundred survivors. The rest had scattered across the worlds, died, or decided that they no longer wanted to live.

Eventually, they understood what had happened.

The cause of the Great Loss was hidden in the very laws governing the worlds. An ordinary person was inseparably bound to the plane on which they had been born. They could pass through a portal opened by someone from the Major’s home plane and could live in another world for several days, but they could not remain there forever. After approximately three days, unbearable pain began, and then the person was returned against their will to their native plane. That was why everyone they had believed they had saved disappeared almost simultaneously and returned to a world that was already engulfed in flames. The laws of the universe knew no mercy and made no exceptions simply because returning meant certain death.

In time, they discovered another pattern. The children of people from their world were born without the ability to open portals and belonged to the world in which they were born. Their parents’ abilities were not inherited. They could not be acquired through learning or persistent training.

All the known portal travellers had appeared in one world at approximately the same time—during the nuclear war. No one knew why some people, in the moment of mortal danger, had unconsciously opened portals and escaped, while billions of others had died. After years of searching, they had still found no answer and eventually accepted it as a fact.

The next five years living alongside the Major passed very differently. His small community gradually recovered from the Great Loss. Local inhabitants began appearing around the settlement, first one by one, then in entire families and tribes. Some came willingly, seeking protection and a better life; others were subjugated by the Major through force. The settlement filled once more with voices, children’s laughter, and everyday concerns, but the fifty or so people who had survived the Great Loss mostly kept to themselves. They knew well that the locals belonged to this world, and the memory of the thousand people they had once called their own made it difficult to become attached to new ones.

The Major himself grew less like the man they had once met in the war with every passing year. Back then, he had saved people because he considered it his duty. Now he had lands, subjects, armed units, and almost unlimited power over the local tribes. Nayrin could not say exactly when the desire to protect had turned into a desire to rule, but by the time they left, the change was already obvious.

The Major had almost stopped exploring neighbouring worlds and focused on building his own state. For Isaac, this was the final proof that it was time to leave. Reconnaissance and travelling had long since become his life. His former existence now seemed too predictable, while the endless road, despite its dangers, restored the feeling that every new day mattered.

Five years earlier, they had said goodbye to the Major and set out to find their home plane.

At first, they thought the journey would not take long. They knew the direction of the branch and hoped to make their way gradually back to where their journey had once begun. But the worlds were in no hurry to let the travellers go. They rarely stayed long in dead planes, but every inhabited world brought new complications. In some places, they had to spend months repairing the vehicle; in others, they became involved in local conflicts; and sometimes they simply could not pass by people who needed help.

That was why they had not travelled through hundreds of worlds in five years. Nor had they tried to. They simply kept moving forward, from one plane to the next, hoping that one day they would see something familiar.

The first worlds still offered hope. People lived there, forests grew, and rivers flowed. But there were completely different ones too. Some planes were saturated with radiation; others were poisoned by unknown gases. In one world, the ground glowed with incandescent cracks between active volcanoes. In another, there was no atmosphere at all, and only the vehicle’s armour separated the five travellers from absolute emptiness.

It was in places like these that they remembered the Major most often. Without his gift, they would most likely have set out on foot and would hardly have covered even half the distance. Instead, he had insisted that they take an armoured all-terrain vehicle with a sealed life-support system, fuel reserves, repair equipment, and everything necessary for a long autonomous journey. However complicated their relationship had become towards the end, each of them had silently thanked the Major for that gift more than once.

Olena took another careful look at the instrument readings, compared them with the portable analyser, and only then slowly exhaled.

“The atmosphere is breathable. Radiation levels are slightly above normal, but safe. No toxic contaminants. It is very cold, though.”

Nayrin nodded silently. He pulled a lever, and the heavy upper hatch slowly opened with a metallic scrape. Icy wind immediately rushed inside. Without hesitation, Nayrin climbed up first and emerged onto the armour. He did not seal his helmet. The freezing air sharply stung his face, and his lungs filled with the smell of snow, cold stone, and distant smoke.

Isaac, Marta, Lior, and finally Olena climbed out after him, one by one. They stood silently beside the armoured vehicle. No one hurried to speak. Each of them stared at the city ahead, feeling a strange, almost painful excitement that no one yet dared to name.

Enormous glass-and-concrete tubes stretched out from the metropolis in every direction. They rose several dozen metres above the ground and disappeared beyond the horizon, like gigantic arteries that still connected the remains of a once-united civilisation. Some sections had been destroyed, but most of the structures had survived. In places, new metal trusses, fresh concrete surfaces, and support pillars could be seen, apparently restored after the war.

“Path Cities…” Olena whispered, staring at the endless web of elevated roads. “Could they really have survived?”

No one answered. All five looked ahead. Only then did they notice a thin dark line moving slowly along one of the elevated roads. It was too regular to be an illusion of light. Lior quickly raised his binoculars, watched in silence for a few seconds, and then handed them to Nayrin.

A column of heavy vehicles was moving slowly along the concrete strip. They looked like enormous steel beetles stubbornly crawling through the snow-covered world.

Olena suddenly froze. Her gaze lost focus, as though she were listening to something beyond the reach of ordinary ears.

“I can sense the mind-link.”

The others almost simultaneously focused their attention. Only then did they hear it. Not one voice, or a dozen. There were hundreds. Distant and close, faint and distinct, they overlapped to form a continuous, living murmur. Someone was talking, someone was calling to another person, fragments of an argument could be heard somewhere, along with laughter and ordinary everyday questions. None of these people had any idea that five travellers were standing in the middle of a snow-covered field, listening to them, unable to believe that they had finally found what they were looking for.

This was life. And it sounded the way it could only sound here.

On their home plane.

“They’ve noticed us,” Olena said a few minutes later. A smile appeared on her face for the first time. “Someone is coming this way. They say they’ll be here in about half an hour.”

Nayrin wanted to answer, but he could not. He stared at the distant Path Cities and was only now beginning to understand that the twenty years since the first nuclear explosions really were behind him. Ten years in a foreign village, five years alongside the Major, and another five years of endless travel—all of it suddenly fitted into one brief moment in the middle of the cold plain.

The half-hour wait seemed unusually long. Isaac stayed beside the all-terrain vehicle with Nayrin, studying the ruins of the metropolis through binoculars. Marta moved a little farther away, examining the young trees pushing through the snow, while Olena continued checking the radiation levels. Lior returned to the vehicle several times, as though unable to decide whether to prepare it for the next leg of the journey, then smiled at the absurdity of the thought.

At last, a dark speck appeared on the snowy plain.

An off-road vehicle approached quickly, raising a long trail of snow dust behind it. It was much newer than their battered machine and stopped a few metres away. The engine fell silent, the doors opened, and three people stepped out one after another.

The first was White.

The second was Black.

And the third was Blue.

Nayrin did not even realise when a pistol had appeared in his hand.

Twenty years vanished in an instant. The sky above his home city flashed before his eyes again, and one after another nuclear mushroom clouds began rising on the horizon. He was once again the young boy standing beside his brother, watching their world come to an end.

Safeties clicked almost simultaneously behind him. Out of the corner of his eye, Nayrin saw that Isaac, Marta, and Lior also had their weapons ready. Even Olena, who was usually the last to reach for a pistol, reacted almost instantly this time.

The three newcomers made no attempt to draw their weapons. The White man quickly stepped forward and placed himself between Nayrin and the Blue man, raising his empty hands. Meanwhile, the Black man shifted almost imperceptibly closer to the Blue man, instinctively shielding his companion with his own body.

“Easy,” the White man said. His voice was wary, but not afraid. “The only enemy you’ll find here is the cold. And maybe radiation in a few places, if you really go looking for it.”

No one laughed.

The pause grew longer. The White man continued to stand with his hands raised, while his two companions kept their palms in plain sight, showing that they had no intention of reaching for their weapons.

Nayrin watched them and gradually noticed what had escaped him at first. They were not afraid of one another.

The White man had stepped in front of the Blue man without hesitation, shielding him from a bullet. The Black man had instinctively stood beside him in the same way. There was not the slightest trace of distrust in their movements, as though the idea that one of them could be an enemy because of skin colour was as absurd to these three as being afraid of a comrade.

Nayrin slowly lowered his pistol. The others lowered their weapons after him.

“Forgive us,” he finally said.

The White man carefully lowered his hands and smiled a little more calmly.

“It’s all right. Believe me, you’re not the first.”

He glanced at his companions, then looked back at the travellers.

“That’s exactly why we come in threes. We never know whom we’ll meet or what kind of place that person has returned from. For some, the war ended twenty years ago. Others have spent all those years in a world where it never ended.”

“But you could have asked us through the mind-link,” Olena pointed out.

“We could,” the man agreed. “But it wouldn’t have changed much. Not everyone who returns knows how to use the mind-link well. Some hardly use it at all. And asking someone what race they belong to before going out to meet them…” He smiled and shook his head. “We decided long ago that it was easier to show them straight away how things work now.”

He nodded towards his companions.

“That’s why newcomers are always greeted by three characterники. White, Black, and Blue. Usually, after the first minute, no further explanation is needed.”

“Characterники?” Lior repeated.

The man raised his eyebrows in surprise, then laughed.

“So you really have been away from home for a long time. That’s what we call ourselves. Remember the old legends about the characterники?”

All five exchanged glances. Of course they remembered. Stories about strange warriors credited with incredible abilities had been part of every one of their childhoods.

“Well, there you are,” the man said, spreading his hands. “That’s us now. And judging by the fact that you made it here, you are too.”

He slowly came closer, deliberately avoiding any sudden movements, and held out his hand to Nayrin.

“I’m Airis. This is Iman,” he said, nodding towards the Black man, “and this is Sukhum.”

Nayrin shook the offered hand. Iman then approached him, and that handshake came much more easily.

Sukhum was the last to stop in front of him. He held out his hand.

Nayrin froze.

The Blue man did not withdraw his palm or say anything. He seemed to understand perfectly well why the person before him was hesitating.

Nayrin stared at his hand and felt something rising inside him that he had believed buried for twenty years. It was the Blue race that had started the nuclear war. Once, Nayrin had known these people only as enemies. They appeared in military reports, their vehicles had been seen through weapon sights, and their missiles had flown towards the cities of the White and Black races on that terrible day.

And now one of them stood before Nayrin unarmed, waiting patiently.

Beside him stood the White and the Black, who had just been ready to shield Sukhum with their own bodies.

Nayrin slowly reached out. Sukhum clasped his hand firmly.

Nothing extraordinary happened. No grand words were spoken, and no one delivered a speech. Two men whose peoples had once tried to destroy each other simply shook hands in the middle of a snow-covered field.

But for Nayrin, the war that had begun twenty years earlier finally ended at that very moment.

As though they had been waiting for this, the others also moved towards one another. Isaac was the first to start talking with Iman; Lior was already asking Airis questions, while Marta and Olena greeted Sukhum in turn. Only a few minutes earlier, the two groups had stood facing one another with weapons in their hands. Now eight people were awkwardly introducing themselves in the middle of the cold plain, unsure which of a thousand questions to ask first.

“Do many people return?” Isaac finally asked.

“Two or three every month, sometimes more,” Sukhum answered. “There are already more than five hundred of us on the planet.”

“Are they all characterники?” Lior asked.

“Everyone who has returned from other worlds,” Airis nodded. “Ordinary people haven’t travelled far.”

The five travellers looked at him almost simultaneously.

Airis noticed their reaction.

“It seems you really do have a lot of questions.”

“You have no idea how many,” Isaac replied.

“Then I suggest we get to the Path City first,” Airis interrupted, rubbing his hands together. “If we stand here for another hour, the first story after your return home will be about how eight characterники froze to death a few kilometres from a warm place to live.”

This time, everyone laughed. Perhaps that was exactly what they had been missing: an ordinary joke, simple words after which the tension that had held them since the portal opened finally began to ease.

Airis glanced towards the distant elevated roads of the Path City.

“You have a lot to see. We’re slowly rebuilding civilisation. And if you ask me, we’re not doing too badly.”

No one objected. A few minutes later, the engines came to life again. The off-road vehicle moved first, clearing a path through the old snow, followed slowly by the battered armoured all-terrain vehicle that had carried the travellers through foreign worlds for five years.

The Path Cities rose ahead. And for the first time in twenty years, Nayrin was driving towards them not as a memory of a lost past. He was coming home.

The off-road vehicle led the way, clearing a path through the snow-covered road, while the armoured all-terrain vehicle followed several dozen metres behind. At first, they travelled across the open plain, but with every kilometre the ruins drew closer. Isolated fragments of concrete beside the road gradually gave way to the remains of buildings, then the first high-rises appeared, and soon the vehicles entered what had been the outskirts of an enormous metropolis twenty years earlier.

Nayrin sat beside Lior, looking ahead through the armoured glass. During five years of travel, they had seen many ruined cities. Some had lain in ruins for hundreds of years; others had been destroyed quite recently. They had walked streets where human bones lay among abandoned cars, spent nights in houses belonging to long-vanished civilisations, and driven past entire metropolises swallowed by forests. It seemed that one more dead city could no longer evoke anything in them beyond their usual caution.

But this time everything was different. This city was not foreign. None of the five knew whether they had ever been here before, but that hardly mattered. The architecture, the width of the streets, the road signs, the shape of the lampposts, even the half-erased inscriptions on the façades belonged to the same world in which they had been born.

Every little detail was painfully familiar. It seemed that if they turned the next corner, they would find an ordinary street with people, shops, and transport, and the twenty years spent in foreign worlds would dissolve like a strange dream. Yet every turn revealed only more ruins.

No one had cleared the road for a long time. The off-road vehicle steered around abandoned cars, fragments of concrete, and trees growing straight through the cracked asphalt. In places, they had to drive onto the pavement because collapsed floors blocked the carriageway. At one point, an entire corner of a high-rise hung over the road, miraculously supported by several blackened pillars. Elsewhere, all that remained of a residential district was an enormous empty space with fragments of concrete walls rising in the middle.

Marta sat beside the observation slit, barely moving. Near one of the crossroads, she suddenly noticed a playground. A rusty slide leaned to one side, one of the swings lay in the snow, and the other swayed slowly in the wind.

“Strange,” she said quietly. “I don’t even know this city, and yet I feel as though I remember it.”

Isaac looked in the same direction.

“Because our cities were alike.”

“Not only because of that.”

Marta did not explain. She herself did not know how to name the feeling. This was not a return home in the way she had imagined it all these years. The home they had been searching for no longer existed. Before them lay its body—vast, cold, and mangled by time. And yet, among these ruins, she felt for the first time in many years that she was no longer a stranger.

Airis’s voice unexpectedly sounded through the internal communications system. Their vehicles had already connected through a short-range channel, so they could speak without using the mind-link.

“Don’t be alarmed by the city’s appearance. Almost nothing here has been rebuilt.”

“We’ve noticed,” Lior replied. “Why not?”

“What for?” Airis asked. “Living here is dangerous and expensive. Some districts are still contaminated, buildings are unsafe, and the underground infrastructure is full of damage. Clearing all this would take decades. There aren’t many people as it is.”

“How few?” Olena asked.

There was silence in the speaker for a few seconds.

“No one knows the exact number,” Airis finally replied. “They try to conduct a census, but you understand what that means now. There are remote sections of the Path Cities and isolated communities. Some have had no contact with anyone for years. But there are very few of us compared with the population before the war.”

Ahead, the off-road vehicle turned onto a wide avenue. It had probably once been one of the main transport arteries of the metropolis. Now its six lanes of asphalt were overgrown with grass and young trees. Hundreds of abandoned cars stood in the middle of the road. Some had burned out; others remained almost undamaged, as though their drivers had simply stepped out and never returned.

Nayrin looked at them and suddenly thought of the first hours of the war.

“What happened here after the strikes?” he asked.

This time Sukhum answered.

“The same thing that happened everywhere. At first, everyone tried to survive on their own. Those who could fled the cities. Those who couldn’t searched for shelter. The authorities ceased to exist almost immediately. Communications lasted a little longer, but there was no one left to coordinate anything.”

“And the Path Cities?”

“They saved us.”

Ahead, between the ruined high-rises, an enormous concrete structure was already visible. At first, Nayrin thought it was another elevated road, but with every minute it grew larger. The road rose towards it along a wide ramp, while the structure itself stretched far in both directions, disappearing among the ruins of the metropolis.

“When the strikes began, most ordinary cities were destroyed or became uninhabitable,” Sukhum continued. “But the Path Cities stretch for hundreds and thousands of kilometres. You can’t destroy an entire structure like that with a single strike. In some places a kilometre collapsed, in others ten; some strikes destroyed major junctions, but intact sections remained beyond them. People gathered there.”

“Without electricity?” Lior asked.

“At first, with almost nothing. The first years were the worst. Surviving generators, water reserves, emergency air-filtration systems. We dismantled equipment in dead sections and moved it to places where people were still living. We grew food wherever we could. We even set up lamps and greenhouses inside technical rooms. Then we began restoring power generation. Then transport. Communications. Step by step.”

“How many Path Cities survived?” Marta asked.

“If we count the entire pre-war network, approximately thirty per cent remained habitable or were restored. But they’re disconnected. In some places you can travel hundreds of kilometres before reaching a destroyed section. So now we’re restoring connections between the largest surviving stretches.”

The off-road vehicle ahead slowed down.

They were approaching the entrance.

Nayrin remembered the Path Cities very differently. Once, these had been enormous structures through which people and cargo moved without interruption. Now the concrete around the entrance was blackened, part of the outer cladding had collapsed, and traces of old explosions were visible above the road. But the heavy airtight gates were new. Searchlights were operating nearby, cameras were turning, and armed people were on duty at a small platform.

After the dead metropolis, their presence impressed Nayrin more than anything else.

One of the guards raised his hand in greeting to Airis. Another was already opening the outer gates. A small truck loaded with metal containers emerged from somewhere inside and stopped to let their column pass.

“Welcome to civilisation,” Airis’s voice came through the speaker. “Or what’s left of it.”

“Or what has already managed to grow again,” Iman objected.

The all-terrain vehicle crossed the gates. Almost immediately, the world around them changed.

After the silent ruins of the metropolis, Nayrin felt as though he had arrived on another planet. Lamps illuminated the internal road. Ventilation systems were working somewhere. A small electric vehicle drove towards them, followed by two men in work clothes. Beside a wall, a woman was explaining something to a child while holding the child’s hand. Farther on, an open repair bay could be seen, with the clanging of metal coming from inside.

People lived here.

They were not merely surviving. They were living. Nayrin did not immediately notice that another sensation had come with this realisation.

The mind-link.

Beyond the Path City, he had sensed it as a distant murmur scattered across an enormous area. Here, as soon as the all-terrain vehicle passed through the gates, the voices became closer and clearer. New presences appeared one after another in the familiar space.

Dozens of characterники. They could sense one another. Someone almost immediately noticed the five new voices. Nayrin felt curiosity, surprise, and brief greetings from complete strangers. News of the return of five more characterники was spreading through the network faster than they could travel along the corridors of the Path City.

Olena laughed.

“I’d forgotten what it was like to hear so many people at once.”

“You’ll get used to it,” Airis replied through the mind-link.

His voice sounded completely different now from the way it had over the radio. Closer. More natural. And suddenly Nayrin sensed another presence. It was strange: weaker than the others, somewhat indistinct, as though the voice were coming through interference. Olena noticed it too.

“Who is that?”

Airis immediately understood whom she meant.

“And this is something we’re especially proud of.”

There was so much satisfaction in his voice that even Nayrin smiled.

“That’s an ordinary person.”

Lior turned towards the off-road vehicle ahead, although of course he could not see Airis through its armour.

“An ordinary person?”

“Absolutely. Not a characterник.”

“Then how can we hear her?”

“A bracelet. Our engineers have been trying for several years to understand how the characterники’s mind-link works. They still haven’t fully figured it out, but they’ve learned how to connect an ordinary person to it artificially. So far, only one.”

“One person on the entire planet?” Marta asked.

“So far, one,” Airis confirmed with obvious pride. “And don’t think that’s insignificant. The first attempts did nothing at all. Then people began to hear noise. Later, individual voices. This woman is the first who can use the mind-link almost as fully as we can.”

“And she’s connected all the time?” Olena asked.

“When she wears the bracelet. The technology is still experimental. The device is large, its power supply doesn’t last long, and there are plenty of side effects. But it works.”

Nayrin silently listened to the unfamiliar presence. An ordinary person. Twenty years ago, the characterники had not even known what they would become. Now their society was already trying to understand the nature of their abilities and pass at least some of those capabilities on to others.

“You’re developing quickly,” Lior said.

Airis laughed.

“Quickly? It took us twenty years to get this far.”

“Believe me,” Lior replied, looking through the observation window at the illuminated corridors, people, and machinery. “We’ve seen worlds that couldn’t take even the first step in twenty years after a catastrophe.”

The conversation fell silent for a while. The all-terrain vehicle continued moving, and the Path City gradually opened up before the travellers.

The pre-war luxury was gone. Many rooms had been rebuilt; decorative panels had given way to bare metal; old shopping centres had been turned into workshops, warehouses, and residential blocks. Greenhouses stood in the middle of some of the wide galleries. Vegetables grew behind transparent walls, irrigation systems were running, and people walked between the rows of plants.

But everywhere there was light.

Elevators worked. Transport moved. Somewhere, a wall was being repaired; somewhere else, new cables were being laid. Children with backpacks hurried along in a group, arguing so loudly that it sounded as though the world had never experienced a nuclear war.

Nayrin watched them and felt his thoughts about home gradually changing. When they had first seen the metropolis, he had thought they were twenty years too late. That they had returned only to see the grave of the civilisation they had once known.

Now he understood that he had been wrong. Their old world really had died. But the people had not.

They had hidden inside the Path Cities, survived the first winters, hunger, radiation, and the collapse of everything that had once seemed unshakable. They had learned to generate electricity again, grow food, repair machines, have children, and build a future among the fragments of the past.

And most importantly, they had done something that had seemed impossible twenty years earlier.

White, Black, and Blue were living together.

Nayrin could see them through the vehicle’s window. They worked side by side, talked, argued, and laughed. Two men of different races were pulling a heavy metal module together. Near the entrance to a greenhouse, a Blue woman was holding the hands of two children while a White man adjusted a scarf on one of them.

No one paid attention to skin colour.

The world had needed a nuclear war for people to finally stop dividing it among themselves. The price had been terrible. But something that had never existed before had been born among the ruins: a single civilisation.

“You haven’t seen anything yet,” Airis’s voice came through the mind-link. “Wait until we reach the residential sectors. There, you can almost forget that the end of the world is happening above us.”

Nayrin looked ahead, towards the illuminated tunnel leading deeper into the Path City. He did not know what awaited them farther on. But for the first time in twenty years, that question no longer frightened him.

They reached the residential sectors about half an hour later. By then, Nayrin had almost stopped watching the road. The Path City held his attention with countless small details that seemed almost unbelievable after five years of travelling through foreign worlds.

People passed by them, workshops were operating, transport was moving, and shops and small canteens could be seen in the wide side galleries. Workers were arguing somewhere, children were laughing elsewhere, and above it all the voices of characterники living here or in other parts of the planet continued to sound through the mind-link.

When the armoured all-terrain vehicle finally stopped on a wide platform beside one of the residential sectors, Nayrin remained seated for several more seconds. During the years of travel, he had grown used to every stop marking the beginning of another unknown.

They had to check the air, find water, assess the danger, and decide whether it was worth staying even for one night. Now, beyond the armoured glass, there was an ordinary human city, and the very thought that they would not have to search for the next portal today seemed unfamiliar.

Lior had already switched off the engine. After many hours of continuous rumbling, the silence inside the vehicle seemed almost unnatural. Isaac opened the rear hatch, and the voices of people and the steady murmur of the Path City flowed into the cabin.

“We’re here,” he said, turning towards the others.

Marta was the first to stand.

“That’s a strange word.”

“Which one?”

“‘Here.’”

She smiled, but there was weariness in her voice.

“We haven’t arrived anywhere for so many years. We’ve only ever stopped before the next journey.”

Nayrin wanted to answer, but at that very moment he heard his own name. Not nearby and not from outside. It sounded through the mind-link.

Among the dozens of voices that had surrounded him since they entered the Path City, another suddenly appeared. Distant, faint, almost impossible to make out. For a moment, Nayrin even thought he had imagined it. In twenty years, he had heard that voice in his dreams too many times and had woken up too often before he could answer.

“Nayrin?..”

He froze, gripping the edge of the seat. Marta was already standing by the open hatch. Isaac was asking Lior something, while outside Airis was explaining to someone where to park their all-terrain vehicle. Nayrin could still see and hear all of it, but suddenly he could no longer understand the meaning of the words.

He knew that voice. He did not merely remember it—he knew it.

Twenty years had not been able to erase it. Not ten years of life in a foreign village, when Nayrin had almost resigned himself to the thought that he would never see his home world again. Not five years alongside the Major. Not the Great Loss. Not the next five years on the road, during which every portal they opened might have been the last, and every inhabited world made him ask again whether his brother might be somewhere there.

Zairin.

Nayrin tried to answer, but he could not.

He slowly stood up, hitting his shoulder against the low ceiling of the all-terrain vehicle, and barely noticed. Lior turned at the sound, but Nayrin was already making his way towards the exit.

“Nayrin! Is that you?!”

This time the voice sounded so strongly that it sent pain somewhere deep inside him.

Nayrin climbed out and stopped beside the vehicle. The city continued living all around him. People walked past the platform, a freight lift was operating nearby, and the clanging of metal came from an open workshop. Airis stood a few metres away, talking to Iman about something.

Nayrin braced his palm against the cold armour of the vehicle.

“Yes.”

That was all he could manage to say. Silence fell at the other end of the mind-link. Then Nayrin felt something no words could convey.

Zairin was alive.

Not a memory. Not a voice from the past that he had carried with him for so many years. Not a hope for which they had travelled from one world to another for five years.

A living person. His brother.

“Where are you?” he finally heard.

Nayrin looked around, suddenly realising that he did not even know the answer. Airis, who had apparently heard their conversation through the mind-link, immediately gave the sector number.

Nayrin repeated it to his brother.

“Stay there.”

“Zairin…”

“Don’t go anywhere,” his brother interrupted, and now Nayrin could hear almost childlike fear in his voice. “Do you hear me? Just stay there.”

Only then did Nayrin understand. Zairin was afraid. Not that he would fail to arrive in time. He was afraid his brother would disappear again. Just as he had twenty years earlier.

Nayrin closed his eyes and pressed his palm against the cold armour of the vehicle that had carried him through dozens of foreign worlds to this moment.

“I’m not going anywhere,” he replied. “I’m home.”
