The Path Cities had become the greatest achievement of humanity’s technological civilization. Their construction had begun several centuries before Nayrin was born, when the planet’s population had grown so large that the old cities could no longer provide people with housing, transport, or security.

Enormous structures made of steel, composites, and ultra-strong glass stretched for thousands of kilometres, crossing continents in unbroken lines. Their bodies stood high on gigantic supports, rising above plains, forests, and rivers. Inside ran high-speed monorail lines, between which lay residential districts, schools, hospitals, industrial complexes, research centres, and parks. A transparent dome let in sunlight, while climate-control systems maintained the same temperature throughout the year. A person could live an entire life without ever leaving the boundaries of a Path City, for it was a world of its own, stretching continuously across the whole European continent.

Nayrin and Zairin lived in an old metropolis near one of these Path Cities. They had been orphans for almost six years. An air crash, caused by enemy forces breaking through the front line, had taken both their parents when Nayrin had barely turned twelve. From then on, the older brother had almost imperceptibly stopped being a child. He had learned to manage the family budget, keep an eye on his younger brother’s studies, cook meals, argue over the mess in their room, and always find words of comfort whenever Zairin remembered their mother. They had no grandmothers, grandfathers, or other relatives left. In the whole world, they had only each other, and with every passing year their bond grew stronger.

Their mornings had followed the same routine for years. Nayrin would wake up first, start the kitchen automaton, and then rouse his younger brother, who always tried to steal a few more minutes of sleep.

“If you tell me one more time that you’re already up while lying there with your eyes closed, I’ll pour a glass of cold water over you,” Nayrin said, pushing open the door to the small bedroom.

“That would be a violation of human rights,” Zairin muttered without opening his eyes.

“The right to sleep expired ten minutes ago.”

“Then I’ll file a complaint against you.”

“With whom?”

Zairin finally opened one eye and smiled.

“Haven’t decided yet.”

While his younger brother grumbled and got ready for the learning centre, Nayrin had already set breakfast on the table. Their apartment was small but cosy. It was on the forty-seventh residential level, and the panoramic window offered a view of dozens of transport tiers branching in every direction like the blood vessels of an enormous living organism. Monorail trains glided silently along their routes, drones carried cargo between industrial sectors, and thousands of people hurried about their business, unaware that the history of their world was already nearing its end.

During breakfast, the information screen switched on automatically and began the morning news broadcast. Footage from the front, maps of military operations, and casualty statistics appeared in turn. Such reports had long since stopped surprising anyone. The war between the White, Black, and Blue races had been going on for centuries, and for most people it had become an ordinary part of life, like the changing seasons or the transport timetable.

“They’re reporting that the Blues have lost two more colonies,” Zairin said, scanning the news. “Can they really do nothing about it anymore?”

Nayrin did not answer at once. He waited until the screen moved on to a report about the opening of a new medical complex, where dozens of the most advanced capsules were being installed.

“Do you remember what they told us about the first medical capsules?” he asked.

“That they were the greatest breakthrough in the history of medicine.”

“Not just medicine. They changed the entire history of humanity.”

The screen showed a young woman stepping out of a capsule after a complicated operation. The presenter explained that a century earlier she would have lost her leg for good, but now she would be back at work in a few days.

“People used to die from diseases, old injuries, and even ordinary infections,” Nayrin continued. “Once the capsules learned to regenerate organs, treat almost any injury, and restore people to normal life, mortality among the White and Black races nearly disappeared. The population kept growing. More people were born every year than died.”

“But that’s a good thing.”

“For us, yes. Not for the Blues.”

Nayrin fell silent for a few seconds.

“They never received this technology. Their lives have remained almost as they were before. People are born, grow old, and die. Every fallen soldier is an irreversible loss for them. But among us, a wounded soldier can often return to the ranks within a few weeks. Because of that, the war stopped being a struggle for territory a long time ago. It became a struggle against mathematics.”

“I don’t get it.”

“Imagine two containers. Water is constantly being poured into one, while it only drains out of the other. It doesn’t matter how strong the second container is. Sooner or later, it will be empty.”

Zairin turned his cup thoughtfully in his hands.

“So they’ve already lost?”

“Military analysts say they have.”

“And what will they do when they realise they’ve lost for good?”

Nayrin did not answer. He only looked at the screen, where a presenter with an unwaveringly calm smile was announcing that several thousand more medical capsules had entered service. Outside, the vast city continued its life. People hurried to work, children ran to their learning centres, and transport platforms slowly drifted above the transparent dome on their way to orbital stations.

A little over three months passed after that conversation.

Life continued at the same steady pace, as though the brothers’ morning discussion about the inevitable defeat of the Blue race had been nothing more than another breakfast argument. The monorails arrived on schedule, learning centres operated seven days a week, medical complexes treated hundreds of patients every day, and new capsules rolled off the production lines faster than they could be installed. It was this familiar routine that reassured people most of all. As long as the enormous Path City carried on at its usual rhythm, the war, as before, remained somewhere far away.

That day, Nayrin and Zairin were returning home after classes in the Path City. Their classes were far from their home, and getting back to the old metropolis took more than an hour, even using the Path City’s high-speed transport.

The monorail carriage was almost full. Some passengers were reading the news, others were dozing after their shifts, and a few children were running along the aisle despite the adults’ disapproval. Zairin was leaning against the carriage’s transparent wall, watching the transport levels flash past, when red alarm signals suddenly lit up above their heads.

At first, no one reacted. During the years of war, the alert system had been activated dozens of times. People were used to training alarms, civil-defence drills, and reports of distant strikes. Only a few passengers in the carriage mechanically raised their eyes to the information panels.

Then an announcement sounded.

“Attention, all residents. A mass launch of strategic nuclear weapons has been detected. Time until possible impact in certain sectors: less than ten minutes. Proceed immediately to the nearest protective shelters. I repeat—”

The announcer did not finish the sentence. The carriage braked sharply, throwing people forward. A moment later, the main lights went out, and emergency strips illuminated the aisle instead. The doors opened before the train had come to a complete stop, and the crowd surged onto the platform almost as one.

“Nayrin!” Zairin shouted.

“Stay behind me! Don’t fall behind!”

They jumped out with hundreds of other people. The platform, which only a minute earlier had been an ordinary transport hub, turned within seconds into a whirlpool of human bodies. Some people were calling for their children, others were trying to reach their families by phone, while soldiers struggled unsuccessfully to direct the flow towards the nearest shelters. Sirens sounded continuously from every direction, and loudspeakers repeated the same command over and over.

“Remain calm! Proceed to the civil-defence sectors! Do not crowd!”

But there was no calm left.

As the brothers ran out onto a wide transport avenue, dozens of fighter jets roared overhead, flying above the transparent dome of the Path City. Almost immediately, long white columns of light shot into the sky: the missile-defence systems had begun operating. High above the dome, blinding fireballs flashed one after another. Some missiles exploded in the atmosphere; others broke apart into clouds of debris that slid across the outer surface of the protective covering like burning rain.

People around them froze. It lasted only a moment. Then someone even began to applaud.

“They’re shooting them down! They’re shooting them down!”

But the joy did not last. A few seconds later, dozens of new points of light appeared in the sky. Then hundreds more. They were approaching from every direction, and even someone with no military knowledge could understand that no defence system could intercept so many targets.

Nayrin tightened his grip on his brother’s hand.

“Run!”

They dashed towards the nearest entrance to a protected sector. They were less than a hundred metres from the massive armoured gates when the ground beneath their feet shook as if the Path City itself had struck an invisible wall. The air filled with a deep rumble. Somewhere far away, many kilometres off, the first nuclear explosion had occurred.

Then came the second. And the third. No one was counting anymore. One after another, blinding white mushroom clouds rose above the horizon, their light passing through the transparent dome until it seemed as though the sun itself were exploding, piece by piece.

The voice of the alert system suddenly cut off, replaced by a dry electronic tone.

“Dome integrity breach. Multiple critical failures. Recommended—”

The announcement broke off. Nayrin had almost pulled Zairin into the airlock when the world around them flared with light.

There was no explosion, no shock wave. Only white light that, in a single instant, swallowed the people, the city, the sky, and reality itself.

Nayrin instinctively clung to his brother’s hand. He could still feel it for one more moment.

Then his fingers touched empty space.
