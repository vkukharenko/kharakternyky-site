Autumn was already drawing to a close. Most of the leaves had fallen, exposing the dark crowns of the trees around the village, and the cold wind increasingly brought heavy, leaden clouds from the north. The forge was warm, which was why people gathered there even when they had no work to be done. While the blacksmith repaired ploughs or forged spearheads, villagers discussed the harvest, news from the surrounding lands, or simply warmed their hands by the furnace.

Nayrin skillfully held the glowing workpiece with the tongs while the blacksmith struck it with precise blows. In five years, they had learned to work almost without words. One of them needed only to shift the metal slightly for the other to understand what came next. After the final blow, the blacksmith carefully examined the blade of the sword taking shape, nodded with satisfaction, and set down his hammer.

“You work faster than I do now,” he said with a smile, wiping the sweat from his forehead. “A little longer, and I’ll have to ask you to take me on as your apprentice.”

Nayrin smiled back.

“No. You still spot mistakes before I do.”

“It’s years that see mistakes, not eyes.”

The blacksmith fell silent, studying the young man.

“You know… when you first came to our village, I was sure you wouldn’t survive even your first winter. Thin, exhausted, silent. And now look at you. If you’d been born here, half the girls would have been arguing over who got to become your wife by now.”

Nayrin laughed softly.

“Good thing I wasn’t born here.”

“Do you still think about your home?”

The smile slowly disappeared from his face.

“Every day.”

The blacksmith asked nothing more. Over the years, everyone in the village had understood that the stranger never spoke about his past. Not because he distrusted them. It was simply that those words belonged to another life, one that no longer existed.

That evening, Boren invited Nayrin to his home. It did not happen often. The elder usually called him over only when he wanted to discuss something without unnecessary witnesses.

The house was warm. Boren’s wife was busy by the stove, ladling stew into wooden bowls, while two grandchildren were already falling asleep on a bench after a long day. The elder waited until the children had gone to bed, poured hot herbal infusion into their mugs, and sat down opposite Nayrin.

For a long time they ate in silence. Rain pattered outside, logs crackled in the stove, and the ordinary quiet of the home somehow reminded Nayrin of the evenings he and Zairin had once spent in their apartment above the transport levels of the Path City. They had often been silent there, too. Not because they had nothing to say, but because people close to one another do not always need words.

Boren was the first to break the silence.

“You’ve lived among us for five years, but sometimes I feel that part of you has spent all that time looking somewhere beyond the horizon. Whom did you leave there?”

Nayrin did not answer for a long time. His gaze rested on the fire dancing calmly behind the cast-iron stove door.

“My brother.”

Boren nodded silently and did not interrupt.

“We were orphans. I had no one but him. I always thought I was the one protecting him… but it turned out that what I feared most was one day being left without him.”

He spoke slowly, as though each word had to be pulled out of his memory.

“That day, we were running together. I was holding his hand. Tightly… I can still remember the touch. And then… it simply disappeared. As though it had never been there.”

Boren sighed heavily.

“Do you think he died?”

Nayrin shook his head.

“I don’t know. That’s the hardest part. If I had seen him die, at least I would know the truth. But now I wake up every morning thinking that perhaps he’s alive. And every night I fall asleep not knowing whether he even exists.”

The elder reached out and gripped his shoulder firmly.

“As long as a person has not lost hope, they have not yet been defeated.”

Nayrin gave only a sad smile.

“Sometimes I feel that hope itself is what keeps me from moving on.”

That night he could not fall asleep for a long time. The conversation with Boren had reopened memories he had kept buried inside for years. He lay with his eyes closed, listening to the rain beating steadily against the wooden roof, the wind rustling dry leaves, and a lone wolf howling somewhere deep in the forest.

And then something happened that he could not have explained in words. It was not like a sound, a dream, or a memory, yet he felt as though another presence had suddenly appeared beside him in the darkness.

It had no face or voice. No image at all. And yet it felt so real that Nayrin abruptly sat up in bed and looked around.

There was no one in the hut. Only the candle, almost burned down, cast trembling shadows on the wooden walls.

He was about to lie down again when the strange sensation returned. This time it came with an emotion. Not his own. Someone else’s—sharp and almost unbearable. The despair of a person who, like him, had lost everything they had. A wave of loneliness followed. Then fear. And then…

Hope. A tiny, barely living spark, yet so strong that Nayrin involuntarily held his breath.

He did not understand what was happening. His mind searched for an explanation, trying to convince him that it was only a dream or the result of exhaustion after a hard day. But his heart already knew the truth before he did.

Somewhere far away, beyond the forests, rivers, mountains, and cities of this world, someone was alive who felt the same.

Someone who did not belong here, whose loneliness had somehow touched his own soul.

Nayrin rose, opened the door, and stepped out into the cold autumn rain. The night was dark and moonless, but he stood in the middle of the deserted street, looking into an invisible distance. He did not yet know what had happened. He did not know what this strange ability was called or why it had awakened now.

But for the first time in five years, he felt that he was no longer the only stranger in this world. From that night on, his long journey was no longer just a search for his younger brother. It had become a search for all those whom the same unknown force had torn from their home worlds and scattered across the boundless expanse of other planes.

For the next few days, Nayrin lived as though nothing had happened. As before, he arrived at the forge before dawn, lit the furnace, helped the blacksmith prepare the metal, and went out with Orm to the training ground beyond the village in the evenings.

From the outside, it might have seemed that life had returned to its usual course. But inside, he felt a strange, restless anticipation slowly growing. Whenever he was alone, the memory of that night returned, along with the чужі feelings that had touched his consciousness from somewhere unknown.

He tried to convince himself that it was all fatigue or a trick of his imagination, but each following night the invisible presence returned. It did not come closer or take on a shape. Nayrin still heard no words. Only brief flashes of someone else’s life touched him so quickly that he could not make sense of them.

Sometimes it was the bitterness of loss, so painful that his heart tightened. Sometimes it was joy, flaring up so unexpectedly that it seemed someone had met a loved one after years of loneliness. Once, such intense fear woke him that he reached for his sword, certain an enemy was standing outside the door. Only a few moments later did Nayrin understand: the fear did not belong to him.

The more he tried not to think about it, the more strongly he felt that an invisible thread was slowly drawing him eastward. He could not explain it even to himself. When he left the village, the strange sensation almost disappeared if he headed in one direction, but grew stronger as soon as he turned another way. It was not like a sound one could hear with one’s ears or a scent carried by the wind. It was more like an inner certainty that somewhere far away there was an answer to the question that had tormented him since the day he woke in an unfamiliar forest.

Eventually, Nayrin stopped fighting himself. He understood that if he stayed in the village for another year or even ten, nothing would change. Every evening he would still gaze beyond the horizon, thinking of his brother, and every night he would still wake to an inexplicable call. It was not curiosity or a thirst for adventure. It was the need to find at least one thread that still connected him to his former life.

He spent almost two weeks preparing for the journey. Together with the blacksmith, he forged new nails for his boots so they would withstand long marches along stony roads. Boren gave him a sturdy leather bag that he had once carried on trading journeys in his youth. The elder’s wife sewed extra pockets onto it and spent several evenings drying meat and baking hard bread that would keep on the road. Orm silently checked the sword, tightened the scabbard straps, and replaced the grip, worn smooth by his student’s hands over the years of training.

No one in the village tried to dissuade Nayrin. His decision surprised no one. People had long noticed that he lived among them, but his thoughts were always somewhere far away. Boren asked him only once whether he knew exactly where he was going.

“No,” Nayrin answered honestly. “I only know the direction.”

The elder was silent for a moment, then smiled with the calm expression of someone who had lived a long life.

“Sometimes that is enough. When a hunter sets out to follow tracks, he doesn’t know where his road will end either. If he knew everything in advance, it wouldn’t be a journey.”

Almost the entire village gathered by the gate that morning. The women made their own kind of blessing over him, the children eagerly asked him to bring back something wondrous from distant lands, the blacksmith embraced him so tightly that his ribs nearly creaked, and Orm, as always, settled for a brief nod.

“Remember to look not only ahead,” the old warrior said. “The most dangerous enemy always comes when a person is convinced they know the right road.”

Nayrin thanked them all. Then he took one last look at the village that had become his second home, adjusted the belt holding his sword, and slowly set off along the road that disappeared into the autumn forest.
