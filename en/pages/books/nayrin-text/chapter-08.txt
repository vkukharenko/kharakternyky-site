Almost two weeks had passed since they joined the Major’s unit.

During that time, the mobile base had stopped feeling unfamiliar. Every morning, it woke to the familiar rumble of diesel engines. Some vehicles returned from night patrols, while others were already preparing for a new raid. As the cooks lit the field stoves, mechanics worked in the open air, lying beneath armoured all-terrain vehicles, and the next shift was finishing its morning training on the improvised parade ground. War here was not an extraordinary event but an ordinary part of life. People had learned not to notice it until bullets began to whistle.

The four quickly found their place among the others. Lior increasingly disappeared into the workshops, where he spent hours arguing with mechanics about the best ways to protect the running gear of armoured vehicles or make a rotating turret faster. To her own surprise, Marta began helping the instructors. Girls and very young fighters were drawn to her; they preferred her calm teaching style to the rough shouting of the senior sergeants. Olena spent almost all her time in the infirmary. She was not a doctor, but she quickly learned to dress wounds, assist during operations, and simply sit beside those who most needed human support. Nayrin, meanwhile, was constantly going out on reconnaissance. Not because anyone demanded it, but because he could not sit in one place for long. Every new horizon instinctively made him search for familiar features of the world he had lost.

The base lived a strange but remarkably well-coordinated life. There was no division here between soldiers and civilians. After breakfast, a man might repair an engine, go on a combat raid after lunch, and return in the evening to teach children to read. The children themselves were no longer surprised by automatic weapons, armoured vehicles, or explosions on the horizon. They ran between the tents, played at being soldiers, argued over wooden swords, and sulked when the adults would not let them take turns guarding the outer perimeter.

Several times, the camp had to be packed up and moved. The four saw for the first time how quickly an entire settlement could disappear. What had looked like a small town in the morning became a long column of armoured all-terrain vehicles by evening. When the vehicles reached a new location, they were parked in a tight ring, side by side. The gaps between them were sealed with heavy steel plates, and within just a few hours a well-fortified fortress appeared once again in the middle of the steppe or forest. It seemed that the people had learned to carry not only their belongings with them, but the very idea of home.

One evening, Nayrin stood for a long time on the outer platform, watching the sun slowly sink below the horizon. Explosions flashed again in the far west. They were so familiar that the guards did not even turn their heads. To them, they were as much a part of the landscape as the trees or clouds.

“Thinking about your brother again?” Lior asked quietly as he came to stand beside him.

Nayrin did not pretend he had misunderstood the question.

“Every day. And the longer we stay here, the more often I catch myself getting used to it. That’s what frightens me most.”

Marta and Olena soon joined them. They sat silently on the armour of one of the enormous all-terrain vehicles. Below, life continued to bustle between the tents. Someone was repairing a generator, children were laughing near the kitchen, a tired nurse came out of the field hospital, and two fighters nearby were playing chess on an overturned ammunition crate.

“These are good people,” Olena said quietly. “In these two weeks, I haven’t regretted once that we ended up here.”

“Neither have I,” Marta replied. “But this still isn’t our home.”

Lior watched the life of the camp for a long time, as though trying to memorise every face.

“The Major saved them. He gave them order, gave them a purpose. Without him, most of these people would have died long ago. But every morning they wake up knowing that sooner or later this war will catch up with them.”

Nayrin nodded silently. For several days, he had been carrying the same thought within him, but each time he had put it off. The longer they lived among these people, the harder it became to keep the truth from them.

“We no longer have the right to stay silent,” he finally said. “If there is another world alongside one where people are born only to fight—a peaceful one—they should at least know about it. After that, they can decide for themselves whether to stay here.”

No one answered immediately. Marta only nodded slowly. Olena turned her gaze towards the dark horizon, where distant explosions were still fading. Lior let out a heavy breath and spoke quietly:

“Tomorrow, we’ll tell the Major everything.”

This time, no one objected. On the contrary, for the first time since they had met, their decision was unanimous. Tomorrow would change not only their own fate, but the fate of all the people who, over ten years, had learned to call this endless battlefield their home.

The next morning, all four of them went to the command tent together. A familiar fighter was on guard at the entrance. Seeing them together, he simply nodded silently and pulled back the flap. The Major was bent over a large map densely covered with coloured markings. Hearing their footsteps, he raised his head, put down his pencil, and smiled warmly.

“All of you at once?” He looked carefully at each of them. “This sounds serious.”

“Exactly,” Nayrin replied with a nod.

The smile slowly disappeared from the Major’s face. He moved the map aside, gestured for them to come closer, and spoke in a different tone.

“Then I’m listening.”

Silence filled the tent for a few seconds. No one hurried to begin, as though each of them were making sure there could be no turning back. At last, Nayrin spoke.

“We haven’t told you everything about how we lived before we met your unit.”

“I realised that a long time ago,” the Major replied calmly. “But I never forced you to talk.”

“We weren’t sure ourselves whether we should.”

The Major looked carefully into his eyes.

“What has changed?”

“We’ve seen your people. We’ve seen how they live, leaving one camp after another and building a new one each time. We’ve seen how much effort you spend just to keep them alive for one more day. And we realised that we no longer have the right to remain silent.”

The Major said nothing. Lior stepped forward.

“We know this world isn’t the only one. Other worlds exist. They’re just as real as this one. And some are safer than this.”

For several seconds, the Major stared at him in silence.

“You mean… other planets?”

“No.”

“Other… dimensions?”

“We don’t know what the correct name for them is,” Marta answered. “But they exist.”

A faint doubt crossed the Major’s face.

“And how do you know that?”

Lior did not try to explain. He simply stretched out his hand, and the air in the middle of the tent shivered softly. At first it looked like a slight shimmer above sun-heated stones. Then space slowly began to part, revealing a narrow dark opening. Beyond it, a green forest rustled. Sunlight filtered through the tall trees, and a light breeze stirred the young branches.

The Major slowly stood up. Without taking his eyes off the portal, he cautiously approached and reached out. His hand passed through the opening without resistance. He touched the rough bark of a tree, picked a small leaf, and silently withdrew. For a few more seconds he examined it carefully, then asked quietly:

“This isn’t a trick? Or a dream?”

“No,” all four travellers answered together.

The Major placed the leaf on the table.

“You can travel there and come back?”

“Yes.”

He remained silent for a long time. At last, he asked the question everyone had been waiting for.

“Can only the four of you do this?”

The four exchanged glances. Nayrin answered.

“We don’t know. But we think not. We all come from the same world. Perhaps we were simply the first to understand how it works.”

The Major abruptly pushed his chair aside.

“Come on.”

He quickly left the tent without explaining anything. The four followed him.

Morning training was just ending on the parade ground. Several dozen people were putting away wooden obstacles, while others cleaned their weapons or prepared to leave on a mission.

“Isaac!” the Major called.

A tall, fair-haired scout immediately stepped out of the crowd.

“Bring Hak and Daina too.”

A minute later, all three stood before him.

“You’re about to do something strange,” the Major said. “And you’ll keep doing it for as long as these four tell you to.”

The scouts exchanged surprised glances, but asked no questions. The next half hour passed in a series of uninterrupted attempts. Lior patiently explained that they should not search for some force or tense themselves up. They only had to sense the boundary between worlds, just as they could sense a door even when it was still closed.

At first, nothing worked. Hak merely shook his head in frustration. Daina laughed nervously several times, deciding that they were being made fun of.

Isaac remained silent. He stubbornly repeated everything again and again. Then, suddenly, the air in front of his outstretched hand shivered almost imperceptibly, as though someone had touched the surface of water. Isaac himself did not understand what had happened.

“Again,” Lior said quietly.

Isaac closed his eyes. This time, the dark fissure appeared more clearly. It was very small and lasted only a few seconds, but everyone saw another forest through it. When the portal vanished, such silence fell over the parade ground that the only sound was the wind. The Major slowly shifted his gaze from Isaac to the four travellers.

“So…”

He did not finish. There was no need to explain anything. He had understood it all himself. Turning towards the people, the Major raised his voice so that everyone could hear.

“Today we’ve learned something that will change our lives. We now have a chance to leave this world.”

A low murmur rolled through the ranks.

“We don’t yet know when it will happen. We don’t know how much time it will take. But there are worlds nearby where this war does not exist. And we will learn to travel there.”

At first, people remained silent, looking at the Major in disbelief. But everyone had seen the portal Isaac had opened, and it took them only a few minutes to grasp what they had witnessed and heard. Then the camp came alive. Questions poured in from every direction, but the Major raised his hand.

“Easy.”

He waited until silence returned.

“Starting today, these four will train everyone who has the ability. We will not flee blindly. First, we’ll find a safe world. We’ll examine it. We’ll prepare for the relocation. Only then will we all leave together.”

This time, the answer was a united rumble of approval. For the first time in many years, people were looking not towards the horizon, from which another war might come, but ahead, where hope had finally appeared.

Late that evening, the four returned to their tent. They silently dropped onto their beds. It had been an exceptionally long day. Only Nayrin remained sitting by the entrance, looking up at the starry sky.

“It seems we changed their lives today.”

“And we did the right thing,” Olena said quietly. “They deserve a chance to live without war.”

Lior nodded slowly.

“But this isn’t the end of our journey.”

No one objected.

“We’ll stay,” Nayrin said. “We’ll help train the people. We’ll help find a safe plane.”

“And then we’ll move on,” Marta finished.

“Each of us knows why,” Olena said softly.

Nayrin gave a faint smile.

“I’ll keep looking for Zairin.”

“I’ll look for the answer to why we were given this ability in the first place,” Lior said.
