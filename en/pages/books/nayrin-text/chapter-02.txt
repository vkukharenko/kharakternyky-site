Nayrin could not tell how long that strange state between light and darkness had lasted. It seemed that the very sense of time had vanished along with the Path City, the people, the sirens, and the blinding flashes beyond the transparent dome. He could not feel his own body, hear a single sound, or even tell whether his eyes were open. Only his hand, which a moment earlier had been gripping his younger brother’s palm, hurt unbearably, as though the memory of that touch refused to disappear with everything else.

He came to abruptly, as if someone had roughly shoved him up from deep underwater. His lungs gulped in air, his chest convulsed, and the dense green canopy of treetops appeared before his eyes in place of the white glare. Sunlight pierced the leaves in thin golden bands. In the tall grass, insects chirred without pause, while birds twittered unhurriedly somewhere nearby. The world around him was so alive and peaceful that, for the first few seconds, Nayrin thought he had lost consciousness during the evacuation and was now lying in some woodland park not far from the city.

He sat up quickly and looked around. Forest stretched in every direction. There were no transport routes, no supports of the Path City, no transparent dome that had always covered the sky above the European continent. Only old trees with thick trunks, ferns, dense undergrowth, and the smell of earth warmed by the sun. Even the air seemed different—warmer, more humid, and astonishingly clean. He slowly got to his feet, feeling his heart beat faster and faster.

“Zairin!” he shouted so loudly that his own voice echoed between the trees.

The forest answered only with a startled flutter of wings. A small animal darted out of a nearby bush and vanished into the undergrowth.

“Zairin! Can you hear me?”

Nayrin was no longer calling. He was almost screaming. Without waiting for an answer, he rushed forward, forcing his way through young trees and bushes, stumbling over roots again and again. He felt that if he ran just a few dozen metres, he would surely find his brother wandering nearby in the same confusion. He shouted over and over until his voice began to break into a rasp, but the summer silence and birdsong around him never changed.

Only after several exhausting minutes of running did he stop. A small clearing opened before him, flooded with sunlight. There were no human footprints. No trampled path. No buildings. Only tall grass, butterflies drifting slowly above wildflowers, and an old oak that had probably stood there for hundreds of years.

Nayrin was breathing heavily, trying to convince himself that there had to be an explanation. Perhaps the blast wave had thrown him far from the city. Perhaps this was some kind of nature reserve. Perhaps rescuers were already searching for survivors. He pulled his communicator from his pocket, and only then fully understood that something impossible had happened.

The screen remained black. There was no network signal. The navigation system did not work. No satellites could be detected. Even the emergency beacon, which was supposed to transmit coordinates under any circumstances, could not find a single communication channel. The device itself was functioning properly, but it behaved as though no technology existed anywhere around him.

Nayrin slowly lowered his hand. In all his years, he had never seen the global network disappear completely. Even during the fiercest storms or military operations, backup channels, orbital satellites, or military transmitters had always remained available. What he was holding now could mean only one thing: there was no connection anywhere.

The thought struck him harder than the panic had a few minutes earlier.

He no longer searched the surroundings for his brother. He looked at the sky. Through the treetops it was unusually clear, with no transport corridors, no orbital platforms, not the slightest trace of civilisation. Even the airspace seemed empty. In the world where Nayrin had grown up, no more than a few minutes would pass without a transport aircraft, cargo drone, or patrol fighter flying over any forest. Here, there was nothing.

He felt a chill slowly growing inside him. Not the kind that creeps through the body, but the kind born deep in the soul when a person suddenly realises that everything that seemed unchanging only a minute ago no longer exists. He did not know where he was. He did not know whether Zairin was alive. He did not know whether even one of the thousands of people who had run with them towards the shelters had survived. His entire life, all the rules he had known since childhood, had fallen apart in an instant, leaving only a silent forest and a lonely seventeen-year-old boy surrounded by unfamiliar nature.

There was no point in standing still. If people existed somewhere, he had to find them. If there was a road, it had to lead somewhere. Nayrin looked around one last time, as though hoping his brother would emerge from behind the trees at any moment and give him his familiar smile. But the forest remained indifferent to his hope. He took a deep breath, chose a direction by the sun, and slowly moved on, still unaware that these first steps along the path would become the beginning of the longest journey of his life.

By evening, Nayrin had understood only one thing: this world had nothing in common with his own. He walked almost without resting, using the sun as his guide as it slowly sank towards the horizon. Yet instead of highways, transport platforms, or even power lines, he saw only endless forest. Several times he came across narrow animal trails, found streams of cold water, and spotted deer fleeing fearfully between the trees, but he encountered no sign of human presence. Hunger had not yet begun to trouble him, but fatigue was steadily building, along with the realisation that he needed to find at least some kind of shelter before nightfall.

He had begun looking at old trees with broad canopies, beneath which he might wait out the darkness, when he caught the smell of smoke. At first it was faint, but it grew stronger with every minute. Nayrin stopped, breathed in again, and quickly changed direction. If a fire was burning somewhere, people had to be nearby.

Beyond a hill, the forest came to an unexpected end. A wide valley opened before him, with a small settlement spread across its centre, enclosed by a tall wooden palisade. Dozens of small houses with thatched roofs stood behind the fence, along with barns, livestock pens, and several vegetable gardens. Grey smoke rose from the village ovens, and two guards stood by the gate, leaning on long spears.

Nayrin had never seen anything like it outside historical reconstructions and educational simulations. The houses seemed far too small and primitive. The road between them had been trampled by human feet and animal hooves, and women were washing clothes in large wooden tubs beside a well. There was no glass, no metal façade, no silent machinery. Only wood, stone, clay, and people accustomed to doing everything with their own hands.

He took a few steps towards the gate, and one of the guards noticed him. The man shouted something sharply in an unfamiliar language and thrust his spear forward. The second immediately drew the string of a short bow. Within seconds, several more armed villagers had gathered by the gate. They called to one another, never taking their eyes off the stranger in his unusual clothes.

Nayrin understood at once that any careless movement would only make things worse. Slowly raising both hands, he showed that he was unarmed and then tried to speak to the people.

“I’m not an enemy. I need help.”

The villagers’ faces remained impassive. He repeated the same words more slowly. Then he tried another language he had studied at the learning centre. Then another. In response, he heard only unintelligible shouts and saw the archer draw his bowstring even tighter.

An elderly man with a thick beard stepped out from among the villagers. Judging by the way the others treated him, he was the village elder. He examined Nayrin carefully from head to toe, pausing to study the fabric of his jacket, his unusual shoes, and the communicator fastened to his belt. Then he asked him something.

Nayrin did not understand a single word. They looked at each other in silence, both aware of the same problem. They were all human. They could all speak. But between them lay an abyss that neither words nor gestures could cross.

At last, Nayrin cautiously placed a hand on his chest.

“Nayrin.”

Then he slowly pointed to the elder. The man was silent for a few seconds, then repeated the same gesture.

“Boren.”

Nayrin smiled and nodded. It was the first thing he had understood since waking in this world.

The elder spoke with his people for a long time. Some were clearly suggesting that the stranger be driven away; others watched him with undisguised curiosity. Eventually Boren gave a short order, and the archers lowered their weapons. The gate slowly opened, but two armed men immediately took their places on either side of Nayrin, making it clear that they were ready to stop him at any moment.

The village went about its unhurried life. Women carrying baskets of vegetables walked past; barefoot children watched curiously from behind fences; an old blacksmith was pulling a glowing billet from his forge, sending sparks flying across the yard with each hammer blow. Cows lowed in their pen, geese wandered leisurely along the street, and the smell of fresh bread drifted from the open door of the bakery. This world was simple and harsh at the same time, but it was alive. After the terrible silence of the forest, this ordinary bustle of human life unexpectedly gave Nayrin the feeling that he was no longer alone.

That evening, Boren gave the stranger a place to sleep in a small storeroom beside his own house. It was not so much an act of hospitality as a precaution: they had decided to keep the stranger close until they could work out who he was and whether he posed a danger. Before leaving, the elder brought him a bowl of thick stew, a piece of still-warm bread, and a mug of water. He silently set everything on the table, touched his own chest, repeated his name, and then pointed just as slowly at Nayrin.

“Nayrin,” the boy replied.

Boren gave a faint smile and left, closing the door behind him. Alone, Nayrin sat for a long time beside the small window, watching dusk gather over the village. Dogs barked somewhere, mothers called their children indoors, and the hammer rang a few more times in the forge before falling silent.

This was a life entirely different from the one he had known since birth. Yet for the first time since the destruction of his world, he felt not only fear but a faint glimmer of hope. If people lived here, if they were capable of showing compassion to a stranger, perhaps this strange world was not yet a sentence passed on him.

Months passed. They became the hardest months of Nayrin’s life, not because he had to work hard or live in unfamiliar conditions. The hardest thing was accepting the thought that the world where he had been born might remain forever somewhere beyond an invisible boundary.

Every morning he woke with a brief surge of hope, as though it were all just a terrible dream and he only had to switch on his communicator or hear the monorail outside the window. But each time he saw the low wooden ceiling, smelled smoke from the stove, and heard the chickens and livestock waking in the yard. With every passing day, his memories of home grew not weaker but more painful, because now nothing could replace it.

He began to understand the local language faster than he had expected. At first he memorised individual words, then entire phrases, carefully observing people as they worked. Boren often took him to the fields, patiently explaining the names of tools, plants, and domestic animals.

The elder had long since stopped treating Nayrin as a dangerous stranger. He could see that the boy was not seeking power, did not behave arrogantly, and was not trying to impose his own ways on the villagers. On the contrary, he worked alongside everyone else, learned quickly, and never refused the hardest tasks.

One day Boren took him to the forge. It was hot inside, the air shimmered with heat, and a broad-shouldered blacksmith was pulling a long strip of glowing metal from the furnace. Several heavy hammer blows sent sparks flying across the workshop. Then the man looked closely at Nayrin and addressed the elder briefly.

Boren smiled and nodded.

“Now you’ll work here,” he said, using almost no gestures. Over the past months, they had learned to understand one another much better.

The blacksmith turned out to be stern but fair. At first, he trusted Nayrin with only the simplest tasks: tending the fire, carrying water, and handing him tools. Later, he allowed him to hold workpieces with tongs, and a few weeks after that he put a heavy hammer in his hands for the first time. In the evenings, his hands hurt so much that he could barely fall asleep. Yet after a month he began to notice something strange.

What exhausted the other apprentices was becoming easier and easier for him. He could work beside the furnace from dawn until dusk and wake the next morning without the usual fatigue. The first to notice this was not Nayrin himself, but the blacksmith.

One day the blacksmith placed a heavy steel beam on the anvil, the kind usually carried by two people, and gestured for Nayrin to help. The boy instinctively lifted the metal and only then realised he was holding the entire weight by himself. The blacksmith did not immediately let go of his end. For several seconds he watched the young man closely, then slowly opened his fingers.

The beam did not even tremble. Nayrin stared at his own hands in surprise. He felt no particular strain. It was as though he were holding an ordinary log. The blacksmith silently took the workpiece away, examined the boy once more, and said nothing. But by that evening, almost the entire village was talking about the stranger’s strange strength.

It soon became clear that strength was not the only thing. During an autumn hunt, one of the hunters carelessly startled a wild boar. The enraged animal charged straight at a young man who had not even managed to raise his spear. Nayrin saw it only out of the corner of his eye. He had no time to think. His body simply sprang into action.

Later, he tried many times to recall that moment. It seemed to him that the whole world had suddenly slowed down. The boar was only just lowering its head to strike when Nayrin was already beside it. He grabbed the young man by his clothes and literally flung him aside, then rolled across the ground, avoiding the animal’s sharp tusks. The beast rushed past, crashed into a tree, and disappeared into the thicket. The hunters stood in silence for a long time, unable to understand how the stranger had managed to do something the human body should not have been capable of.

After that incident, the elder asked a local warrior to begin teaching Nayrin how to use weapons. His instructor was named Orm. He had once served as a prince’s retainer, fought in several wars, and, after returning to his native village, had begun teaching the young everything he knew. Seeing the boy’s unusual strength, Orm merely said:

“Strong arms are worth nothing if a foolish head is in control of them.”

It was from him that Nayrin first took a real sword in his hands. Not a light practice stick, but a heavy combat weapon that an ordinary boy of his age would not have been able to hold for long. During the first few weeks, Orm hardly allowed him to attack at all. For hours, he made Nayrin repeat the same stance, the same steps, the same movements with sword and club, explaining that a warrior won not through strength, but through the habit of making the right move at the right moment.

Years passed. Nayrin grew older. His shoulders broadened, his movements became more confident, and his body seemed to have forgotten fatigue altogether. He could spend half a day working in the forge, train with Orm for several hours, and still help the villagers repair the palisade after a storm in the evening. Even the old hunters were amazed that the boy who had come to them from the forest, thin and bewildered, could now easily cover distances in a day that left them needing a rest.

Nayrin himself increasingly caught himself thinking that the changes were not happening only to his body. It was as though some incomprehensible power were awakening inside him. He still did not know what had happened to him that day during the nuclear war. He did not know the word “characternyk”. He did not know that thousands of other people, scattered across countless worlds, were at that very moment being just as astonished by their own strength, speed, and endurance. But every night, before falling asleep, he looked up at the starry sky and silently asked the same unanswered question:

“Zairin… If you’re alive… what have you become?”
