The wail of sirens cut through the camp before dawn. Nayrin woke so abruptly that it felt as though someone had yanked him by the shoulder. Beyond the thin tent walls, people were already shouting; engines rumbled and metal equipment clattered. Someone was running between the rows of vehicles, repeating the same order, but the general noise made the words impossible to make out.

A few seconds later, Lior burst into the tent. He had not even fastened his jacket.

“West! Quickly!”

All four rushed outside almost at once. Dawn was only beginning to turn the sky grey above the camp. The cold air smelled of grease and smoke from the field kitchens, but no one was paying attention to breakfast or the usual morning routine. The entire camp was focused on a single command. Mechanics were starting engines, soldiers were pulling ropes off the tents, and medics were loading the wounded into ambulances. Even the children were not crying. They sat silently in the beds of trucks, clutching their few belongings.

Nayrin looked west. At first, he thought a storm was gathering there. A dark band towered above the horizon, stretching across almost the entire sky. But within a few seconds he realised that clouds could not glow from within.

The entire western sky was slowly filling with a crimson light. And that light was growing brighter.

The Major was already standing on the roof of one of the armoured vehicles, binoculars in his hands. He did not take his eyes off the horizon, and his usually calm face was unnaturally tense.

“The forest caught fire during the night,” he said briefly when the four approached. “We thought it was another fire after an artillery strike. An hour ago, the drones sent back new footage.”

He silently handed Nayrin a tablet.

The screen showed a shaky image taken from high above. Flames stretched from horizon to horizon. Not a single patch of green remained ahead of them. Forests were catching fire before the flames even reached them. The air itself was turning into fire. Enormous fiery whirlwinds spun above the ground, while thick black smoke rose so high that it blocked out the sun.

“It’s coming straight towards us,” the Major said quietly. “In a few hours, there’ll be nothing left here.”

Lior looked at the screen once more.

“This isn’t an ordinary fire.”

“That’s right,” the Major replied. “I don’t know what it is or where it came from, but I’m asking you to save my people.”

The camp continued to churn around them. Engines rumbled, cranes creaked, and the heavy all-terrain vehicles were already beginning to form a column, although this time no one intended to lead them to another area of the same world.

“Yesterday you said there was a world where no one shoots,” the Major said quietly. “If that’s true… the time has come to show it to everyone.”

Nayrin nodded silently.

“We’ll have to move all the armoured vehicles and trucks,” Lior added. “We’ve never opened portals this large.”

“Then today you’ll learn.”

Lior nodded and stretched out his hand without hesitation. The air immediately shuddered, and the familiar dark fissure opened soundlessly in front of the vehicle column. But a moment later, it became clear that this was not enough. The portal was the same size as dozens of others before it: a person could pass through without difficulty, but it was too small for an armoured personnel carrier.

The Major glanced briefly at the enormous vehicles, then at the dark passage.

“We won’t fit.”

Lior did not close the portal. On the contrary, he concentrated even harder, trying to force its edges apart, but space barely yielded. The dark boundary only trembled slightly before becoming still again.

“Together,” Nayrin said quietly.

Marta and Olena stepped beside them without a word. All four stretched out their hands towards the opening at once. At first nothing changed, but after a few seconds the darkness began slowly to recede. The portal widened reluctantly, as though the world itself were resisting the passage of so much metal. A little more effort, and the edges had moved far enough apart for an armoured personnel carrier to drive through.

“The first vehicle!” the Major ordered at once.

The heavy engine roared, and the lead all-terrain vehicle slowly moved towards the dark passage. When it passed through without obstruction and disappeared on the other side, the camp seemed to come alive. The remaining vehicles moved forward one after another, followed by the people, livestock, field hospital, workshops, and everything else that only minutes earlier had been their home.

Meanwhile, the fire kept drawing closer. At first, only the smell of burning reached the camp—distant, almost imperceptible. Then the wind changed. It grew hotter and drier, and the first small flakes of ash came with it. They settled quietly on the armour of the vehicles, on people’s shoulders, and on the tents that had not yet been taken down, covering everything with a thin grey layer.

No one needed to look at the horizon anymore. Everyone knew there was almost no time left.

When the last vehicle disappeared through the portal, the flames were no more than a few kilometres away. They rolled forward as a solid wall, higher than the trees. Fiery whirlwinds spun wildly in front of it, and the sky overhead had turned almost black.

The Major looked back one last time. All that remained where the camp had stood were trampled earth, a few extinguished campfires, and tracks left by the vehicles. Ten years of life were contained in a single brief glance.

“Let’s go,” he said quietly.

No one in the group replied. The four stepped through the portal together with the Major. The dark opening closed soundlessly behind them.

A moment later, the wave of fire swept across the place where the camp had stood only minutes earlier. Nothing remained but flames, ash, and hot wind carrying smoke onward, towards new lands destined to suffer the same fate.

As the armoured all-terrain vehicles formed a defensive ring one after another, life in the camp gradually returned to its familiar rhythm. Soldiers installed steel shields between the vehicles, mechanics checked the engines after the hurried evacuation, medics set up the field hospital, and cooks were already lighting the portable stoves. People worked in silence. Over the past years, they had grown so used to starting over that even the destruction of an entire world could not throw them off course.

The Major watched the camp at work for a few minutes, then called Isaac over.

“Did you find out what caused that wall of fire that burned the plane?”

Isaac took out a tablet displaying photographs taken by drones over the world they had left, just the day before.

In the first picture, a black cloud completely covered the horizon. In the second, an unbroken front of flames was already visible. In the third, nothing remained but fire and ash.

“We’ve reviewed all the recordings,” he began calmly. “This wasn’t a war. Not a bombing. Not a fire after a battle.”

He enlarged one section of the map.

“There was a mountain range here. A few hours before we evacuated, it literally exploded.”

Isaac traced a finger across the screen.

“Judging by the scale of the destruction, this was a supervolcano eruption. Billions of tonnes of ash were thrown into the air, while incandescent debris buried forests and plains hundreds of kilometres around. Everything that could burn caught fire: forests, peat bogs, oil deposits, gas fields. The fire no longer needed people. It was feeding itself.”

“And how long will it last?” the Major asked quietly.

“If the estimates are correct,” Isaac replied, “that world will keep burning for weeks. Possibly a month. After that, it will most likely be covered by a period of cooling lasting many years.”

The Major shook his head, taking in the scale of the catastrophe that had overtaken them. But he quickly pulled himself together, and when he gave his next order, he was once again the confident Major who had led more than a thousand people towards salvation.

“We need to understand where we’ve arrived. Reconnoitre the area, but don’t go too far. I’m interested first of all in water, settlements, roads, and people. If you see anything you don’t understand, don’t play the hero. Come back and report.”

Isaac nodded. A few minutes later, two small reconnaissance drones were already buzzing above the camp. One headed north, the other east.

The first few kilometres revealed nothing unexpected. Fertile fields, neat forests, and low hills stretched all around. The roads were well maintained, road signs stood at crossroads, and traces of recent work could still be seen in the fields. Everything looked as though people had left these places only recently.

The first drone detected a small village seven kilometres from the camp. Isaac climbed into a light armoured vehicle with three scouts and set off along the route the drones had just mapped.

The journey did not take long. Half an hour later, they switched off the engine a few hundred metres from the village and covered the rest of the distance on foot.

An unnatural silence greeted them. An overturned bucket lay beside the well. The doors of most houses stood ajar. Chickens wandered through the yards, and cows lowed in the barns, though no one had fed them for several days.

They saw the first dead man right in the middle of the street. He lay on his back, as though he had fallen while walking. There was not a drop of blood on his body, not a single wound.

Farther on, the bodies became more frequent.

An elderly woman sat on the porch of a house, dead with her knitting still clutched in her hands. An entire family had frozen around a table. In the forge, a blacksmith lay beside the anvil, his hammer still on the ground nearby.

There were survivors too, but very few. They barely reacted to the people around them. Some were delirious with fever; others coughed so violently that blood appeared on their lips. Dark patches covered their necks, chests, and arms, and their eyes were cloudy and could barely see.

Isaac did not risk approaching them. He ordered the scouts to take only a few photographs, then the group returned to the vehicle.

The second village identified by the other drone turned out to be almost the same. People lived there too. And there, too, they were dying.

By evening, Isaac was already reporting to the Major. The unit commanders, medics, and the four travellers gathered in the headquarters tent. Photographs from the drones, maps of the area, and the doctors’ notes lay on the table.

Isaac spoke first.

“There are no signs of war. The houses are intact, the fields cultivated, the roads maintained. But in two settlements we found the same picture. Most of the people are already dead. The rest are close to death. There are no signs of violence.”

One of the doctors pulled the photographs closer and shook his head.

“This isn’t poisoning.”

He placed several of his own notes beside them.

“Judging by the symptoms, we’re dealing with a highly aggressive infection. If it spreads through the air or water, a single infected person could be enough to infect an entire town within a few weeks.”

The Major remained silent for a long time. Then he slowly looked around at the people seated at the table.

“We’ve just escaped from a world that is burning, only to arrive in one where people are being killed by disease. I don’t want us to have to choose between two kinds of death.”

He looked at Nayrin.

“If other planes exist… we’ll keep searching.”

Nayrin nodded silently. No one objected. The decision was obvious to everyone present. It was easier to set out into the unknown once again than to build a new home in a place where the very land carried death.
